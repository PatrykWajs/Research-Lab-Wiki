    Collagen: What It Actually Does vs What People Think¶

    Collagen is sold for hair, skin and nails and increasingly for sleep, gut health, "detox", glutathione, inflammation, anxiety, arteries and joints. Which of those are real, how strong is the evidence, and are they collagen-specific or just a glycine effect? This is the whole picture, ranked by how strongly the evidence holds — settled first, single-study and debunked last.

    Complete · Published 4 July 2026 · Rebuilt 14 July 2026 — every cited study re-read from source and re-verified · 74 cited studies · Confidence: High on the collagen-vs-glycine framing · Moderate on the modest musculoskeletal effects.

    Start here¶

    Bottom line

    Collagen is basically expensive glycine, and almost every benefit it's sold for is a glycine effect you can get cheaper somewhere else.

    Myths vs. reality

    What most people believe What the evidence actually shows
    The collagen you eat rebuilds the collagen in your skin and joints Your gut breaks it into amino acids like any protein. It does not travel to your skin "as collagen." Finding it in blood is not the same as it working.
    Collagen smooths wrinkles and firms skin Strip out the manufacturer-funded and low-quality trials and the skin benefit disappears. The best independent review (23 trials, 1,474 people) found no clear evidence. Unproven, not disproven.
    Collagen builds muscle and counts as protein It's an incomplete protein with no tryptophan and low leucine. Two tracer studies show it can't switch on muscle building. Whey, and even pea protein, beat it.
    Collagen boosts glutathione and detoxes your liver Glutathione needs cysteine, which collagen has none of. The strong data use glycine PLUS NAC, not collagen. The liver "detox" is a rat pathway, never shown in humans.
    Collagen makes you pee less at night No trial measured urine output or the hormone (ADH) behind that story. The only weak signal is a bladder reflex from glycine, not less urine made.
    Collagen is good for hair and nails Hair and nails are keratin, not collagen. The one nail study had no placebo group; the hair evidence is industry-only.
    Any collagen powder is the same UC-II (40 mg, joints) and hydrolyzed peptides (grams) are different products. Evidence for one does not transfer to the other. Marketing blurs this on purpose.
    Collagen "rebuilds your arteries" The best causal tests (a genetics study and an animal feeding study) were both null. Digestion never routes collagen to artery walls.

    What you actually learn

    • Collagen is about one-third glycine and an incomplete protein. Its protein-quality score is basically zero, so it should not count 1:1 toward your daily protein target.
    • A 15 g scoop of collagen gives only ~3.5 g glycine, which happens to be the exact glycine sleep dose. Plain glycine powder costs several times less per gram of glycine.
    • The skin benefit only shows up in manufacturer-funded and low-quality trials, and vanishes in the independent, high-quality ones. Two 2025-26 reviews openly disagree, so the honest verdict is "unproven."
    • Nearly every positive collagen trial has a collagen maker as an author (Rousselot, Gelita, Nitta, and others). Who paid for the study is the single best predictor of a positive result.
    • Collagen cannot build muscle. It carries only ~0.9 g leucine per serving versus ~4.3 g in whey, below the ~2.5-3 g needed to trigger muscle growth.
    • Collagen's best real use is joints. It cuts osteoarthritis pain by a small amount (about SMD 0.35), but that sits near the "you'd barely feel it" line and one independent trial found nothing.
    • The famous "collagen for sleep" study had 13 people, was maker-funded, and missed all its main sleep targets. Only "fewer awakenings" improved, and it had no protein-matched comparison.
    • The one thing collagen does that plain glycine can't: a single specific hydrolysate blunts a post-meal blood-sugar spike by ~40%. But only its own maker has tested it, and the mechanism was measured in mice.
    • Safety catch: collagen carries hydroxyproline that turns into oxalate (urinary oxalate rose ~43% versus whey), a real flag if you get kidney stones. Plain glycine skips this problem.

    What this means for you

    • If you want the sleep or calming effect, buy plain glycine (~3 g before bed). It's cheaper and better matched to the evidence than collagen.
    • Don't count collagen toward your protein, and don't use it to build muscle. Use whey, eggs, or another complete protein for that.
    • Skin, hair, nails, gut, detox, arteries, anxiety: don't buy collagen for these. The evidence is null, industry-only, or a flat misconception.
    • Try collagen only if your goal is joint comfort alongside training, and even then expect a modest effect and choose a third-party-tested product (roughly 47% of protein powders fail a conservative heavy-metal threshold).
    • If you're a kidney-stone former, skip collagen and gelatin (the hydroxyproline raises oxalate). Plain glycine is the safer swap.

    TL;DR

    Collagen protein is ~⅓ glycine plus proline/hydroxyproline, and an incomplete protein (essentially no tryptophan — don't count it as protein). Trace each benefit to a mechanism and most of the "wellness" claims are GLYCINE effects, not collagen-specific — collagen is a costly glycine delivery vehicle, and plain glycine is cheaper. The popular skin benefit is unproven, not disproven: the pooled positive signal collapses in independent, high-quality trials. Sleep is a modest glycine effect; gut-barrier, "detox", glutathione, anti-inflammatory, anxiety and artery claims are weak-to-unproven for collagen. Where collagen is best-evidenced is musculoskeletal — joint/osteoarthritis pain (a moderate but funding-limited effect, with an independent null), postmenopausal bone density, tendon size with training, and wound healing in patients — all modest, product-specific (UC-II ≠ hydrolyzed peptides ≠ gelatin), and it does not build muscle (it can't raise muscle protein synthesis; whey beats it). The single most reliable pattern across the whole literature is manufacturer funding driving the positive results.

    The picture, ranked by how strongly the evidence holds

    • Tier 1 — settled: collagen is ~⅓ glycine + an incomplete protein (no tryptophan, leucine-poor);1 most "wellness" benefits are glycine effects, delivered expensively;4 collagen does NOT build muscle (can't raise muscle protein synthesis — whey and even pea beat it);25 the skin benefit is null once you remove industry-funded and low-quality trials;6 and manufacturer funding is the dominant confound.3
    • Tier 2 — real but modest: joints/OA is collagen's best-evidenced use (pain SMD −0.35, but manufacturer-funded with an independent null);89 sleep is a modest glycine effect (the seed trial missed its primaries, control not protein-matched);123 the glucose-blunting hydrolysate is peptide-specific but single-funder;14 glutathione needs GlyNAC (collagen has no cysteine);16 bone density + tendon size are funded surrogates (structure, not strength).1718
    • Tier 3 — weak/conditional: gut-barrier, anti-inflammatory, wound healing (sick patients / topical dressings), nail/hair, nocturia (a weak glycine bladder-reflex, not ADH), liver, arteries.23
    • Tier 4 — myths: anxiety (dose-impossible extrapolation);24 "rebuilds arteries" (the causal test is null);25 "less urine at night via ADH" (a misconception);23 and a fabricated glycine-sleep citation people circulate.
    • Safety: heavy metals are brand-variable (buy third-party-tested); the kidney-stone risk is hydroxyproline-specific, so plain glycine dodges it.2627

    Tier 1 — The strongest, settled points¶

    Collagen is ~⅓ glycine and an incomplete protein¶

    Collagen is the body's main structural protein, built on a repeating Gly-X-Y triple helix in which glycine is every third residue (~33% by count, ~19% by weight), with proline and hydroxyproline in the X/Y positions.1 It is an incomplete protein: tryptophan is absent, essential-amino-acid content is only ~14%, and it is leucine-poor (~0.9 g per serving vs ~4.3 g in whey, below the ~2.5–3 g needed to trigger muscle protein synthesis), so its protein-quality score (PDCAAS/DIAAS) is near-zero and collagen should not be counted toward daily protein for muscle.12 Eaten as gelatin or hydrolyzed peptides, it is digested to free amino acids plus a small fraction of stable di/tri-peptides (Pro-Hyp, Hyp-Gly) that appear in blood and stimulate fibroblasts in vitro — but it does not reach the skin as collagen, and plasma peptide levels are micromolar and transient, so detection is not efficacy.

    The load-bearing crux: most benefits are glycine effects¶

    Almost every downstream "wellness" effect can be explained by supplying glycine + proline and by collagen being a cheap, glycine-dense protein — not a unique peptide mechanism proven in humans. A 15 g collagen dose delivers only ~3.5 g glycine, exactly the dose used in the glycine sleep trials.3 Where a benefit exists, the parsimonious reading is glycine, delivered expensively — and free glycine is several-fold cheaper per gram.4

    Collagen does NOT build muscle¶

    Two independent tracer trials show that collagen cannot raise muscle protein synthesis: whey (and even pea) stimulate it while collagen does not, because collagen is leucine-poor and incomplete.25 "Fat-free mass" on a body-scan includes connective tissue and water, so a body-comp signal can be real yet not be muscle. For muscle or sarcopenia, a complete protein beats collagen — it should not displace it.

    The skin benefit collapses under bias control¶

    Pooled meta-analyses look positive, but the single independent, bias-stratified review is decisive: in non-industry-funded trials and in high-quality trials the benefit disappears (Myung & Park 2025, 23 RCTs / 1,474 — "no clinical evidence to support collagen supplements to prevent or treat skin aging").6 A newer independent umbrella review (Ravindran 2026) reports the opposite, but it pools mostly low-quality reviews and inherits their bias — two independent reviews disagreeing is itself a sign the primary trials are too weak to settle it.7 Crucially, no skin RCT uses an equal-protein comparator — every one compares collagen to inert placebo, never to isonitrogenous whey — so a general protein effect cannot be ruled out. Verdict: unproven, not disproven. (Nail evidence is one single-arm, open-label, no-placebo study; hair evidence is scarce and industry-adjacent — and nail/hair are keratin, not collagen.)

    Funding bias is the dominant confound¶

    Nearly every positive collagen trial has a collagen manufacturer among the authors (Rousselot, GELITA/VERISOL, Nitta, Jellice, Nippi, Newtree); the benefit disappears in the independent, high-quality subgroups.63 This one pattern explains most of the gap between the marketing and the evidence.

    Tier 2 — Real but modest (joints, sleep, glucose, glutathione, bone, tendon)¶

    Joints / osteoarthritis — the strongest case¶

    A trial-sequential meta-analysis (35 RCTs / 3,165 patients) finds collagen reduces OA pain (SMD −0.35) and improves function (SMD −0.31), and the analysis reached its required information size.8 The catch: effects sit near the minimal-clinically-important line, the trial base is heavily manufacturer-funded, and a manufacturer-independent RCT (N=68) found no benefit over placebo.9 Undenatured type-II collagen (UC-II, 40 mg) is a different product with an oral-tolerance mechanism and its own positive trial — but "beating glucosamine + chondroitin" is a low bar, because that comparator is itself at/near placebo in the large independent GAIT trial, and UC-II's evidence is almost entirely from its maker.1011 No human trial shows cartilage regeneration; every benefit is a symptom score.

    Sleep — a modest glycine effect, primaries null¶

    The whole collagen-sleep case is one small (n=13), manufacturer-funded RCT whose primary sleep outcomes were null; only a secondary "fewer awakenings" (21.3 vs 29.3, p=0.028) and a near-ceiling cognition score improved.3 A decisive flaw compounds this: the comparator was an inert taste-matched drink, not protein-matched, so even "fewer awakenings" can't be pinned on collagen over any bedtime protein. The glycine sleep literature it leans on is real but small and high-risk-of-bias: ~3 g glycine before bed shortens sleep-onset latency via a core-temperature drop.1213

    The one collagen-peptide-specific signal: post-meal glucose¶

    A specific hydrolysate cut the post-meal glucose rise ~40–53% — genuinely collagen-peptide-specific — but every human trial shares one funder (Rousselot), it has not been independently replicated, its GLP-1/gastric-emptying mechanism was measured in mice only, and it is mechanistically a general protein-preload effect that whey shares.1415

    Glutathione, bone, tendon¶

    Glutathione needs GlyNAC — glycine plus N-acetylcysteine — used in aged/diseased people, from a single lab, mostly open-label; collagen supplies no cysteine, so it cannot reproduce it, and the mainstream view is that cysteine, not glycine, is the rate-limiting precursor in the first place.16 Bone: König 2018 (postmenopausal women, 5 g/day, 12 months) found significant spine and femoral-neck density gains — Gelita-funded, a surrogate endpoint (density, not fractures), heterogeneous when pooled.17 Tendon — loading does the work: 5 g specific collagen peptides during 14 weeks of heavy training increased tendon cross-sectional area, but stiffness, strength and performance rose equally in both groups; a 2026 review grades this formally — GRADE A that size/stiffness rise with training, GRADE A against an added strength effect.1819 Muscle gains are real only as generic added protein (small fat-free-mass effect, SMD 0.48, with training), not as a muscle-builder.20

    Tier 3 — Weak, single-study, or conditional¶

    Gut, inflammation. Gut-barrier "leaky gut" repair is in-vitro/animal only (one mouse study found collagen worsened colitis, and a glutamine gut-permeability meta-analysis is null); anti-inflammatory benefit is confined to diseased cohorts, and the collagen trial's own inflammation markers were null.3 A collagen serving delivers ~3.5 g glycine — roughly 4× below the ~15 g/day that moved markers in the glycine trials.

    Wound healing. In clinical nutrition, collagen peptides speed pressure-ulcer healing — a dose-response trial (a high-dipeptide formula beating a low-dipeptide one at equal protein) is the cleanest evidence a benefit is peptide-specific, and an independent RCT found ~2× faster healing.2122 But this is enteral nutrition in malnourished patients, and much of the "collagen heals wounds" evidence people cite is actually topical collagen dressings — a physical scaffold device, a different route.

    Nocturia has a direct human signal, but for glycine, not collagen (~6 g/day in overactive-bladder patients), via a bladder/spinal reflex — not antidiuretic hormone and not "deeper sleep".23 Liver "detox" is a real conjugation pathway but not a proven human outcome (and the rodent bile-acid arm doesn't transfer — mouse bile acids are taurine-conjugated). Arteries: human data are small, manufacturer-linked, and surrogate-only.

    Tier 4 — Myths and misconceptions¶

    Anxiety. No human trial has tested collagen against an anxiety endpoint. The claim extrapolates from 30–60 g isolated glycine in psychiatric trials (mixed-to-null; the largest, CONSIST, was negative) — doses 7–20× the glycine in a collagen serving.24 At food doses this is a misconception.

    "Rebuilds arteries." The glycine→heart-disease causal link fails the best genetic and mouse tests (pleiotropy-robust Mendelian randomization and ApoE-knockout feeding, both null);25 digestion does not route dietary collagen to arterial tissue.

    "Less urine at night." No trial measured urine volume or ADH, so the "collagen makes you produce less urine at night" claim is a misconception — the real (weak) signal is bladder-signaling, not reduced urine output.23

    Product type is everything. UC-II (40 mg, immune tolerance) ≠ hydrolyzed peptides (grams, substrate/signalling) ≠ gelatin + vitamin C (synthesis cofactor) ≠ topical dressings (a device). A trial on one form is not evidence for another — and a widely-circulated "2025 N=48 glycine sleep RCT" does not exist in PubMed; don't cite it.

    Dose, cost, and safety¶

    • Cost: if the goal is the glycine benefits, plain glycine (~3 g) is several-fold cheaper per gram of glycine than collagen.4
    • Heavy metals: brand-variable — testing found ~47% of protein powders exceeded a conservative safety threshold. Buy third-party-tested (NSF/USP).26
    • Kidney stones — hydroxyproline-specific. Hydroxyproline (unique to collagen/gelatin) is metabolized to oxalate; gelatin raised urinary oxalate 43% vs whey at 5–10 g, while glycine itself contributes under 5% of urinary oxalate — so the caution applies to collagen, and plain glycine dodges it. A flag for recurrent calcium-oxalate stone-formers, negligible for others with good hydration.27
    • Allergen / pregnancy: marine collagen is a fish allergen (rare anaphylaxis; a serious skin-reaction case report exists); bovine carries a theoretical, undocumented prion caveat; pregnancy safety data are limited — treat as insufficient, not "proven safe".

    The honest bottom line¶

    Collagen is, first, a glycine/proline-rich incomplete protein. Its most-marketed benefit (skin) is unproven once funding and quality are controlled; its "wellness" benefits are mostly glycine mechanisms extrapolated past the human evidence, and glycine is cheaper bought directly. Its genuinely best-evidenced uses are musculoskeletal — joint/OA pain, bone density, tendon size, and wound healing in patients — all modest, product-specific, and manufacturer-entangled, and it is an inferior protein for muscle. The evidence-based reason to take collagen, if any, is joint or connective-tissue support alongside training — not the wellness claims it is mostly sold on.

    The full findings — all 54¶

    Every conclusion this review reached, grouped by how strongly the evidence backs it. Each finding is distilled from the fully-cited evidence above.

    Tier 1 — Strongest, settled (composition + the load-bearing crux) (10 findings)

    1. Collagen is ~1/3 glycine and an INCOMPLETE protein

    Collagen's Gly-X-Y triple helix puts glycine at every third residue (~33% by count, ~19% by weight) plus proline/hydroxyproline; tryptophan is absent, so its protein-quality score (PDCAAS/DIAAS) is near-zero and it should NOT be counted 1:1 as dietary protein.

    2. The crux: most "actual" benefits are glycine effects, not collagen-specific

    Almost every downstream benefit traces to supplying glycine + proline (collagen is a glycine-dense delivery vehicle); a 15 g collagen dose carries only ~3.5 g glycine — the exact glycine sleep dose — and the authors of the anchor sleep trial cannot separate a glycine from a collagen effect.

    3. Collagen is expensive glycine

    A 15 g collagen serving delivers only ~3.5-5 g glycine, so hitting a 3 g sleep dose or the ~10 g "gap-filling" dose via collagen costs several times more than plain glycine powder per gram of glycine.

    4. Collagen does NOT build muscle — it can't raise muscle protein synthesis

    Collagen is a leucine-poor, tryptophan-free incomplete protein; two independent tracer RCTs show it does not raise muscle protein synthesis above baseline, while whey (and even pea) do — so for muscle/sarcopenia a complete high-leucine protein beats collagen and collagen should not displace it.

    5. Collagen is leucine-poor by the numbers — the quantified reason it can't build muscle

    A collagen serving carries ~0.9 g leucine vs ~4.3 g in whey, below the ~2.5-3 g needed to trigger mTORC1/muscle protein synthesis; its protein-quality score is ≈ 0 — the concrete crux of "don't count collagen as protein for muscle".

    6. The skin benefit collapses under bias control

    The independent meta-analysis (Myung & Park 2025, 23 RCTs / 1,474) found the skin benefit exists only in industry-funded and low-quality trials and is null in independent, high-quality ones: "no clinical evidence to support collagen for skin aging" — though industry flags data-extraction errors, so the honest read is unproven, not disproven.

    7. Skin, updated: two independent 2025-26 reviews openly contradict

    Myung & Park 2025 (bias-controlled subgroups null) and the Ravindran 2026 umbrella review (high-GRADE positive, SMD 0.71-1.01) are both non-industry-funded yet reach opposite conclusions; the umbrella pools mostly AMSTAR-2 "critically low" reviews and inherits their bias, so the skeptical "unproven" verdict stands with an explicitly-flagged dissent.

    8. No collagen SKIN RCT uses an equal-protein comparator

    Every skin trial compares collagen against an inert placebo (maltodextrin/silica), never against isonitrogenous whey/casein, so even the pooled-positive signal cannot be separated from a generic protein/glycine effect — and the one place collagen WAS tested against equal protein (muscle,) it lost.

    9. Funding bias is the dominant confound across the whole literature

    Nearly every positive collagen trial has a collagen manufacturer among the authors (Rousselot, GELITA/VERISOL, Nitta, Jellice, Nippi, Newtree); the benefit disappears in the independent, high-quality subgroups.

    10. Oral collagen does not reach skin "as collagen" — detection is not efficacy

    Ingested collagen is digested to free amino acids plus small peptides (Pro-Hyp, Hyp-Gly) that appear in blood and stimulate fibroblasts in vitro, but plasma levels are micromolar/transient while the cell-culture effects use far higher concentrations, so absorption does not prove a clinical outcome.

    Tier 2 — Well-supported, real but modest (joints, sleep, glucose, GlyNAC, bone, tendon, muscle-FFM) (19 findings)

    11. Joints/osteoarthritis is collagen's single best-evidenced use — a real but modest, funding-limited effect

    A trial-sequential meta-analysis (35 RCTs / 3,165) found collagen derivatives reduce OA pain (SMD −0.35) and improve function (SMD −0.31, high certainty), effects near the minimal-clinically-important line, but the trial base is heavily manufacturer-funded and a manufacturer-independent RCT (N=68) found no benefit over placebo.

    12. "Collagen for joints" is two different products that don't transfer

    Hydrolyzed peptides (8-12 g, a substrate/signalling effect) and undenatured type-II collagen (UC-II, 40 mg, an oral-immune-tolerance mechanism) are mechanistically distinct; UC-II's positive trials are almost entirely manufacturer-generated, and evidence for one product does not transfer to the other.

    13. No human evidence collagen regenerates cartilage

    Every positive OA result is on symptom scales (WOMAC/VAS), which are patient-reported surrogates; no RCT shows cartilage regrowth on validated imaging, and symptom relief is not structural repair.

    14. UC-II "beats glucosamine+chondroitin" is beating a placebo-level comparator

    UC-II's headline superiority is hollow: glucosamine+chondroitin is at/near placebo in the independent GAIT trial (N=1,583), UC-II's own positive evidence is manufacturer-generated, and a 2023 limited meta-analysis flags its thin independent replication.

    15. The seed collagen-sleep trial missed its primary endpoints

    Thomas 2023 (PMC10799148), a Rousselot-funded n=13 RCT, found NO change in the primary sleep outcomes (onset latency, efficiency, quantity); only a secondary "fewer awakenings" improved (PSG 21.3±9.7 vs 29.3±13.8, P=0.028), and inflammation + core temperature were null.

    16. Even the seed sleep trial had no protein-matched control

    The one objective positive — fewer awakenings — cannot be attributed to collagen: the comparator was an inert taste-matched drink, NOT isonitrogenous, so the result can't be separated from any bedtime protein, and every primary sleep endpoint was still null.

    17. Sleep is a modest glycine effect

    ~3 g glycine before bed shortens latency to sleep onset and slow-wave sleep and improves next-day fatigue (Yamadera 2007; Bannai 2012), but an independent review grades the glycine-sleep evidence as small-sample/high-risk-of-bias, and several trials are manufacturer-linked.

    18. Glycine's sleep mechanism is a core-temperature drop — but it was null in humans

    Glycine acts on NMDA receptors in the suprachiasmatic nucleus to trigger peripheral vasodilation and a core-temperature fall (rat mechanism); the one human study that measured core temperature (the collagen RCT) found no temperature effect.

    19. The one reproducible glucose signal is glucose-blunting — and it IS collagen-peptide-specific

    A specific screened hydrolysate (H80/Nextida GC) 5-10 g pre-meal cut the post-meal glucose rise ~40-43% overall and ~48-53% in prediabetics (Grasset 2024, n=16) — but manufacturer-only, acute, and its GLP-1/gastric-emptying mechanism was measured in MICE, not the human arm.

    20. The glucose-blunting effect is still NOT independently replicated

    Both human collagen glucose/GLP-1 trials (Grasset 2024, Reynolds 2025) come from the same Rousselot-funded network, and the effect is mechanistically a general protein-preload/GLP-1 effect (whey does the same) with no equal-protein comparator — real in those trials but single-funder and not shown collagen-specific.

    21. Glutathione needs GlyNAC — collagen has no cysteine

    The strong human GSH data use glycine PLUS N-acetylcysteine in aged/diseased people (Kumar 2023; Sekhar 2011); collagen supplies no cysteine, so it cannot reproduce the GlyNAC effect, and in healthy young adults glycine is unlikely to be the limiting precursor.

    22. Cysteine, not glycine, is the classical rate-limiting glutathione precursor

    The "glycine is rate-limiting for glutathione" claim is a McCarty/DiNicolantonio advocacy-review hypothesis; mainstream biochemistry holds cysteine as the limiting precursor — and collagen supplies neither cysteine nor (reliably) the glycine dose that matters, so the collagen→glutathione story is doubly weak.

    23. The whole GlyNAC evidence base is one lab, mostly open-label, largest RCT n=24

    Every positive GlyNAC study is from the single Sekhar/Baylor group — Kumar 2020 (HIV, n=8, open-label), 2021 (n=16, open-label), 2023 (n=24 RCT, isonitrogenous-alanine control) — all small, disease-specific cohorts with pre-existing glutathione deficiency; not a broad, replicated, healthy-population result, and it needs NAC collagen lacks.

    24. Bone: a real BMD signal in postmenopausal women, but surrogate and funded

    König 2018 (N=131, 5 g/day, 12 months) found significant spine + femoral-neck BMD gains with favourable bone-turnover markers, but it is Gelita-funded, the endpoint is BMD (not fractures), and the pooled bone effect is highly heterogeneous (I²~80%).

    25. Tendon: collagen + heavy training grows tendon size but not its function

    5 g specific collagen peptides during 14 weeks of resistance training increased patellar-tendon cross-sectional area vs placebo, but stiffness, strength and performance rose equally in both groups — the loading is the driver, the CSA trials are one manufacturer's product (TENDOFORTE), and ~96% of participants are men.

    26. For tendon, loading is the proven driver; collagen is at most an adjunct

    A 2026 systematic review grades GRADE A that tendon cross-sectional area/stiffness rise WITH training and GRADE A AGAINST an added strength effect; no factorial trial isolates gelatin/vitamin-C/loading — so the training does the work and collagen adds a modest structural increment at most.

    27. Muscle: small fat-free-mass gains only WITH resistance training, from one funded group

    Collagen peptides + resistance training give a real pooled fat-free-mass effect (meta SMD 0.48, moderate certainty) but a small, low-certainty strength effect (SMD 0.19); every arm trained, and the positive trials are dominated by one German group using one Gelita product.

    28. "Fat-free mass" gains are not the same as contractile muscle

    DXA/BIA fat-free mass includes connective tissue, tendon, fascia and water; collagen demonstrably stimulates collagenous-tissue synthesis, and proteomics (Oertzen-Hagemann 2019) shows collagen + training upregulates contractile + Z-disk proteins while muscle protein synthesis still does not rise — so an FFM signal can be real yet not be muscle-fibre hypertrophy.

    29. Body-composition gains without training are tested by one funded trial

    The "only WITH resistance training" pattern is near-universal but not absolute — Zdzieblik 2021 (untrained middle-aged men, 15 g/day, no structured RT, Gelita-funded) is the single test of the no-training case, so the training-dependence rests on that one caveat.

    Tier 3 — Weak, single-study, or conditional (gut, anti-inflammatory, wound, recovery, nail/hair, nocturia, liver, arterial) (13 findings)

    30. Gut-barrier / "leaky gut" repair is unproven in humans

    Collagen barrier data are in-vitro/animal only, a glutamine gut-permeability meta-analysis is null, and one mouse study found collagen peptide WORSENED colitis — so the direction of effect is not even settled preclinically.

    31. Anti-inflammatory effect is disease-restricted, and null in the collagen trial

    The glycine-gated chloride channel is a real anti-inflammatory mechanism, but human benefit appears only in diseased cohorts (one glycine RCT in T2D at 15 g/day), an independent review finds no effect in healthy people, and the manufacturer's own collagen trial found IL-6/CRP null.

    32. Collagen can't even reach the glycine dose that moved inflammation markers

    The glycine-alone trials that lowered oxidative-stress/cytokine markers used 15 g/day (Cruz, Díaz-Flores); a 15 g collagen serving delivers only ~3.5 g glycine — roughly 4× under-dosed — so even granting the glycine mechanism, a collagen serving is below the effective dose.

    33. Wound healing: real in clinical nutrition, and the cleanest peptide-specific signal — but for sick patients

    Collagen peptides speed pressure-ulcer healing, and a dose-response trial where a high-dipeptide formula beat a low-dipeptide one at equal protein (Sugihara, N=112) is the best evidence a collagen benefit is bioactive-peptide-specific — but this is enteral nutrition in malnourished ulcer patients (with an independent, non-Nitta ~2× corroboration), not evidence a healthy person's supplement heals faster.

    34. The "collagen heals wounds" evidence people cite is largely topical dressings

    Holmes 2022 (11 RCTs) shows collagen wound dressings/matrices help chronic wounds — but that is a physical scaffold device, not the oral supplement; no adequately-powered oral post-surgical-recovery RCT in healthy people exists.

    35. Recovery/DOMS: one small manufacturer trial, inconsistent across the field

    A fish-collagen crossover (Kuwaba, N=18, Nippi employees) reported less post-exercise soreness, but systematic reviews find DOMS results inconsistent and a general protein-recovery effect is not excluded.

    36. Nail evidence is one open-label, no-placebo study

    The only nail trial (Hexsel 2017, single-arm, open-label, no control, n=25) reported +12% growth / −42% breaks, but the design cannot rule out regression/placebo, and the nail plate is keratin, not collagen.

    37. Hair evidence is genuinely scarce and industry-only

    There is no placebo-controlled meta-analysis for hair; the hair shaft is keratin, giving no collagen-specific rationale, and marketed density percentages could not be independently verified (confirm).

    38. Nocturia: a real but weak glycine signal, via a bladder reflex — not ADH

    The only direct nocturia evidence is for glycine (~6 g/day in overactive-bladder patients, Sugaya 2021), the design is weak (no parallel control; placebo also improved), and the mechanism is a spinal glycinergic bladder reflex, NOT antidiuretic hormone and NOT "deeper sleep".

    39. Liver "detox" is a pathway, not a proven outcome

    Glycine conjugates benzoate/bile acids and protects the liver in rodent alcoholic/NAFLD models via the Kupffer-cell glycine-gated chloride channel, but no human trial shows collagen or glycine improves liver function or xenobiotic clearance — mechanism is not benefit.

    40. The liver "detox" bile-acid arm is rodent-only and species-specific

    Glycine protects rodent liver (e.g. 83% vs 0% survival in a rat IR+LPS model), but mouse bile acids are almost entirely taurine-conjugated, so the glycine-bile-conjugation "detox" mechanism does not transfer to humans.

    41. Arterial evidence is small, manufacturer-linked, and surrogate-only

    Human collagen cardiovascular data cover only stiffness/BP/LDL (no MI/stroke/death); the top trial is open-label with no placebo (Tomosugi, Jellice), and a 12-RCT meta shows SBP −5/LDL −4 but with I²≈99% and the BP effect vanishing at 12 weeks.

    42. The tendon biomarker story is weak — PINP is a bone marker

    Gelatin + vitamin C raised serum PINP +153% (Shaw, N=8), but PINP overwhelmingly reflects bone turnover (not tendon), and the effect failed to replicate (Lis & Baar, non-significant) — so a blood-PINP rise does not prove tendon collagen was made.

    Tier 4 — Myths, misconceptions, and discredited single sources (8 findings)

    43. Anxiety: no human trial exists; the claim is dose-impossible extrapolation

    No study tested collagen against an anxiety endpoint; the evidence is receptor pharmacology from 30-60 g isolated glycine in schizophrenia/OCD (mixed-to-null; the largest trial, CONSIST, was negative), doses 7-20× the glycine in a collagen serving, so it does not scale to food doses.

    44. The collagen sleep trial found no shift in stress physiology

    The seed RCT specifically measured and found NO change in cortisol, sympathetic normetanephrine, or inflammatory markers — evidence against a nervous-system-calming effect, not for it.

    45. The only collagen "cognition" signal is a ceiling-effect Stroop result

    The manufacturer trial's single positive cognitive endpoint was Stroop accuracy 1.00±0.00 vs 0.97±0.05 (P=0.009) — a 0.03 near-ceiling difference on 1 of 4 cognitive tests (the others null), multiplicity-prone, and possibly sleep-independent; not evidence collagen sharpens next-day cognition.

    46. The "less urine at night" (ADH) story is a misconception

    No trial in this literature measured ADH/vasopressin or nocturnal urine volume for collagen or glycine, so the claim that collagen cuts urine production at night is unsupported; the real signal (if any) is bladder-signaling, not reduced urine output.

    47. "Collagen rebuilds arteries" fails the best causal tests

    Pleiotropy-robust Mendelian randomization and an ApoE-knockout mouse feeding study were both null for atherosclerosis (Hazen lab, 2023-24); "collagen rebuilds artery walls" is a misconception because digestion does not route collagen to arterial tissue.

    48. No isolated-collagen ligament/ACL trial with a hard endpoint exists

    Every human ligament-graft trial bundles collagen with hyaluronic acid + chondroitin + plasma proteins + vitamin C, so collagen's independent contribution is unmeasurable, and UC-II has zero tendon/ligament outcome data — the connective-tissue-injury claims rest on cocktails, not isolated collagen.

    49. Product type is everything — the evidence does not transfer across collagen forms

    UC-II (40 mg, immune tolerance) ≠ hydrolyzed peptides (grams, substrate/signalling) ≠ gelatin + vitamin C (synthesis cofactor) ≠ topical collagen dressings (a physical wound scaffold, a device not a supplement); marketing blurs these, but a trial on one form is not evidence for another.

    50. Guard-rail: the "2025 N=48 3 g glycine PSG RCT" is a fabricated citation

    A widely-blog-cited "2025 randomized double-blind N=48 3 g glycine polysomnography RCT" does not exist in PubMed — it appears only on supplement blogs and looks like a re-description of Bannai & Kawai 2012; do not cite it.

    Safety (cross-cutting) (4 findings)

    51. Kidney-stone caution: hydroxyproline becomes oxalate

    Ingested hydroxyproline (unique to collagen/gelatin) is metabolized to oxalate; gelatin raised urinary oxalate +43% AND glycolate 5.3× vs whey at 5-10 g (Knight 2006), null at 1-2 g — a real caution for recurrent calcium-oxalate stone-formers / hyperoxaluria, likely negligible for others with good hydration.

    52. The kidney-stone risk is hydroxyproline-specific — plain glycine dodges it

    The oxalate load comes from hydroxyproline, not glycine (glycine contributes <5% of urinary oxalate), so the stone caution belongs to collagen/gelatin, not to the cheaper glycine alternative.

    53. Heavy-metal contamination is real but brand-variable

    Clean Label Project testing found ~47% of protein powders exceeded a conservative Prop-65 metal threshold (organic and plant-based averaged higher lead/cadmium); "non-detect" is achievable, so choose third-party-tested (NSF/USP/Clean Label) products.

    54. Safety beyond metals/stones: mostly mild-GI, but real edge cases

    Hydrolyzed collagen/gelatin is GRAS with mostly mild GI effects, but the honest safety tail includes marine (fish) collagen allergy/anaphylaxis, a Stevens-Johnson/TEN case report, a theoretical (undocumented) bovine prion caveat, and insufficient pregnancy/lactation data; for the glycine alternative, ≤15 g/day is well tolerated but a ~0.8 g/kg single bolus caused adverse effects.
