# Magnesium — Findings

Distilled, atomic findings from the magnesium research project (151 sources, 147 PubMed-verified). Numbered `**N. Heading**` (one flashcard each — feeds `/flashcards --type research --target magnesium`), ordered by **evidence strength**: Tier 1 settled → Tier 4 myth, then a Practical block. Each finding ends by linking the `[[wiki-pages]]` it feeds. Source tags `[S#]` map to [[sources]].

## Tier 1 — Settled: strongest, cleanest evidence

**1. Magnesium is genuinely essential, and serum testing hides most of it.**
Magnesium is a cofactor for over 600 enzymes and ATP is biologically active only as Mg-ATP [S1]. About 99% of body magnesium is intracellular (bone ~60%, soft tissue ~39%) and only ~1% is extracellular, with serum holding ~0.3% of the total — the anatomical reason a "normal" serum magnesium can't rule out tissue depletion [S2][S9]. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

**2. The single strongest evidence in the whole field is IV magnesium as emergency medicine, not the supplement.**
Intravenous magnesium sulfate more than halves eclampsia seizures (MAGPIE, N=10,141, RR 0.42) [S66], beats diazepam and phenytoin for recurrent eclamptic convulsions [S67], is first-line for torsades de pointes even when serum magnesium is normal [S70], and is a proven add-on in acute *severe* asthma [S68][S69]. This is pharmacologic IV dosing under monitoring — the opposite end of the spectrum from a daily oral pill. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

**3. Antenatal magnesium for fetal neuroprotection is the highest-certainty magnesium indication on record.**
The updated 2024 Cochrane review found antenatal magnesium sulfate cuts cerebral palsy (RR 0.71, HIGH grade) and severe intraventricular hemorrhage (RR 0.76) in babies at risk of preterm birth [S145][S146][S74][S75], reconfirmed by an independent 2025 meta-analysis [S147]. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

**4. Leg cramps — the flagship consumer claim — is refuted.**
The Cochrane review (11 RCTs, N=735) found oral magnesium "unlikely to provide clinically meaningful" cramp prophylaxis [S48], an independent JAMA Internal Medicine RCT was null [S49], and so was an independent 2026 three-arm RCT (compression stockings worked; magnesium did not) [S101]. The one clearly positive nocturnal-cramp trial was funded by the product's manufacturer [S50]. The effect survives only for *pregnancy* cramps, and even there trials split [S51][S52][S102]. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

**5. The dietary shortfall is real — which makes magnesium different from vitamin D.**
Roughly half of Western adults consume less than the magnesium reference intake (in NHANES, 68% of US adults below the RDA, 19% below half) [S3][S4]. Unlike the vitamin-D "everyone is deficient" panic — which was mostly a cutoff artifact — the magnesium intake gap is a genuine dietary shortfall. Feeds: [[magnesium]], [[supplement-stack]].

**6. But "everyone is clinically deficient" still overstates it.**
Measured hypomagnesemia (serum <0.75 mmol/L) is only ~2–15% of the general population [S5][S6] — higher in type 2 diabetes (~32%) [S140], ICU, and PPI/diuretic users. The scary "everyone" figure conflates dietary-intake inadequacy with clinical deficiency, and no alternative status marker (RBC, ionized, the loading test) has a validated standardized cutoff [S9][S10][S11]. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

**7. Safety is a kidney story: the 350 mg ceiling is the diarrhea threshold, not a poison dose.**
The 350 mg/day upper limit applies only to *supplemental* elemental magnesium and is set by the osmotic-diarrhea threshold, not systemic toxicity; food magnesium has no upper limit because healthy kidneys excrete the excess [S148]. Diarrhea is the dose-limiter. Dangerous hypermagnesemia is essentially a renal-impairment event — reduced kidney function plus magnesium-containing laxatives or antacids [S62][S143][S144]. Feeds: [[magnesium]], [[supplement-stack]].

**8. Magnesium oxide is a genuine, guideline-recommended laxative — a real oral use marketing under-sells.**
Two double-blind RCTs found magnesium oxide (~1.5 g/day) roughly tripled the constipation-response rate over placebo and matched the stimulant laxative senna [S111][S112], and the 2023 AGA–ACG guideline conditionally recommends it for chronic idiopathic constipation [S114][S115]. This is a lumen-level osmotic effect that works regardless of magnesium status — not deficiency correction. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

**9. Oxide is inefficiently absorbed but not "useless," and exotic forms matter less than folklore says.**
Magnesium oxide's fractional absorption is only ~4%, far below soluble salts [S14][S16], but at ~60% elemental magnesium by weight it still delivers a usable amount. Citrate, chloride and chelates are better absorbed than oxide [S15][S16][S151], yet a systematic review shows inter-form differences shrink at steady state when taken with food [S124]. "Premium" delivery forms don't reliably beat cheap salts — liposomal magnesium showed no absorption advantage even in its own maker's trial [S119]. Feeds: [[magnesium]], [[supplement-stack]].

## Tier 2 — Real but modest, and deficiency-gated

**10. Blood pressure: a small drop, cleanly concentrated in deficiency and hypertension.**
Magnesium supplements lower blood pressure only modestly (~2 mmHg average) [S31][S32], and the 2025 Hypertension meta-analysis (38 RCTs) localized the effect sharply: SBP up to −7.7 mmHg in medicated hypertensives and −6 in the hypomagnesemic, but ~null in normotensive replete adults, and larger at ≥400 mg/day [S86][S87]. (One 2024 meta found some effect in normotensives too [S103], so this isn't unanimous.) Feeds: [[magnesium]], [[supplement-stack]].

**11. Higher dietary magnesium tracks lower type 2 diabetes — but supplements barely move glucose.**
Dietary magnesium intake is consistently and dose-dependently associated with lower type 2 diabetes risk (RR 0.86 per 100 mg/day) [S39][S36]. Supplements produce small glycemic gains in diabetics (fasting glucose −0.58 mmol/L, HbA1c ~−0.16%) [S88][S41], and the largest 2026 insulin-resistance meta-analysis was actually null, with the effect gated by baseline status [S105][S40]. Feeds: [[magnesium]], [[carb-macro-framework]].

**12. The causal cardiovascular story is genuinely contested.**
Mendelian randomization once supported a causal protective effect on ischemic (especially cardioembolic) stroke [S38] and coronary disease [S125], but every later, larger MR analysis found no causal link to type 2 diabetes, coronary disease, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, or cardiac structure [S126][S127][S96], leaving only a weak metabolic-syndrome signal [S128]. Composite CVD is null in dose-response meta-analysis [S36]. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

**13. Dietary magnesium lowers mortality; supplemental magnesium doesn't — the healthy-diet-marker tell.**
In a 19-cohort dose-response meta-analysis (1.17M people), *dietary* magnesium tracked lower all-cause (0.87) and cancer (0.80) mortality, but *supplemental* and *total* magnesium showed no mortality benefit [S129] — the classic signature of a nutrient that partly marks a healthier overall diet rather than causing the benefit itself. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

**14. Benefit is deficiency-correction, not dose-response — and "more is better" is false.**
In 1.28M diabetic US Veterans, magnesium–cardiovascular risk was U-shaped (both low AND high serum magnesium were harmful), and propensity-matched oral magnesium replacement cut cardiovascular events only in the hypomagnesemic (HR 0.89), not the normomagnesemic (HR 1.07) [S130]. Feeds: [[magnesium]], [[supplement-stack]].

**15. Magnesium depletion status strongly predicts mortality — the biomarker-vs-supplement paradox.**
A higher Magnesium Depletion Score (a kidney-reabsorption index, not a serum level) predicts roughly 2.5–2.9× all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, reproducibly across 10+ cohorts of older adults, cancer survivors, fatty-liver, diabetes and stroke [S92][S93][S94][S95]. As with omega-3, *status* is a powerful predictor — which does not prove that *supplements* fix the outcome. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

**16. Migraine prophylaxis: guideline-level but modest, and the definitive review isn't in yet.**
Oral magnesium (~600 mg/day, which exceeds the supplemental upper limit and causes diarrhea in some) has guideline-level support for migraine prevention (an American Academy of Neurology Level-B rating, since formally retired) and a 2025 dose-response signal [S57][S58][S82][S116]. IV magnesium does not reliably abort an acute attack [S59], and the definitive Cochrane review is still only a registered protocol [S100]. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

**17. Depression: a fragile signal in the already-diagnosed only.**
Magnesium's antidepressant evidence is a statistically significant but fragile pooled effect in *diagnosed depressed* adults, built on few small, heterogeneous trials — the most-cited of which (Tarleton) was unblinded with no placebo [S28][S29]. Independent meta-analyses give at most a tiny effect (SMD 0.16), and it is null for perinatal depression [S107][S108]. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

**18. Real depletion drivers: PPIs, diuretics, metformin, alcohol.**
Long-term proton-pump-inhibitor use is associated with hypomagnesemia (pooled OR ~1.43) via impaired intestinal uptake, strongest with concurrent diuretics [S64][S63][S65], and PPI-induced hypomagnesemia still causes seizures and neurological syndromes [S133][S134]. Loop/thiazide diuretics waste magnesium, metformin is an under-recognized driver [S141], and alcohol depletion concentrates in drinkers with liver fibrosis or kidney impairment [S71][S142]. Feeds: [[magnesium]], [[supplement-stack]].

**19. SGLT2 inhibitors *raise* magnesium — a genuinely new, direction-reversing drug fact.**
Unlike PPIs, SGLT2-inhibitor diabetes drugs increase serum magnesium by ~0.07 mmol/L as a class effect [S135][S136], can correct hypomagnesemia even in non-diabetics and transplant patients [S137], and have been used to treat refractory hypomagnesemia [S138]. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

## Tier 3 — Weak or unproven: marketing outruns the data

**20. Sleep: a small, low-certainty signal for falling asleep faster in older insomniacs — and no more.**
The only meta-analysis (3 RCTs, N=151 older adults) found magnesium cut sleep-onset latency ~17 minutes but did NOT change total sleep time, at low-to-very-low certainty [S23][S25]. The foundational trial used poorly-absorbed oxide in 43 elderly insomniacs and showed no change in objective sleep time [S22]. Feeds: [[magnesium]], [[supplement-stack]].

**21. "Magnesium glycinate is special for sleep" has zero head-to-head evidence.**
The single clean bisglycinate sleep RCT (N=155) was only marginally significant (ISI p=0.049, effect size d≈0.2, subjective-only), and its responders were the low-dietary-magnesium subgroup [S26]. No trial shows glycinate beats any other form for sleep — the "glycinate" reputation borrows glycine's own separate sleep story. Feeds: [[magnesium]], [[glycine]], [[supplement-stack]].

**22. L-threonate cognition and sleep: rodent brain data, and every human trial is manufacturer-linked.**
The brain-magnesium elevation behind L-threonate is shown only in rats — no human study has measured raised brain or CSF magnesium [S18]. Every positive human trial (MMFS-01 cognition, Magtein cognition, the threonate sleep RCT) is small and run or funded by the compound's commercial owner [S19][S20][S109][S121]; there is still no independent human RCT, and threonate delivers little elemental magnesium per capsule. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

**23. Anxiety, cortisol and stress: suggestive only in vulnerable groups, unproven in healthy people.**
Magnesium's anxiety benefit appears only in already-anxiety-vulnerable samples and the evidence quality is poor [S27]; no well-powered RCT shows it blunts the cortisol/stress response in replete, healthy adults, and the most-cited stress trial was industry-funded with no placebo [S30]. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

**24. Bone: higher intake tracks modestly higher density but not fewer fractures.**
Higher magnesium intake is associated with slightly higher bone mineral density in observational cohorts (~3% in the WHI top-intake group) but NOT with lower fracture risk in that cohort or in meta-analysis [S54][S55]; one cohort dissents [S56], no RCT proves benefit, and Mendelian randomization even suggested lower lumbar bone density [S126]. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

**25. No ergogenic effect in already-replete athletes.**
Any performance signal is confined to magnesium-deficient, older or untrained people; there is no good evidence of a supra-physiologic strength or endurance boost in replete athletes [S53]. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

**26. PMS mood is unproven; the menstrual signal is for period pain, not premenstrual mood.**
Serum magnesium is not reliably lower in PMS [S118], and a 2025 systematic review found insufficient evidence that magnesium relieves psychological PMS symptoms [S61] (the supporting evidence is one small 1991 trial [S60]). The better signal is for dysmenorrhea (period-cramp) pain [S117]. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

**27. ADHD: unproven, and Mendelian randomization is null.**
Magnesium's ADHD evidence is low-quality and usually confounded by co-administered vitamin B6, and a 2025 Mendelian-randomization study found no causal magnesium effect on ADHD (it flagged a novel, watch-list magnesium–autism signal instead) [S83][S84]. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

## Tier 4 — Myths and refuted claims

**28. "Magnesium boosts testosterone" rests on a single flawed trial.**
The entire claim comes from one small 2011 trial with no true placebo arm and unreported baseline magnesium status, in which testosterone rose even in the exercise-only group — so exercise, not magnesium, plausibly drove it [S76]. The proposed SHBG-binding mechanism was shown only in a test tube [S77], the human link is cross-sectional [S78][S79], and no regulator recognizes a testosterone claim. Only deficiency-correction is plausible. Feeds: [[testosterone-protocol]], [[testosterone/index]], [[magnesium]].

**29. ZMA is a clean null, replicated three times.**
The best-designed ZMA (zinc-magnesium aspartate) trial found no effect on testosterone, body composition or strength [S80], and two independent 2024 crossovers found no sleep or performance benefit either [S122][S123]. The only positive ZMA study is non-indexed and co-authored by the ZMA patent-holder. Feeds: [[testosterone-protocol]], [[magnesium]].

**30. Transdermal "magnesium oil" and Epsom-salt "detox" are unproven.**
The only human transdermal-cream pilot missed significance on its primary serum endpoint (p=0.29), reaching it only in a subgroup [S81], and a topical-magnesium review judged skin absorption unsupported [S21]. The flagship Epsom-salt absorption claim was never peer-reviewed or indexed, and "detox" has no defined mechanism or endpoint. Oral remains the evidenced route. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

**31. "Your food has no magnesium anymore" (soil depletion) is mostly a mis-citation.**
The most-cited "declining nutrients" study never actually measured magnesium [S13], and the most rigorous review attributes apparent historical mineral declines mainly to changed analytical methods and high-yield-crop "dilution," not depleted soil [S149][S150]. Some datasets show declines, but the confident claim outruns the data. Feeds: [[magnesium]].

## Practical — what to actually do

**32. Food first — and it's easy to see where magnesium lives.**
Magnesium is the central atom of chlorophyll, so leafy greens are rich, along with pumpkin seeds, almonds, legumes, whole grains and dark chocolate; refining strips ~80% of a grain's magnesium [S1][S2]. A whole-food diet closes most of the intake gap. Feeds: [[magnesium]], [[weekly-shopping-list]].

**33. If you supplement: citrate or glycinate, split-dosed, with food — don't chase exotic forms.**
Aim for a modest elemental dose (~200 mg), take it with food and split it to blunt the laxative effect; citrate and bisglycinate are sensible, well-absorbed, well-tolerated picks, and form differences shrink at steady state [S16][S124][S151]. Oxide is fine only for cheap bulk dosing or when you actually want the laxative effect. Feeds: [[magnesium]], [[supplement-stack]].

**34. The real reason to top up is a plausible gap or a depleting condition — not the marketing claims.**
Benefits cluster in people who are magnesium-insufficient or already symptomatic [S130][S26][S30]. A genuine intake gap (few greens/nuts/legumes), heavy sweating, or a depleting drug/condition (type 2 diabetes, alcohol, long-term PPI or diuretic use) is the evidence-based reason to supplement — not sleep, testosterone, or cramp marketing. Feeds: [[magnesium]], [[supplement-stack]].

**35. Stack cautions: spacing, kidneys, and drug interactions.**
Space magnesium several hours from tetracycline/fluoroquinolone antibiotics and bisphosphonates (it chelates them) [S65]; avoid magnesium-containing laxatives/antacids in kidney impairment (hypermagnesemia risk) [S62][S143]; expect more depletion on a PPI or diuretic — and note an SGLT2-inhibitor diabetes drug raises magnesium instead [S135]. Feeds: [[magnesium]], [[supplement-stack]].
