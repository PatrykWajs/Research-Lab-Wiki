    Electric vs Manual Toothbrushes: What the Evidence Shows¶

    Evidence checked: 2026-11-24 · 29 sources · 40 findings.

    Evidence grades

    Start here¶

    Main finding

    Powered brushes improve average plaque and gingivitis control. A well-used manual brush remains valid. Neither the motor nor a premium price replaces fluoride, complete coverage, and a consistent routine. 1 7 25 28

    Myths and evidence

    Claim What the evidence supports
    Electric means fewer cavities by the same percentage as plaque Plaque indices and cavity incidence are different outcomes. 1 5
    Every person with braces benefits more Recent orthodontic reviews disagree and have low or very-low certainty. 16 17
    An app or AI is necessary One app trial found no added benefit; another trial tested a clinical coaching bundle. 20 21
    Powered brushes inevitably damage gums Pooled recession evidence shows no clear difference, with very-low certainty. 8
    A manual brush cannot work Official guidance accepts either type with effective use. 25 28

    What you will learn

    • How to read the size of a plaque or gingivitis benefit without turning it into a cavity claim.
    • Why randomized cleaning trials and observational tooth-retention studies answer different questions.
    • How children, older adults, disability, braces, and recession change the evidence.
    • Which features may help a routine, and which claims remain unproved.

    A practical choice

    Use a comfortable brush you can maintain and afford. A powered brush is a reasonable option if it helps complete cleaning. Keep fluoride toothpaste and suitable interdental care in the routine. Treat an app as optional and pressure feedback as a cue, not a safety guarantee.

    Answer

    A powered toothbrush offers a real average advantage for plaque removal and gingivitis control. It is a reasonable choice if it helps you clean consistently. A manual brush remains an effective option. Strong claims about fewer cavities, lifelong tooth retention, compulsory apps, or universal superiority in special populations exceed the evidence.

    1. The question and the evidence limits¶

    This review compares ordinary home use of powered and manual toothbrushes. It separates plaque, gum inflammation, cavities, tooth retention, tissue safety, and practical use. These outcomes are related, but a change in one does not establish a change in every other outcome.

    The strongest repeated-use synthesis supports powered brushing for average plaque and gingivitis scores. The Cochrane review included 56 eligible trials; 51 trials with 4624 participants supplied meta-analysis data. Only five trials had a low risk of bias. Its certainty assessment was moderate, with substantial variation across studies. Its search ended in January 2014, so this review also examines newer evidence rather than treating that search as current. 1

    This is a structured evidence review, not a newly registered systematic review or a new meta-analysis. Searches of PubMed, primary journal pages, and official dental guidance took place on November 24, 2026. Twenty-six cited PubMed records received live title and identifier checks. Three additional sources are professional/public guidance or a primary publisher article. Reviews can include the same trials, so their participant totals must not be added as independent evidence.

    2. How large is the average benefit?¶

    The familiar Cochrane estimates correspond to roughly 11% lower plaque indices at one to three months and 21% beyond three months. The corresponding gingivitis-index estimates were 6% and 11%. These are relative differences on particular clinical indices. They do not mean 21% fewer cavities, 21 percentage points fewer people with disease, or an equal improvement for every person. The review itself left the clinical importance uncertain. 1

    An updated 2026 adult gingivitis review provides additional support. Of its 37 randomized trials across three research questions, 21 addressed powered versus manual devices, with outcomes at least three months after treatment started. Its abstract reports better plaque and gingival-bleeding outcomes with powered devices. Exact pooled numerical estimates were not extracted from the full supplementary analyses, so this report does not invent them. 2

    Outcome What the evidence supports Confidence and limit
    Average plaque and gingivitis indices Powered brushes generally perform better Moderate certainty in the main Cochrane synthesis 1
    Oscillating-rotating versus sonic A small average advantage for oscillating-rotating Device generations, study methods, and sponsorship vary 3
    Cavities and tooth loss over years Favorable observational signals, without a simple causal guarantee Long-term cohorts are not randomized brush assignments 5 6
    Recession and abrasion No clear pooled recession difference Very-low-certainty safety evidence 8
    Braces, older age, or disability Effects depend on the population and evidence set Adult averages cannot establish subgroup superiority 13 16 14 17

    3. Oscillating-rotating, sonic, and marketing labels¶

    Oscillating-rotating and high-frequency sonic devices use different movements. A review of 32 publications and 38 comparisons lasting at least four weeks found a small average advantage for oscillating-rotating devices. For example, the between-group end-score difference on the Quigley-Hein plaque index was 0.13 units, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.05 to 0.21, in favor of oscillating-rotating. This is a scale-specific difference, not a 13% reduction in disease. The review received partial P&G support and disclosed past manufacturer relationships. 3

    A separate network review of 28 publications and 56 comparisons found a small powered-brush advantage after a single brushing exercise. Such experiments can test immediate plaque removal. They cannot determine which device a person will use consistently for years, or how many teeth that person will retain. 4

    The practical conclusion is narrower than a brand ranking. A pooled technology advantage cannot establish that every current model, premium handle, special mode, or high movement count provides an additional clinical benefit. A preference for one comfortable device is different from proof of universal superiority.

    4. Tooth retention: promising associations, small absolute differences¶

    The eleven-year SHIP cohort included 2819 adults at baseline. Powered use was associated with 0.36 additional teeth retained, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.11 to 0.62. The widely repeated 19.5% headline concerns the relative difference in modeled tooth-number decline. It does not mean that users possessed approximately one-fifth more total teeth. The same study found 0.09 mm less probing-depth progression in powered users, not a total powered-group progression of 0.09 mm. 5

    A separate seven-year SHIP-TREND cohort of 2214 participants also reported favorable associations for plaque, bleeding, periodontal measures, and missing teeth. It did not find a significant effect on decayed or filled surfaces. These are separate cohorts, not independent randomized experiments. Differences in age, health behavior, resources, and prior oral health can remain after statistical adjustment. 6

    This evidence supports the possibility of a long-term benefit. It does not establish that buying an electric handle causes the full observed difference. The first cohort also had P&G personnel support. That disclosure deserves attention without erasing its actual findings. 5

    5. Cavities: do not replace fluoride with a motor¶

    The first cohort's favorable decayed/missing/filled-surface result includes missing tooth surfaces. Its decayed-and-filled-surface estimate was −0.40 surfaces, with a 95% confidence interval from −0.89 to 0.10, which does not establish a clear difference. The second cohort likewise found no significant effect on decayed or filled surfaces. Neither finding proves that powered brushing cannot prevent cavities. They show why a confident, quantified cavity-prevention claim is premature. 5 6

    Fluoride toothpaste has a more direct evidence base for caries prevention. A Cochrane review of 96 studies supports fluoride toothpaste over non-fluoride toothpaste, while the best concentration depends on age and risk. Device choice and fluoride exposure answer different questions. 7

    6. Safety, recession, and pressure¶

    A 2026 review included ten randomized trials of gingival lesions. Three trials entered each recession pool. The mean difference was −0.03 mm at six months (95% confidence interval −0.07 to 0.02) and −0.03 mm at twelve months (−0.08 to 0.02). Certainty was very low. The result does not support claims that powered brushes inherently destroy gums. It also does not prove equal safety for every design or exclude harm from poor technique. Abrasion findings were inconsistent. 8

    One 36-month examiner-blinded trial randomized 92 people susceptible to recession; 87 completed it. Mean recession change at selected index teeth was +0.17 mm with manual brushing and −0.10 mm with one powered device. The 0.27 mm difference favored that device. This P&G-funded trial concerns existing recession in a selected population, not universal prevention in healthy gums. 9

    Pressure feedback can improve behavior. In one 30-day trial, a display reduced the time above a device pressure threshold more than use of the same brush without the display. It did not measure lifetime recession prevention. A newer analysis of 42 powered users related pressure to bristle wear, without randomly assigning the sensor on or off. Its device-specific thresholds are not universal pressure targets. 10 11

    7. Children and older adults¶

    The 2025 children's review included 12 publications and 30 comparisons. It found a small plaque-removal advantage, mainly for rechargeable oscillating-rotating brushes. The gingival-index meta-analysis did not show a clear advantage. The included children brushed themselves and did not have fixed braces. The result therefore cannot establish the same effect for parent-delivered brushing or for every child. 12

    The six-publication review in people aged 55 and older found no clear pooled difference in plaque, gingival, or bleeding indices. For plaque, the standardized mean difference was −0.10, with a 95% confidence interval of −0.37 to 0.18. This is not proof of equivalence, and an older person may still find a powered handle easier to use. Practical ease and demonstrated clinical superiority are distinct claims. 13

    8. Disability and caregiver brushing¶

    A 2023 review of physical or intellectual disability included 16 publications and 25 comparisons. Most did not show a clear difference. Clinical variation and limited numerical data prevented meta-analysis. Its findings do not support a universal rule that disability makes powered brushing superior. 14

    The 2026 supervised intellectual-disability review included 11 studies, but only three contributed to its plaque meta-analysis. It did not establish clear overall superiority. The review's certainty descriptions are internally inconsistent, so this report does not repeat a high-certainty label. Test grip, sensory tolerance, access to the mouth, and the caregiver's ability to clean effectively. This is individualized practical advice, not a measured superiority effect. 15

    9. Braces: the newer reviews disagree¶

    The June 2026 review included 23 papers and 39 comparisons. Its pooled change scores favored powered brushes, but evidence quality was low. Heterogeneity reached I² values of 92% for plaque, 93% for gingivitis, and 80% for bleeding. Its included studies and methods were not identical to those of later reviews. 16

    The November 10, 2026 network review included 18 trials and 986 randomized participants. Its initial oscillating-rotating plaque advantage did not survive sensitivity analyses. Neither oscillating-rotating nor sonic brushing demonstrated a clear gingival or bleeding advantage in its main time window. Certainty was very low, and the review lacked prospective registration. These results establish uncertainty, not equivalence. 17

    A twelve-month randomized braces trial provides useful context: 92 adolescents were randomized and 84 entered its final sample. It found no overall device effect on plaque, gingival scores, or bleeding, while oral hygiene worsened after appliance placement in both groups. Colgate-Palmolive funded the trial. The practical response is appliance-specific instruction and monitoring, rather than assuming that a motor solves the extra cleaning demand. 18

    10. Timers, apps, and AI¶

    A review of brushing duration found averages of 87 seconds for uninstructed manual users and 143 seconds for powered users. Only two evaluations concerned powered users. These observations cannot isolate the timer's effect or establish what a particular buyer will do. A timer can support either brush, but it cannot identify every missed surface. 19

    In an eight-week randomized trial of 90 dental students, the same powered brush with or without an app produced similar plaque, gingivitis, and abrasion results. This does not prove that all apps are ineffective. It does show why an app is not automatically a necessary clinical upgrade. 20

    A different trial randomized 100 periodontitis patients to ordinary care or a sensing brush plus targeted doctor messages during periodontal treatment. 95 patients entered its stated analysis. At six months, the between-group difference in inflamed-pocket proportions favored the digital bundle by 7.9 percentage points, with a 95% confidence interval of 1.6 to 14.6. The brush, professional messages, and treatment worked together. The result cannot isolate AI, Bluetooth, or the motor as the cause. 21

    11. Time and technique still matter¶

    Two minutes removed more plaque than one minute in a five-publication review covering both brush types. A 2026 review of six publications found very small to absent added plaque benefit from three versus two minutes. Both concern single-brushing outcomes. Neither means that a person should stop before cleaning every surface or disregard tailored dental advice. 22 23

    The 2024 network review of named manual techniques found limited and uneven evidence. Do not present one technique name as a guaranteed way to outperform every powered brush. General guidance still emphasizes complete coverage, appropriate pressure, and effective instruction. 24 25

    12. Environmental impact and ongoing cost¶

    A five-year 2020 life-cycle model ranked electric brushes highest in 15 of 16 environmental-impact categories. A wider, P&G-funded 2026 model included complete home routines and periodontal treatment and found no statistically detectable difference between manual and powered home routines. Different products and system boundaries can produce different answers. Neither model proves a universal environmental winner or that a powered brush prevents enough treatment to offset its manufacture. 26 27

    This review did not establish current retail prices or a premium-versus-basic cost-effectiveness ratio. Compare the handle, replacement heads, local availability, and how long you expect to use the device. Keep this practical budget decision separate from a quantified claim that a particular brush saves money.

    13. A practical decision¶

    The following is our judgment from the evidence, not a prescription derived from one trial:

    • Choose a comfortable powered brush if it helps complete, consistent cleaning and you accept its ongoing cost. Oscillating-rotating has a small average advantage over sonic, but that does not mandate a premium model. 1 3
    • Keep a manual brush if you use it effectively. A separate timer and dental instruction can support that routine. 25 28
    • ADA guidance recommends soft bristles, gentle pressure, and replacement around three to four months, sooner if bristles visibly fray. 25
    • NHS guidance recommends fluoride toothpaste, about two minutes twice daily, and spitting excess toothpaste without immediately rinsing. Adult toothpaste should contain at least 1350 ppm fluoride. 28
    • Children need help or supervision until at least seven according to NHS guidance. Use a smear under three and a pea-sized amount from three to six. A motor does not replace adult assistance. 28
    • Ask the dental team to tailor interdental cleaning to the spaces between your teeth. Low-certainty trial evidence suggests added gingival or plaque benefits; brushing technology alone does not settle this need. 29

    14. What remains uncertain¶

    The exact long-term causal effect on cavities and tooth loss, the best device for every special population, the independent value of each digital feature, and the best lifetime environmental or economic choice remain uncertain. Absence of a clear difference is not proof of equivalence. An absent funding field in a PubMed record is not proof of independence.

    The useful conclusion is practical and bounded: powered brushing usually improves average plaque and gingivitis control, while fluoride, coverage, consistency, suitable pressure, and professional care still matter. Buy help with a routine, not a promise of disease-proof teeth.

    The full findings — all 40¶

    Every conclusion this review reached, grouped by evidence type and practical application. Each finding is distilled from the fully-cited evidence above.

    Tier 1 — Core evidence and care foundations (8 findings)

    1. Powered brushes improve plaque and gingivitis indices on average.

    The Cochrane review found moderate-certainty evidence favoring powered over manual brushes in repeated home use. The average benefit does not mean every powered model or every user performs better.

    2. The familiar percentages describe indices, not fewer cavities.

    Cochrane translated its pooled effects into approximately 11% and 21% lower plaque indices, and 6% and 11% lower gingivitis indices, at one to three months and beyond three months respectively. These are not percentage-point reductions in disease incidence.

    3. Long-term in the main review means more than three months.

    The review classified follow-up beyond three months as long-term. That category must not become a claim that randomized trials establish lifelong tooth retention or cavity prevention.

    4. Fluoride toothpaste has direct cavity-prevention evidence.

    A separate Cochrane review of 96 studies supports fluoride toothpaste over non-fluoride toothpaste for caries prevention. Choosing a motor does not replace choosing an appropriate fluoride toothpaste.

    5. Two minutes removes more plaque than one minute.

    A review of five publications and 16 comparisons found better plaque removal after two rather than one minute with both brush types. These were single-brushing experiments, not long-term cavity trials.

    6. Three minutes is not a demonstrated major upgrade over two.

    The 2026 synthesis of six publications and 20 single-brushing comparisons found a very small to absent additional plaque benefit from three minutes. Its authors considered the clinical relevance negligible; this does not excuse incomplete coverage.

    7. Both brush types can support an effective routine.

    ADA and NHS guidance accept effective manual or powered brushing. Clean the inner, outer, and chewing surfaces with fluoride toothpaste; a trial-average device advantage and an acceptable manual routine can both be true.

    8. A powered brush does not settle the interdental-cleaning question.

    The interdental Cochrane review found low- to very-low-certainty evidence that floss or interdental brushes added to brushing may improve gingivitis or plaque. It did not establish that a powered handle replaces these aids or proves long-term interdental caries prevention.

    Tier 2 — Technology and population-specific evidence (10 findings)

    9. Oscillating-rotating has a small average advantage over sonic.

    A review of 32 publications and 38 comparisons lasting at least four weeks favored oscillating-rotating over high-frequency sonic brushes. This is a small average technology comparison, not proof that every model from one brand beats every rival.

    10. A single-brushing ranking cannot predict years of disease prevention.

    The 2022 network review of 28 publications and 56 comparisons supported a small powered-brush advantage after one brushing exercise. Its design cannot establish adherence, cavity prevention, or tooth retention over years.

    11. The updated adult gingivitis review supports powered devices.

    The 2026 review included 37 randomized trials across three intervention questions, with 21 in its powered-versus-manual device question. It reported better gingival bleeding and plaque outcomes with powered brushes. The retrieved abstract does not provide a numerical pooled effect, so this finding reports the direction of benefit only.

    12. Children show a small plaque-removal advantage.

    The 2025 review included 12 publications and 30 comparisons of self-brushing children without fixed braces. It found a small plaque advantage, mainly for rechargeable oscillating-rotating brushes; it does not establish the same effect when a parent does the brushing.

    13. Better plaque scores in children did not establish better gingival scores.

    The same children's review found no significant pooled gingival-index difference. A plaque benefit must not become a claim of proven gingivitis or cavity prevention in children.

    14. Superiority is not established specifically in adults aged 55 and older.

    The 2024 six-publication review found no clear difference in plaque, gingival, or bleeding indices. Its plaque SMD was −0.10, with a 95% CI from −0.37 to 0.18. An individual may still prefer the powered brush for easier handling.

    15. Disability does not guarantee an electric-brush advantage.

    The 2023 review of physical or intellectual disability included 16 publications and 25 comparisons. Most comparisons found no clear plaque or gingival difference, and heterogeneity prevented pooling. Ease, sensory tolerance, and caregiver access remain individual considerations.

    16. Supervised brushing does not show a clear device winner in intellectual disability.

    The 2026 supervised intellectual-disability review included 11 studies, but only three entered its plaque meta-analysis. It found no clear overall superiority. The limited pooled evidence cannot justify a universal powered-brush prescription or replace caregiver training.

    17. One 2026 braces review found benefits with low certainty.

    The June review included 23 papers and 39 comparisons and favored powered brushing in pooled change scores. Heterogeneity was substantial: I² was 92% for plaque, 93% for gingivitis, and 80% for bleeding. Its authors rated the evidence low quality.

    18. The November braces analysis did not establish superiority.

    The newer network review included 18 trials with 986 people randomized. Its initial oscillating-rotating plaque advantage failed sensitivity checks; gingival and bleeding superiority was not demonstrated. Very-low certainty means uncertainty, not proof of equivalence.

    Tier 3 — Long-term outcomes, safety, and evidence limits (13 findings)

    19. The tooth-retention headline corresponds to a small absolute difference.

    In the 11-year SHIP cohort of 2,819 adults, powered use was associated with 0.36 more teeth retained, 95% CI 0.11 to 0.62. The headline 19.5% describes less modeled tooth-number decline relative to manual users, whose modeled decline was 1.86 teeth. It does not mean 19.5% more total teeth.

    20. The periodontal cohort difference was measured in fractions of a millimeter.

    Powered use was associated with 0.09 mm less probing-depth progression over 11 years, 95% CI 0.02 to 0.16 mm less. That is the between-group difference in progression, not the total progression in the powered group.

    21. The cohort does not settle a direct cavity-prevention claim.

    The combined decayed/missing/filled-surface measure favored powered use, but the decayed-and-filled-surface estimate was not statistically clear: −0.40 surfaces, 95% CI −0.89 to 0.10. Mixing missing surfaces into a score can mix tooth retention with decay experience.

    22. An adjusted cohort is not a randomized brush assignment.

    SHIP powered-brush users differed from manual users, including in age. Adjustment reduces measured confounding but cannot remove every behavioral or socioeconomic difference. The association supports a possibility, not a causal guarantee for a buyer.

    23. Pooled recession evidence does not show a clear safety difference.

    The 2026 review included ten trials. Three trials contributed to each recession pool: the mean difference was −0.03 mm at six months (95% CI −0.07 to 0.02) and −0.03 mm at twelve months (−0.08 to 0.02). Certainty was very low; these results establish neither universal powered-brush harm nor safety equivalence.

    24. One three-year recession trial favors a specific powered device.

    Of 92 randomized adults susceptible to recession, 87 completed the trial. Mean index-tooth recession changed by +0.17 mm with manual and −0.10 mm with powered brushing. The 0.27 mm difference concerns one P&G-funded device trial, not every brush or prevention in healthy gums.

    25. Pressure feedback has behavioral evidence, not a universal injury-prevention guarantee.

    A 30-day randomized display study reduced time above its pressure threshold more with feedback. The 2026 analysis of 42 powered users related pressure to bristle wear without randomizing a sensor. Neither establishes that all sensors prevent recession.

    26. An app did not add clinical benefit over the same brush in one trial.

    Ninety dental students entered an eight-week three-group trial. App-assisted and non-app use of the same powered brush produced similar plaque, gingivitis, and abrasion outcomes. This limited null result is neither a need for Bluetooth nor proof that all apps are useless.

    27. The AI trial tested a brush-plus-clinician-coaching bundle.

    The trial randomized 100 periodontitis patients; 95 entered its stated analysis. A sensing brush plus doctor messages improved inflamed-pocket outcomes during periodontal treatment. Its six-month between-group difference was 7.9 percentage points, not an isolated AI or motor effect.

    28. Evidence does not identify one universally best manual technique.

    The 2024 network review found limited and uneven evidence for named manual techniques. It does not justify claiming that one named method always overcomes the average powered-brush advantage. Individual instruction and actual coverage remain practical priorities.

    29. Manufacturer ties require disclosure and careful interpretation.

    The oscillating-rotating-versus-sonic review received partial P&G support, and the recession trial and 2026 environmental model also had P&G funding. These ties do not automatically invalidate findings, but agreement across overlapping sponsored trials is not wholly independent replication.

    30. The 2020 environmental ranking was model-specific.

    A five-year toothbrush life-cycle model gave electric brushes the largest impact in 15 of 16 categories. This depends on its device, lifetime, replacement, and system assumptions; it is not a universal ranking of every present-day routine.

    31. The wider 2026 environmental model produced a different comparison.

    A P&G-funded model including complete home routines and periodontal treatment found no statistically detectable manual-versus-electric difference for home routines. Its different system boundary matters. A null model comparison neither proves equivalence nor proves that electric brushing avoids enough treatment to offset manufacturing.

    Tier 4 — Practical judgment and guidance (9 findings)

    32. Choose a routine you can afford and sustain.

    Our practical recommendation is to compare the handle and replacement-head costs, availability, comfort, and ability to clean all surfaces. This review establishes no current retail-price ranking or quantified lifetime saving from buying a premium model.

    33. A timer is a time cue, not proof of coverage.

    A duration review found average uninstructed brushing times of 87 seconds with manual and 143 seconds with powered brushes, with only two powered evaluations. This is not randomized proof of a timer effect. A separate timer can also support a manual routine.

    34. Soft bristles and gentle pressure are a reasonable default.

    ADA guidance recommends soft bristles and gentle pressure to limit gingival injury. Do not transfer aggressive manual scrubbing to every powered device; use its instructions and seek tailored advice for recession or sensitivity.

    35. Maintain the brush instead of treating the handle as the whole intervention.

    ADA advises replacement around every three to four months, sooner for visibly frayed bristles, with rinsing and air drying after use. A powered head still needs maintenance; motor operation does not establish effective bristle condition.

    36. Children still need adult help and appropriate toothpaste amounts.

    NHS guidance advises help or supervision until at least age seven, a smear under age three, and a pea-sized amount at ages three to six. A powered brush does not replace this support. Use age-appropriate fluoride advice rather than increasing swallowed toothpaste.

    37. Keep fluoride in contact after brushing.

    NHS guidance recommends spitting excess toothpaste without immediately rinsing with water. Its adult recommendation is toothpaste with at least 1,350 ppm fluoride. This is public guidance, not a powered-versus-manual effect estimate.

    38. A product seal is not a universal superiority ranking.

    The ADA Seal evaluates safety and efficacy against acceptance requirements. Both manual and powered products can qualify. Acceptance does not establish that one device is superior to every other accepted device.

    39. Tailor the choice for braces, disability, or caregiver brushing.

    Our practical recommendation is to test access around appliances, grip, sensory tolerance, and caregiver technique with the dental team. Uncertain subgroup evidence supports individual assessment rather than a universal instruction to buy electric.

    40. A powered brush is a reasonable option, not a compulsory premium purchase.

    Our evidence-based judgment is to favor a comfortable powered brush if it helps consistent, complete cleaning and its ongoing cost is acceptable. A well-used manual brush remains valid. Judge the routine by actual plaque control and professional assessment, not price or advertised movements per minute.
