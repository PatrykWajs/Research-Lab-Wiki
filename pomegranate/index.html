    Pomegranate: What It Actually Does vs What People Think¶

    A fruit whose famous compound never reaches your bloodstream, whose best effect keeps shrinking as more trials are run, and whose most-repeated claim cites a paper that was never published.

    Literature reviewed to 15 September 2026. 147 sources, every identifier resolved against PubMed.

    Start here¶

    Bottom line

    Pomegranate's famous compound never reaches your bloodstream, the one effect it does have keeps shrinking as more trials are run, and the testosterone claim cites a paper that does not exist.

    Myths vs. reality

    What people believe What the evidence shows
    Pomegranate is packed with powerful antioxidants that work inside your body The main compound, punicalagin, is not absorbed. A litre of juice daily for five days left it undetectable in blood and urine.
    Lab studies prove it fights cancer and inflammation Those studies use concentrations 170 to 1,700 times higher than a human ever reaches.
    A glass of juice for 40 days raises testosterone 30% The source is a 2012 conference abstract, never published. It ran two weeks, reported ~24%, measured saliva not blood, and 63% of the sample was women.
    "Khan et al. 2012" proved the testosterone effect No such paper exists. A search for any pomegranate-testosterone paper by that author returns zero records.
    It shrinks prostate tumours and cleans out arteries Both failed when tested against placebo. In the prostate trial the placebo group improved and the pomegranate group did not.
    Eating the seeds gives you what the juice gives you Commercial juice draws its punicalagin from the rind. Juice from the arils alone contains only traces.
    It works the same way for everyone The active compounds are made by gut bacteria, and roughly one person in ten makes none at all.
    "100% pure" on the label means pure juice An independent regulator's survey found 17 of 20 such juices failed authenticity testing.

    What you actually learn

    • Why absorption, not potency, is the question that settles most supplement claims.
    • How an effect that shrinks as more trials are added reveals bias rather than biology.
    • What it means when one company funds both the celebrated positive study and the null one that overturns it.
    • Why a surrogate marker like PSA or artery thickness is not the same as living longer.
    • How a single unpublished conference abstract becomes a protocol repeated for over a decade.

    What this means for you

    • Eat it if you enjoy it. It is fruit, and that is a perfectly good reason.
    • Do not take it for testosterone. The claim traces to an abstract that was never published, and the mechanism runs the opposite way.
    • Do not expect a heart or cancer benefit. Those were tested properly and failed.
    • If you buy juice, expect adulteration. Most products tested were not what their label said.
    • If you want lower blood pressure, the effect here is a few mmHg at best and fading under scrutiny. Established approaches do far more.

    TL;DR

    Pomegranate's laboratory reputation rests on punicalagin, and punicalagin is not absorbed. Give someone a litre of juice a day for five days - about 4.4 grams of it - and neither punicalagin nor ellagic acid is detectable in plasma or urine.1 The concentrations used in cell studies run 170 to 1,700 times higher than anything a human reaches.2

    What does reach your blood is made by gut bacteria, not by the fruit - and roughly one person in ten makes none of it at all.6

    The best-evidenced effect is a small drop in blood pressure, and it is shrinking as the evidence grows: from -7.87 mmHg across 22 trials to -3.52 mmHg across 33.1012 No trial of any size has ever tested pomegranate against a hard clinical outcome - stroke, heart attack, death, metastasis - as a powered primary endpoint.

    The famous cancer and heart results were funded by one company and failed when properly controlled. In the placebo-controlled prostate trial, the placebo arm improved significantly and the pomegranate arm did not.9 A federal appeals court found the health advertising deceptive in 2015.

    The testosterone claim is the weakest thing here. It traces to a 2012 conference abstract that was never published in the fourteen years since, and three of the numbers attached to it are wrong - including a citation that refers to no paper that exists.

    The picture, ranked by how strongly the evidence agrees

    • Settled: not absorbed; the active compounds are bacterial; ~10% of people make none; no hard-outcome evidence anywhere.
    • Real but weakening: a small blood-pressure effect that shrinks as trials accumulate, with ~90% heterogeneity and a dose-response that runs backwards.
    • Failed when tested properly: prostate cancer, carotid artery thickening, erectile dysfunction.
    • Refuted: the testosterone claim, and the aromatase mechanism invoked to support it.

    What this review covered¶

    Nine independent evidence sweeps: composition and pharmacokinetics; urolithin metabotypes; the testosterone claim traced to its source; other hormones; fertility and erectile function; blood pressure and vascular function; cardiovascular disease and lipids; prostate cancer; and product quality and regulation.

    Every identifier was then resolved against the PubMed summary database and checked against the claim it was cited for. 147 resolved, none wrong, none missing. Two retracted papers were identified and are cited only as evidence of the retraction record. Funding was recorded per study, because in this literature it is the dominant confound.

    Tier 1 - The strongest, settled evidence¶

    The compound pomegranate is famous for does not get into you. This is the fact everything else depends on. In a human study that deliberately pushed the dose - one litre of juice daily for five days - neither punicalagin nor ellagic acid was detectable in plasma or urine.1 Absorption fails at the first step.

    Laboratory concentrations are unreachable. Free ellagic acid peaks in human plasma at about 60 nanomolar and clears within hours.2 The in-vitro work generating pomegranate's headlines runs at 10 to 100 micromolar.3 That gap spans two to three orders of magnitude, and because it crosses a unit boundary it tends to pass unnoticed.

    Concentrating the product does not help. A three-arm human crossover compared juice, liquid extract and powdered extract at matched doses. Ellagic acid exposure was statistically identical across all three.4 You cannot engineer past the absorption barrier.

    The juice is made from the rind, not the part you eat. Commercial juice carries 1,500-1,900 mg/L of punicalagin, while juice pressed from the arils alone contains only traces.5 Industrial pressing extracts tannins from the peel. Eating the seeds is not a smaller dose of what was studied.

    What reaches your blood is bacterial. Gut microbes convert ellagitannins into urolithins, which do reach meaningful levels. The authors of the absorption study concluded that any systemic effect "should be attributed to the colonic microflora metabolites rather than to the polyphenols present in the juice".1 In the same study those metabolites lacked the antioxidant activity that made the parent compound interesting in the first place.

    About one person in ten produces none. People sort into urolithin metabotypes, and roughly 10% produce no urolithins at all.6 Metabotype shifts with age and tracks body fat and gut health.7 A widely repeated claim that 50% of people are non-producers contradicts the primary literature and could not be traced to any paper.

    Nothing has been tested against an outcome that matters. Across the entire cardiovascular and cancer literature, every endpoint is a surrogate: blood pressure, artery wall thickness, PSA, tissue markers. No trial reports stroke, heart attack, mortality or metastasis as a powered primary endpoint.89

    Tier 2 - Real, with limits¶

    Blood pressure is the one defensible effect, and it is getting smaller. Four meta-analyses, ordered by size, report -7.87 mmHg systolic across 22 trials, -5.02 across 14, -4.96 across 8, and -3.52 mmHg across 33 trials with 1,490 participants.101112 An effect that declines as evidence accumulates is the signature of small-study bias washing out.

    Three further problems sit inside those numbers. Heterogeneity in the largest analysis is about 90%, meaning nearly all the variation between trials is genuine disagreement rather than chance.10 The dose-response runs backwards - the lower dose produced a three-fold larger effect than the higher one.12 And one analysis reports no overall pooled diastolic estimate at all, no significant systolic effect above 300 mL/day, and states the benefit was "lost after 2 months".11

    The mechanism is narrower than advertised. Pomegranate protects nitric oxide that already exists, but the key paper states explicitly that it had no effect on eNOS expression or activity13 - it does not make your body produce more. That study was funded by the trust that owns the juice company, and the supporting results come from animal and cell work by the same group.

    Downstream measures do not cooperate. A systematic search to late 2024 found flow-mediated dilation measured in only one study across the entire literature - too few to combine.14 The one clean placebo-controlled trial of arterial stiffness found no effect on pulse-wave velocity while blood pressure did fall,15 which undercuts the antioxidant explanation.

    Metabotype is the interesting open question. One dose-response trial found cholesterol and LDL improvements of 12-47%, but only in one metabotype subgroup.16 A second trial found nothing even after stratifying the same way.17 That is one positive and one null - a real research question, not a consumer recommendation.

    No metabolic benefit in diabetes. Seven trials with 350 participants found no effect on fasting glucose, HbA1c, insulin, HOMA-IR, weight, or any lipid fraction.18

    Tier 3 - Failed when tested properly¶

    Prostate cancer is the cleanest refutation in the field. The famous result - PSA doubling time rising from 15 to 54 months - came from a trial that was single-arm with no control group.19 Its own investigator noted that men commonly show this lengthening after treatment regardless of what they consume.

    When the trial was run properly, with 183 participants and a placebo, there was no significant difference between groups. More tellingly, the placebo arm improved significantly while the pomegranate arm did not.9 The original design had been measuring the natural course of the disease.

    Every placebo-controlled prostate trial has failed its primary endpoint: the phase III above; an advanced-disease trial where progression was 41% versus 38%;20 a neoadjuvant trial whose authors wrote "our primary endpoint was negative";21 and a twelve-month trial funded by the NIH with no industry sponsor.22 The study most often cited as positive compared two doses with no placebo arm and found no difference between them.23

    The heart claim follows the same shape. It began with ten patients in an uncontrolled study reporting artery-wall regression.24 The proper test - randomised, double-blind, 289 participants, up to 18 months - found no significant difference.25 It was funded by the juice company's parent corporation. The two studies are routinely conflated, and the reason is that the author of the ten-patient pilot co-authored the larger trial that failed to replicate it.

    A second, fully independent one-year placebo-controlled trial was also null on total plaque volume. Significance in that lineage appears only in later papers applying new image-analysis methods to the same null dataset.

    Erectile dysfunction did not reach significance. The pilot trial reported p=0.058 on its primary endpoint and missed its secondary endpoint entirely.26 Its authors wrote that larger studies "may achieve statistical significance". A properly powered 120-person trial was then run by the same funder, completed in 2012, and has never been published.

    Data is missing from the record. Alongside that unpublished erectile-dysfunction trial, two further sponsor-funded prostate studies - about 230 patients, including a 48-month extension that would be the longest placebo-controlled dataset in the field - remain registered with no results posted. Roughly 350 patients of sponsor-funded data are unaccounted for. The null prostate trial itself took from December 2010 to September 2015 to appear.

    A court ruled on the advertising. The FTC found, and the D.C. Circuit affirmed in 2015, that the health claims were deceptive; the Supreme Court declined to hear the appeal. The objection was that favourable studies were promoted while more rigorous ones with opposite results were omitted. A separate case the company won against Coca-Cola is frequently miscited as vindication - it is a labelling dispute and has nothing to do with whether pomegranate works.

    Most of the juice is not pomegranate. An independent survey funded by a national food-safety regulator found 17 of 20 juices labelled "100% pure" failed authenticity testing,27 and a separate analysis found 4 of 20 preparations contained no detectable punicalagins at all.28 Concentrate costs six to ten times more than apple or grape juice, which is the whole incentive.

    Tier 4 - Refuted¶

    The testosterone claim rests on a paper that was never published. The source is a 2012 conference abstract. It has not appeared as a peer-reviewed paper in the fourteen years since, confirmed through four independent routes including a search for "pomegranate" and "salivary testosterone" together, which returns zero records.

    Three of its circulating numbers are wrong. The duration is two weeks, not 40 days - no study of any design used 40 days. The effect is about 24%, not 30%. And the citation commonly attached to it, "Khan et al. 2012", refers to no paper that exists.

    The abstract itself is weak. It was uncontrolled, with no placebo arm, measured saliva rather than blood, and pooled 22 men with 38 women into a single percentage - so 63% of the sample was female, where salivary testosterone is far lower and noisier.

    The rest of the evidence points the other way. A controlled trial found testosterone declining on pomegranate relative to apple juice.29 The preclinical mechanism is anti-androgenic - pomegranate compounds inhibit aromatase in cell culture.3 If that translated, it would lower androgen signalling, not raise it.

    And the mechanism fails a direct measurement. Urolithin measured in actual human breast tissue came to roughly 0.000026 micromolar, about 100,000-fold below the concentration where aromatase inhibition occurs in cells. The same researchers tested those tissue-level metabolites directly and found they "do not exert antiproliferative or estrogenic/antiestrogenic activities".30

    The trials that did raise testosterone tested something else. Two manufacturer-funded studies showed increased free testosterone in men - but the product is a blend of pomegranate rind plus cacao seed, with no arm isolating pomegranate.3132 Estradiol, DHT and cortisol were measured in those same trials and were null.

    Bottom line¶

    Pomegranate is a pleasant fruit with a modest and probably shrinking blood-pressure signal, no demonstrated effect on any outcome that matters clinically, and an advertising history a federal court found deceptive. Its active compounds are manufactured by gut bacteria that roughly one person in ten does not have. Most commercial juice is not what the label claims.

    Eat it because you like it. It will not raise your testosterone, and the specific protocol circulating for that purpose cites a paper that does not exist.

    This is informational, not medical advice.

    The full findings — all 42¶

    Every conclusion this review reached, grouped by how strongly the evidence backs it. Each finding is distilled from the fully-cited evidence above.

    Tier 1 - Settled, multiply corroborated (10 findings)

    1. The compound pomegranate is famous for is not absorbed.

    Punicalagin is the fruit's signature ellagitannin and the basis of nearly all its laboratory literature. In the one human study that pushed the dose hard - 1 litre of juice daily for five days, about 4.4 g of punicalagin - neither punicalagin nor ellagic acid was detectable in plasma or urine. Absorption fails at the first step, so any claim that punicalagin acts on a human organ has to explain how it got there.

    2. Laboratory concentrations are 170 to 1,700 times higher than a human ever reaches.

    Free ellagic acid peaks in human plasma at about 60 nanomolar and is gone within hours. The in-vitro work that generates the headlines runs at 10 to 100 micromolar. That is a gap of two to three orders of magnitude across a unit boundary, and it means most mechanistic pomegranate research describes conditions that do not occur in a person.

    3. What does get absorbed is made by gut bacteria, not by the fruit.

    Ellagitannins are converted by colonic microbes into urolithins, which do reach meaningful blood levels (up to ~18 µM). The authors of the null-absorption study concluded that any systemic effect "should be attributed to the colonic microflora metabolites rather than to the polyphenols present in the juice". The active agent is a bacterial product.

    4. The absorbed metabolites lack the property that made the parent compound interesting.

    In the same study, the microbial metabolites "did not show significant antioxidant activity compared to punicalagin". The potent molecule is not absorbed; the absorbed molecule is not potent. This single inversion undercuts the antioxidant rationale for the whole fruit.

    5. Roughly one person in ten produces no urolithins at all.

    People sort into urolithin metabotypes: UM-A, UM-B, and UM-0, who produce none. UM-0 prevalence is approximately 10% of the population. Whether pomegranate can do anything for you depends on bacteria you may not have.

    6. The widely repeated claim that 50% of people are non-producers is wrong.

    A figure of "50% metabotype 0" circulates in secondary sources and contradicts the primary literature, which reports ~10%. It could not be sourced to any paper.

    7. Extracts and capsules do not beat juice.

    A three-arm human crossover compared juice, liquid extract and powdered extract at matched polyphenol doses. Ellagic acid exposure was statistically identical across all three. You cannot engineer around the absorption barrier by concentrating the product.

    8. Commercial juice draws its punicalagin from the rind, not the part you eat.

    Commercial juices contain 1,500-1,900 mg/L punicalagin, while juice pressed from arils alone contains only traces. Industrial whole-fruit pressing extracts rind tannins. Eating pomegranate seeds is not equivalent to drinking the juice that was studied.

    9. Metabotype is not fixed, and it shifts with age.

    The largest cohort of its kind (n=839, ages 5-90) concluded that ellagic acid metabolism "is mainly determined by aging" - UM-A declines and UM-B rises across the lifespan. Metabotype also tracks adiposity and dysbiosis, and about half of non-producers converted during sustained intake.

    10. No trial has ever tested pomegranate against a hard clinical outcome as a primary endpoint.

    Across the cardiovascular and cancer literature, every endpoint is a surrogate: blood pressure, intima-media thickness, PSA, PSA doubling time, tissue biomarkers. No trial of any size reports stroke, heart attack, mortality, or metastasis as a powered primary endpoint.

    Tier 2 - Real but much weaker than marketed (10 findings)

    11. The blood pressure effect shrinks as the evidence base grows.

    Four meta-analyses, ordered by size: -7.87 mmHg systolic from 22 trials, -5.02 from 14, -4.96 from 8, and -3.52 mmHg from the newest and largest at 33 trials (n=1,490). An effect that declines as more data accumulates is the signature of small-study bias attenuating, not of a robust drug effect.

    12. Heterogeneity in the largest meta-analysis is about 90%.

    The 22-trial analysis reports I² of 90.0% for systolic and 91.8% for diastolic pressure. At that level essentially all the variation between trials is real disagreement rather than sampling noise, which makes a single pooled average weakly interpretable. Its own authors call for "further high-quality studies."

    13. The dose-response runs backwards, which is a bias signature.

    In the 2017 meta-analysis the lower dose produced a three-fold larger effect than the higher dose (-11.01 vs -3.62 mmHg). A genuine pharmacological effect should not strengthen as the dose falls. The authors present this as robustness; it is the opposite.

    14. The benefit does not appear to persist.

    The 14-trial meta-analysis found diastolic significance only within subgroups, reported no overall pooled diastolic estimate at all, found no significant systolic effect at doses above 300 mL/day, and states plainly that the benefit was "lost after 2 months".

    15. Endothelial function has barely been measured.

    A systematic review searching to late 2024 found flow-mediated dilation and nitric oxide "assessed in only one study" across the entire literature - too few to pool. The vascular mechanism most often invoked has almost no human data behind it.

    16. Pomegranate protects existing nitric oxide but does not make more of it.

    The key mechanistic paper found pomegranate juice a potent protector of nitric oxide against superoxide, but stated explicitly that it "elicited no effects on eNOS protein expression or catalytic activity" and did not enhance eNOS gene promoter activity. The synthesis-side story rests on animal and cell work from the same research group. That paper was funded by the Resnick trust, which owns POM Wonderful.

    17. Arterial stiffness did not move in the one clean trial designed to test it.

    A randomised placebo-controlled parallel trial (n=51, 4 weeks) found no effect on pulse-wave velocity (p=0.694) and none on antioxidant status (p=0.700), while blood pressure did fall. Blood pressure moved, the antioxidant surrogate did not, and the stiffness measure did not - which undercuts the antioxidant-mediated explanation.

    18. The one metabotype-stratified trial that worked, worked only for a minority.

    In a dose-response crossover (n=49), pomegranate extract improved cholesterol, LDL, oxidised LDL and apolipoprotein B by 12-47% - but only in UM-B subjects, with no effect in UM-A or UM-0. This is the most interesting positive result in the literature and it applies to a subgroup.

    19. A second metabotype-stratified trial found nothing at all.

    In metabolic-syndrome patients (n=50), pomegranate extract did not affect markers "as a group or stratifying by UMs" across 40 biomarkers; the dominant determinant of response was the patient's drug therapy. The metabotype hypothesis is promising, not established.

    20. Pomegranate did not improve metabolic status in type 2 diabetes.

    Across 7 trials (n=350): no significant effect on fasting glucose, HOMA-IR, HbA1c, insulin, weight, BMI, or any lipid fraction. No publication bias detected.

    Tier 3 - The negatives the marketing omits (14 findings)

    21. The famous prostate result came from a trial with no control group.

    The 2006 study reporting PSA doubling time rising from 15 to 54 months was single-arm and open-label. Its own investigator noted that men commonly show PSA doubling-time lengthening after surgery or radiation regardless of what they consume - a point a federal court later recorded as a finding of fact.

    22. When the trial was run properly, the placebo arm improved and the treatment arm did not.

    The randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled test (n=183) found no significant difference between groups. Placebo went 11.1 to 15.6 months (p<0.001 within-arm); the extract arm went 12.9 to 14.5 and was not significant (p=0.13). This is the direct empirical refutation of the 2006 design.

    23. Every placebo-controlled prostate trial has failed its primary endpoint.

    The phase III on PSA doubling time, the advanced-disease trial (41% vs 38% progression, p=0.83), the neoadjuvant tissue trial (16% reduction, p=0.095, authors: "our primary endpoint was negative"), and the 12-month active-surveillance trial are all null. The last was NIH-funded with no industry sponsor.

    24. The trial most often cited as positive had no placebo arm.

    A randomised comparison of 1 g versus 3 g extract found no difference between doses (p=0.554) and is frequently read as showing both doses worked. Both arms received the active product, so the within-arm change has nothing to be measured against. The paper states the limitation itself and calls for placebo-controlled studies.

    25. The largest, longest cardiovascular imaging trial was null, and its sponsor was the juice company's parent.

    An 18-month randomised double-blind trial (n=289) found no significant difference in carotid intima-media thickness progression. Positives appear only in post-hoc tertile subgroups of a failed primary endpoint. It was funded by Roll International, POM Wonderful's parent company.

    26. The positive it replaced was ten patients with no randomisation.

    The famous "up to 30% regression" result came from 10 patients, of whom 5 continued to three years, against a non-randomised comparison group. Its effect peaked at one year and did not continue. It also carries a published erratum correcting a blood-pressure figure. This study is the origin of the pomegranate cardiovascular claim.

    27. The original investigator co-authored the larger trial that failed to replicate his own result.

    Aviram is a co-author on the null 18-month trial, which is how the two studies get conflated in citation. They differ on every axis: 10 versus 289 patients, uncontrolled versus randomised double-blind, 50 versus 240 mL/day, and regression versus null.

    28. An independent trial, not industry-funded, was also null.

    A one-year placebo-controlled trial at Western University (n=171) found no significant difference in total plaque volume, p=0.34. Significance appears only in later papers applying novel image-analysis biomarkers to that same null dataset, whose stated purpose is reducing required sample size. They are methodology papers and must not be counted as independent positives.

    29. In the one trial that counted cardiovascular events, the result was uninformative.

    A one-year trial in dialysis patients (n=101) recorded first cardiovascular events at a rate ratio of 0.61, 95% CI 0.25 to 1.5, p=0.30. The confidence interval comfortably includes no effect. This is the closest the literature comes to an outcome trial.

    30. A federal court ruled the health claims deceptive.

    The FTC found, and the D.C. Circuit affirmed in 2015, that POM Wonderful deceptively advertised its products as treating or preventing heart disease, prostate cancer and erectile dysfunction. The central objection was that the company promoted favourable studies while omitting more rigorous ones with opposite results. The Supreme Court declined to hear the appeal.

    31. Roughly 350 patients of sponsor-funded data were never published.

    A 120-person POM-funded erectile dysfunction trial completed in October 2012 has never been published and has no results posted. Two further Roll International prostate trials (about 230 patients, including a 48-month extension that would be the longest placebo-controlled dataset in the field) remain registered with status unknown and no results.

    32. The null prostate trial took nearly five years to appear.

    It completed in December 2010 and was published in September 2015. During that interval the company was litigating the FTC case on the strength of the uncontrolled 2006 study. The interval is documented; the reason is not.

    33. Most commercial juice is not what the label says.

    An independent market survey funded by the Korean food-safety regulator found 17 of 20 juices labelled "100% pure" failed authenticity testing - markers indicated grape, apple and stone-fruit adulteration plus added sugars. A separate analysis found 4 of 20 preparations contained no detectable punicalagins at all.

    34. The economics explain the fraud.

    Pomegranate concentrate runs $30-60 per gallon against $5-7 for apple or grape, a six- to ten-fold margin on every diluted gallon. In 2013 the FDA issued an import alert permitting detention of pomegranate juice without physical examination, after finding substitution with cheaper juices and undeclared colours and sweeteners.

    Tier 4 - The testosterone claim and other myths (8 findings)

    35. The testosterone claim rests on a conference abstract that was never published.

    The source is a 2012 abstract presented at a Society for Endocrinology meeting. It has never appeared as a peer-reviewed paper in fourteen years, confirmed through four independent routes: the author's complete PubMed record, Europe PMC, Crossref, and a PubMed search for "pomegranate" and "salivary testosterone" together, which returns zero records.

    36. Three separate numbers in the circulating claim are wrong.

    The duration is 2 weeks, not 40 days - no study of any design used 40 days. The figure is ~24%, not 30%. And the commonly attached citation, "Khan et al. 2012," does not exist: a PubMed search for any pomegranate-testosterone paper by that author returns zero records. The claim as it circulates is more corrupted than the abstract it came from.

    37. The abstract measured saliva, not blood, in a mixed-sex sample with no control group.

    It was an uncontrolled within-subject design with no placebo arm, using salivary rather than serum testosterone, pooling 22 men and 38 women into a single percentage - so 63% of the sample was female, where salivary testosterone is an order of magnitude lower and noisier.

    38. A controlled trial found testosterone moving the other way.

    In postmenopausal women (n=64) randomised against apple juice, testosterone declined on pomegranate. The trial was null on its primary comparison and the subgroup confidence intervals cross zero, so it is best read as no effect - but the direction is opposite to the marketing.

    39. The preclinical mechanism points anti-androgenic, not pro-androgenic.

    Pomegranate compounds inhibit aromatase in cell culture and downregulate androgen-synthesising genes. If that mechanism translated, it would lower oestrogen and androgen signalling, not raise testosterone. The mechanism literature contradicts the claim it is used to support.

    40. The only peer-reviewed trials showing raised testosterone tested a different product.

    Two manufacturer-funded RCTs did show increased free testosterone in men - but the product is a branded blend of pomegranate rind plus cacao seed. No arm isolates pomegranate, so the effect cannot be attributed to it. Both declare no competing interest while acknowledging the manufacturer funded the work.

    41. Measured in human tissue, the aromatase mechanism fails by five orders of magnitude.

    Urolithin in actual human breast tissue measured about 0.000026 µM, roughly 100,000-fold below the concentration where aromatase inhibition occurs in cells. The same authors tested the tissue-level metabolites directly and found they "do not exert antiproliferative or estrogenic/antiestrogenic activities." The tissue form is also conjugated, whereas the active laboratory form is free.

    42. The erectile dysfunction result was not statistically significant.

    The pilot trial reported p=0.058 on its primary endpoint and failed to reach its secondary endpoint. It was funded by POM Wonderful and announced by company press release. Its authors wrote that larger cohorts "may achieve statistical significance" - the larger trial was then run, and has never been published.
