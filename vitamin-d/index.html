    Vitamin D: What It Actually Does vs What People Think¶

    Vitamin D became the "everyone is deficient, megadose to be safe" supplement of the 2010s — sold for bones, immunity, mood, testosterone, cancer and longevity. Which of those are real, how strong is the evidence, and is any of it a benefit of supplementing a sufficient person — or is the payoff only ever correcting a genuine deficiency? This is the whole picture, ranked by how strongly the evidence backs each claim — settled consensus first, single-study and myth last.

    Complete · Published 6 August 2026 · One 8-cluster literature sweep; every one of the 90 cited study PMIDs was independently title-matched against PubMed — 3 wrong PMIDs were caught and corrected, and 8 retraction / Expression-of-Concern notices confirmed (two nonlinear-Mendelian-randomization papers were pulled) · 90 cited sources · Confidence: High on the primary-prevention nulls, the "deficiency is a cutoff artifact" and reverse-causation story, and the bolus/high-dose harm; Moderate on the deficiency-gated wins (mortality in the elderly, type-2 diabetes, pregnancy, respiratory infection) and the exact shape of the cancer-mortality signal.

    Start here¶

    Bottom line

    If you are not actually deficient, vitamin D pills do almost nothing for your health, and taking megadoses "to be safe" can quietly hurt you.

    Myths vs. reality

    What most people believe What the evidence actually shows
    "Almost everyone is deficient, so I probably am too." A cutoff trick. At the evidence-based line (20 ng/mL) only about 6% are truly deficient. Move the line to the industry-linked 30 ng/mL and suddenly "70 to 80% are insufficient."
    "Vitamin D is great for your bones." In healthy adults it does not cut fractures or raise bone density. The 25,871-person VITAL trial found no fewer fractures, not even in people with low levels, and 10,000 IU a day actually lost bone density over 3 years.
    "It protects your heart and prevents cancer and early death." Null in the big trials. An 83,000-person analysis put heart-event risk at exactly 1.00, and cancer and death rates did not move in already-sufficient people.
    "More is better, so megadose to be safe." The curve flattens, then bends toward harm. High daily doses lose bone, and a single big annual dose raised falls and fractures. The safe ceiling is 4,000 IU a day.
    "Low vitamin D causes all these diseases." Mostly backwards. Obesity lowers your level, and low D is usually a marker of being unwell, not the cause. The famous study "proving" low D causes heart disease and death was retracted twice.
    "It boosts testosterone, mood, and immunity." Null for testosterone even in men with genuinely low T, null for depression in an 18,353-person trial, and null for preventing COVID.
    "Sunbathe to get your vitamin D naturally." A cheap pill gets you the same blood level with none of the skin-cancer, melanoma, or ageing cost.
    "It cures winter depression (SAD)." That claim traces to one 1999 study of 15 people with no placebo group. The large trials show nothing.

    What you actually learn

    • The "everyone is deficient" panic is a number game: at the honest cutoff only about 6% of people are truly deficient, but shift the line and it jumps to 70 to 80%.
    • The Endocrine Society reversed its own 2011 guideline in 2024 and now says do NOT routinely test healthy people. The US Preventive Services Task Force agrees.
    • The classic "vitamin D for strong bones" idea collapsed. The biggest trial (25,871 people) found no fracture benefit at all, even in the low-level subgroup.
    • More is not safer: 10,000 IU a day lost bone density over 3 years versus a low dose, and big infrequent megadoses increase falls and fractures.
    • The celebrated science claiming low vitamin D causally drives heart disease and death was retracted twice because the method was broken.
    • Being overweight lowers your vitamin D reading, not the other way around, so a low number is often just a sign of poor health you cannot fix with a pill.
    • It does not raise testosterone (tested even in low-T men) and does not prevent depression (tested in 18,353 people) or COVID.
    • Where it genuinely works is correcting real deficiency: it cures rickets and osteomalacia, cuts fractures in the frail housebound elderly (with calcium), and lifelong sufficiency lowers multiple-sclerosis risk.
    • To fix a real deficit, a modest 1,000 to 2,000 IU taken daily is the sweet spot, under the 4,000 IU/day ceiling. Daily beats big weekly or monthly doses.

    What this means for you

    • If you feel fine and are not in a risk group, do not bother testing or supplementing. A sufficient level gains you almost nothing.
    • Only test your 25(OH)D if you are genuinely at risk: dark skin, housebound, obese, or with gut malabsorption.
    • Taking 5,000 IU "to be safe" with no recent blood test? Dial it down to about 1,000 to 2,000 IU a day unless a test shows you are low. Keep it daily, with K2 and a fatty meal.
    • If you are low, fix it with a modest daily dose, never a big weekly or monthly megadose. Bolus dosing backfires.
    • Do not sunbathe for vitamin D and do not chase 10,000 IU. More costs you bone; it does not add it.

    TL;DR

    Vitamin D is a real treatment for real deficiency that got mis-sold as a health booster for everyone. Correcting a genuine deficiency matters — it cures rickets and osteomalacia, cuts fractures in the deficient housebound elderly (with calcium),26 and lifelong sufficiency lowers multiple-sclerosis risk with rare causal support.27 But in already-replete adults, supplementation moves almost no hard outcome: it does not prevent heart disease,2 cancer,3 death,1 fractures,56 falls,9 depression,24 cognitive decline,25 COVID,28 or low testosterone.2021 Past ~1,000–2,000 IU/day the curve flattens then bends toward harm — 10,000 IU/day lost bone density in a 3-year trial,29 and bolus megadoses increase falls and fractures.30 "Everyone is deficient" is a cutoff artifact (~6% truly deficient at the IOM's 20 ng/mL, versus 70–80% "insufficient" at the industry-linked 30 ng/mL),1013 and the Endocrine Society reversed itself in 2024, now advising against routine screening of healthy people.14 The honest rule: test if you're at-risk, correct a real deficit with a modest daily dose, and stop chasing a number if you're already sufficient.

    The picture, ranked by how strongly the evidence agrees

    • Tier 1 — strongest consensus: in replete adults, supplements are null for CVD, cancer incidence, and death;123 the classic bone/fall indication collapsed (no fracture, fall, or BMD benefit — USPSTF advises against);569 "everyone is deficient" is a cutoff artifact and the Endocrine Society reversed itself in 2024;1014 obesity causally lowers vitamin D (reverse causation);17 and it does not raise testosterone or prevent depression.2024
    • Tier 2 — real but bounded: the genuine wins are all deficiency-correction or narrow — rickets/osteomalacia and deficient-elderly fractures (with calcium),26 lower MS risk (causal),27 lower incident autoimmune disease (HR 0.78),34 a modest causal blood-pressure drop,36 and a small D3 mortality benefit in the deficient elderly.37
    • Tier 3 — deficiency-gated or contested: type-2-diabetes prevention (deficient subgroup only),40 pregnancy (low-certainty),41 respiratory infection (small, now borderline),4446 and cancer mortality (daily dosing hints yes, bolus went the wrong way).34
    • Tier 4 — myths & marketing: "everyone is deficient, megadose everyone";12 the nonlinear-MR "low-D causes heart disease" proof (retracted twice);4850 "more is better" (10,000 IU loses bone);29 "sunbathe for your vitamin D" (same 25(OH)D, skin-cancer cost);58 and "it cures SAD" (one n=15 uncontrolled trial).59

    What this review covered¶

    An eight-part search swept the peer-reviewed literature across the flagship CVD/cancer/mortality trials, bone/fractures/falls, immune/respiratory/COVID/autoimmune, the deficiency-threshold and reverse-causation question, testosterone/mood/depression, toxicity and upper limits, the Mendelian-randomization/genetics evidence, and special populations (MS, diabetes, pregnancy). Every candidate PMID — 90 — was then independently title-matched against PubMed, which caught 3 wrong PMIDs the search agents had supplied (the VITAL-autoimmune trial, the CYP2R1/multiple-sclerosis study, and the COPSAC wheeze RCT), each corrected against the paper's own title and DOI, and confirmed 8 retraction/Expression-of-Concern notices. A recurring honesty note runs through it: the loudest "vitamin D is powerful" claims tend to come from an industry-linked guideline and small deficient-population trials, while the large, independent, publicly-funded mega-trials keep coming back null.

    Tier 1 — The strongest evidence¶

    In replete adults, supplements don't prevent heart disease, cancer, or death¶

    The flagship primary-prevention trials are large and mostly publicly-funded (vitamin D is generic, so there is no industry money inflating the positives here). VITAL (n=25,871, 2000 IU/day) was null on both co-primary endpoints — invasive cancer HR 0.96 and major cardiovascular events HR 0.971 — and the definitive 21-trial, 83,000-person meta-analysis put cardiovascular events at a risk ratio of 1.00.2 Cancer incidence is unmoved (RR 0.98),3 and all-cause mortality is null in the replete (D-Health, n=21,315, HR 1.04).4 The mortality benefit that does exist is confined to the deficient elderly (see Tier 2).

    The classic bone-and-fall indication collapsed¶

    This is the result that most surprises people. VITAL's fracture ancillary found no reduction in total, hip, or nonvertebral fractures — not even in the low-25(OH)D or low-bone-density subgroups5 — an 81-trial trial-sequential meta-analysis concluded further trials won't change the null and that vitamin D has no meaningful effect on bone density,67 and DO-HEALTH (adults ≥70) was null across fractures, function, infection, blood pressure and cognition.8 The US Preventive Services Task Force now recommends against vitamin D to prevent falls in community-dwelling older adults — a reversal of its earlier stance.9

    "Almost everyone is deficient" is an artifact of the cutoff you pick¶

    At the IOM's evidence-based 20 ng/mL, true deficiency is ~6% of US adults10 and ~13% of Europeans at <12 ng/mL.11 The 30 ng/mL cutoff — popularized by a guideline and review whose lead author has extensive vitamin-D-industry ties12 — manufactures the "70–80% insufficient" pandemic.13 The clearest sign of the correction: the Endocrine Society reversed its own 2011 guideline in 2024, now recommending against routine 25(OH)D screening in healthy people and abandoning the 30 ng/mL target,14 joining the IOM15 and USPSTF.16 And the causal arrow often runs backwards: Mendelian randomization shows obesity lowers 25(OH)D (largely volumetric dilution), not the reverse,1718 and low vitamin D is mostly a marker of ill health, not its cause.19

    It does not raise testosterone, or prevent depression¶

    Two dedicated trials found no testosterone effect — in healthy men with normal T (it even worsened one insulin index)20 and in men with genuinely low T21 — and a pooled meta-analysis is null.22 The whole "sunshine vitamin boosts T" belief traces to one small, deficient, weight-loss-confounded, never-replicated study.23 Mood and cognition are the same story: VITAL-DEP, the largest depression-prevention trial ever run (n=18,353), found no benefit,24 and the VITAL cognition ancillaries were null in healthy elders.25

    Tier 2 — Real but bounded (all deficiency-correction or narrow)¶

    • Rickets, osteomalacia, and fractures in the deficient housebound elderly are the genuine, settled wins: 800 IU D3 + calcium cut hip fractures 43% in institutionalized, deficient, calcium-poor women (Chapuy) — deficiency + calcium + a housebound population, which is why it does not generalize to replete community-dwellers.26
    • Lower multiple-sclerosis risk — two large cohorts (US military serum OR 0.38 top-vs-bottom quintile)3132 plus Mendelian randomization27 and a CYP2R1 single-gene instrument33 make MS the cleanest "deficiency is genuinely harmful" story. It is about lifelong sufficiency, not treating established MS.
    • Lower incident autoimmune disease — VITAL's autoimmune ancillary cut new autoimmune disease HR 0.78 over ~5 years,34 the strongest hard-outcome win in the project (it attenuated after supplements stopped, so it may need continuous use).35
    • A modest, genuinely causal blood-pressure reduction (Mendelian randomization).36
    • A modest all-cause-mortality benefit — D3 only, in the deficient/elderly/institutionalized (Chowdhury RR 0.89; Cochrane), not in replete adults.3738

    Tier 3 — Deficiency-gated or genuinely contested¶

    • Type-2 diabetes: null overall in replete prediabetics (D2d, DPVD),39 with a modest ~15% reduction concentrated in the deficient or those reaching high 25(OH)D on treatment.40
    • Pregnancy: probably reduces pre-eclampsia and low birthweight — but at moderate-to-low certainty, and the 2024 Cochrane update downgraded several outcomes.41 Prenatal vitamin D and offspring asthma/wheeze is real-but-borderline (both landmark RCTs missed their primary endpoint).4243
    • Respiratory infection: the benefit was always modest (2017 OR 0.88, daily/weekly only, strongest in profound deficiency),44 and after the large null CORONAVIT trial45 and newer studies it lost significance entirely in 2025 (OR 0.94, p=0.057).46
    • Cancer mortality (not incidence): daily-dosing meta-analyses hint at ~13–16% lower cancer mortality,347 but monthly-bolus D-Health went the opposite way (HR 1.15).4 Unsettled and dosing-dependent.

    Tier 4 — Myths & marketing¶

    • "Everyone is deficient — test and megadose everyone." Rests on the contested, industry-linked 30 ng/mL cutoff;12 the IOM,10 USPSTF,16 and the 2024 Endocrine Society14 all reject routine screening of healthy adults.
    • "Mendelian randomization proves low vitamin D causes heart disease and death." The celebrated "L-shaped benefit at deficiency" was retracted twice — the Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology nonlinear-MR paper (retracted and republished with no causal effect at any level)4849 and the European Heart Journal cardiovascular paper (retracted 2025)50 — because the residual-stratification method was biased.51 Robust MR is null for CVD, type-2 diabetes, and most cancers.5253
    • "More is better — megadose 10,000 IU to be safe." Vitamin D is fat-soluble and stored: high daily doses lost bone density,29 bolus megadoses increased falls and fractures,3054 and the IOM upper limit is 4,000 IU/day.15 The much-feared kidney-stone risk is really the calcium co-supplement, not vitamin D alone;5556 and a reverse-J mortality curve shows harm at very high serum too.57
    • "Sunbathe to get your vitamin D." The same UVB that makes vitamin D also drives skin carcinogenesis; an oral supplement achieves the identical 25(OH)D with none of the DNA-damage, melanoma, or photoaging cost.58
    • "Vitamin D cures seasonal affective disorder." The claim traces to a 1999 study of n=15 with no placebo arm;59 the large depression trials are null.24
    • "Vitamin D toxicity is common." Frank intoxication needs sustained very high intake or manufacturing errors (e.g. men who took 186,400 and 970,000 IU/day from mislabeled products),60 not typical 1,000–4,000 IU use.

    Who actually benefits¶

    • People with a genuinely low measured 25(OH)D — dark skin, housebound, malabsorption, obesity, the institutionalized elderly. Correcting a real deficit is the whole point.2610
    • The deficient elderly, with calcium — for fractures, in a deficient/housebound population, not replete community-dwellers.26
    • Anyone concerned about MS risk — lifelong sufficiency, not high-dose treatment.27
    • Pregnant women with low status — targeted, moderate-certainty benefit.41
    • Everyone else (healthy, replete): little to gain from supplementing, a real harm to lose from megadosing or bolus dosing,2930 and no reason to routinely test.14

    The bottom line¶

    Vitamin D is neither the cure-all the 2010s made it nor useless. It is a genuine treatment for genuine deficiency — rickets, osteomalacia, the deficient housebound elderly, lifelong MS-relevant sufficiency — with a couple of real bounded wins (autoimmune-disease incidence, a modest causal blood-pressure effect). But for a healthy, replete adult taking it "to be safe," the strongest evidence says it moves almost no hard outcome — not heart disease, cancer, death, fractures, falls, mood, cognition, COVID, or testosterone — and megadosing or bolus dosing adds real harm. The "everyone is deficient" panic was a cutoff artifact the Endocrine Society itself has now walked back. The evidence-based move is to correct a measured deficiency with a modest daily dose and otherwise leave a sufficient level alone.

    The full findings — all 40¶

    Every conclusion this review reached, grouped by how strongly the evidence backs it. Each finding is distilled from the fully-cited evidence above.

    Tier 1 — Strongest, settled (14 findings)

    1. Vitamin D supplements do not prevent cardiovascular disease in primary prevention

    The flagship trials converge on null: VITAL (2000 IU/d, n=25,871) major CV events HR 0.97, ViDA (monthly bolus) HR ~1.02, and the definitive 21-RCT / 83,291-person meta-analysis MACE RR 1.00 with no effect on MI, stroke, or CV death.

    2. Vitamin D supplements do not reduce total cancer incidence

    VITAL invasive-cancer HR 0.96 (null) and the RCT meta-analysis cancer-incidence RR 0.98. Any cancer signal is in mortality, not incidence (see #23).

    3. Vitamin D does not reduce all-cause mortality in unscreened, largely-replete adults

    VITAL and D-Health (n=21,315, monthly 60,000 IU) both null for all-cause mortality (D-Health HR 1.04). The mortality benefit that exists is confined to the deficient/elderly (see #15).

    4. In replete community adults, vitamin D does not prevent fractures or raise bone density

    VITAL's fracture ancillary (n=25,871) found no reduction in total, hip, or nonvertebral fractures — not even in low-25(OH)D or low-BMD subgroups; an 81-RCT trial-sequential meta-analysis says the same and that further trials won't change it; DO-HEALTH (≥70 yr) was null across fractures, function, infection, BP, and cognition; and BMD meta-analyses show no meaningful effect.

    5. Vitamin D does not prevent falls — the USPSTF recommends against it for that purpose

    The trial-sequential meta-analysis found no fall benefit, and the US Preventive Services Task Force recommends AGAINST vitamin D to prevent falls in community-dwelling adults ≥65 — a reversal of its 2012 stance.

    6. "Almost everyone is deficient" is an artifact of the cutoff you pick

    At the IOM's evidence-based 20 ng/mL, true deficiency is ~6% of US adults (Manson) and ~13% of Europeans at <12 ng/mL (assay-standardized, Cashman); the industry-linked 30 ng/mL cutoff manufactures the "70–80% insufficient" pandemic. The IOM RDA (600–800 IU) already covers ~97.5% of people at 20 ng/mL.

    7. The Endocrine Society reversed itself in 2024 — against routine screening of healthy people

    The 2024 guideline (Demay) recommends AGAINST routine 25(OH)D screening in healthy adults and abandons the 30 ng/mL target — a major about-face from its own industry-linked 2011 guideline; the USPSTF concurs that screening evidence is insufficient.

    8. Obesity causally LOWERS 25(OH)D (not the reverse) — mostly volumetric dilution

    Bidirectional Mendelian randomization shows higher BMI causes lower 25(OH)D (~4% ↓ per 10% ↑BMI), while low 25(OH)D does not cause higher BMI; the low level in obesity is best explained by distribution across a larger body volume, not fat sequestration. Much "deficiency → disease" is this reverse direction.

    9. The real, settled benefit is correcting genuine deficiency — rickets/osteomalacia, and fractures in the deficient, housebound elderly

    Repletion cures rickets and osteomalacia (the original reason vitamin D matters), and in institutionalized, deficient, calcium-poor women (mean 84 yr, 25(OH)D ~16 ng/mL) 800 IU D3 + calcium cut hip fractures 43% and nonvertebral 32% (Chapuy). This is deficiency + calcium in a housebound population; it does not generalize to replete community-dwellers.

    10. Vitamin D does not raise testosterone — in replete OR low-testosterone men

    Two dedicated RCTs found no androgen effect: 20,000 IU/wk in healthy men with normal T (it even worsened one insulin index) and vitamin D in men with LOW T + deficiency; a pooled RCT meta-analysis is null. The "sunshine vitamin boosts T" belief rests on one small, deficient, weight-loss-confounded, never-replicated subgroup (Pilz).

    11. Vitamin D does not prevent depression or cognitive decline in the general population

    VITAL-DEP — the largest depression-prevention RCT ever run (n=18,353, 2000 IU/d, ~5 yr) — found no reduction in depression and no mood-score change, matched by a null in symptomatic older adults and null VITAL cognition ancillaries.

    12. The evidence-based ceiling is the IOM's 4,000 IU/day upper limit; sufficiency is 20 ng/mL

    Routine megadosing exceeds the tolerable upper intake level set by the IOM and reaffirmed (restrained intakes) by the 2024 Endocrine Society.

    13. Vitamin D does not prevent or treat COVID-19 in the general population

    Bias-resistant Mendelian randomization found no causal effect on COVID susceptibility, hospitalization, or severity; a hospital RCT (single 200,000 IU) was null; and the pragmatic 6,200-person test-and-treat CORONAVIT trial found no reduction in COVID or all-cause respiratory infection.

    14. Large intermittent/bolus dosing INCREASES falls and fractures — a reproducible harm

    A single annual 500,000 IU raised falls (RR 1.15) and fractures (RR 1.26), worst in the first 3 months; monthly 60,000 IU produced more falls than a lower dose (66.9% vs 47.9%) — which is why the USPSTF advises against it for falls.

    Tier 2 — Probable, real but bounded (8 findings)

    15. A modest all-cause-mortality reduction exists — but only with D3, concentrated in the deficient/elderly/institutionalized

    Meta-analyses find D3 (not D2) lowers all-cause mortality ~6–11% (Chowdhury RR 0.89; Cochrane ~RR 0.94), driven almost entirely by elderly, largely institutionalized cohorts — i.e. deficiency correction, not "boosting".

    16. Supplementation lowers incident autoimmune disease over ~5 years — the rare genuine hard-outcome win

    VITAL's autoimmune ancillary (2000 IU/d, n=25,871) cut incident autoimmune disease HR 0.78 in a large, non-deficient population — the strongest "real win" in the project. Caveat: the benefit attenuated and lost significance two years after supplements stopped, so it may need continuous use.

    17. Higher vitamin D status lowers multiple-sclerosis risk — with rare causal support

    Two large cohorts (Nurses' Health, ~187k; US military, OR 0.38 top-vs-bottom quintile) plus Mendelian randomization (genetically-lower 25(OH)D → ~2× MS risk) and a strong CYP2R1 single-gene instrument make MS the cleanest "deficiency is genuinely harmful" story. It is about lifelong sufficiency/avoiding deficiency, not high-dose treatment of established MS.

    18. Genetically higher 25(OH)D causally lowers blood pressure — modestly

    Mendelian randomization found each 10% higher instrumented 25(OH)D → lower systolic/diastolic BP and ~8% lower hypertension odds — one of the few hard outcomes where MR survives (see also #34).

    19. High daily doses (10,000 IU) dose-dependently LOSE bone density vs 400 IU

    The 3-year Calgary RCT (400 vs 4,000 vs 10,000 IU/d) found higher doses caused greater loss of radial/tibial volumetric BMD with no strength gain — from a supplement-friendly funder. A single striking RCT, not yet replicated, but it directly refutes "more is better for bone."

    20. Vitamin D raises hypercalcemia/hypercalciuria even at sub-toxic doses; the kidney-stone risk is the CALCIUM, not D alone

    A 48-study meta found higher hypercalcemia (RR 1.54) and hypercalciuria (RR 1.64) but no significant stone increase from D alone; the +17% stone signal comes from the calcium co-supplement (WHI), and D-alone VITAL did not raise stones.

    21. Low 25(OH)D behaves mainly as a MARKER of ill health, not a cause

    A systematic review of ~290 cohorts + 172 RCTs found low D tracks disease observationally but supplementation doesn't reproduce the benefit — so low D is largely a downstream marker of illness, inactivity, and inflammation (Autier).

    22. The respiratory-infection benefit is small and has now decayed to borderline

    It started modest (Martineau 2017 ARI OR 0.88, daily/weekly only, strongest in profound deficiency), attenuated (2021, OR 0.92), and after adding the null CORONAVIT and newer trials LOST significance (2025, OR 0.94, p=0.057).

    Tier 3 — Weaker, mixed, or conditional (10 findings)

    23. Vitamin D may modestly lower cancer MORTALITY (not incidence) — but the dosing evidence contradicts itself

    Daily-dosing meta-analyses hint at ~13–16% lower cancer mortality (Keum RR 0.87; Zhang RR 0.84; VITAL post-hoc HR 0.75) — but monthly-bolus D-Health went the OPPOSITE way (cancer mortality HR 1.15; 1.24 excluding early years). Unsettled and dosing-dependent.

    24. Vitamin D does not prevent type-2 diabetes in replete prediabetics — only a modest signal in the deficient

    D2d (4,000 IU/d) and DPVD (active analog) were both null overall (HR 0.88 / 0.87, NS); the 3-RCT IPD meta found ~15% lower T2D (HR 0.85) concentrated in those deficient at baseline or reaching 25(OH)D ≥50 ng/mL — a clean deficient-vs-replete split.

    25. In pregnancy, vitamin D probably reduces pre-eclampsia and low birthweight — at moderate-to-low certainty

    The Cochrane review found D alone probably lowers pre-eclampsia (RR 0.48), gestational diabetes, and low birthweight, but the 2024 update downgraded several outcomes — genuine but not settled.

    26. Prenatal vitamin D and offspring asthma/wheeze is real-but-borderline

    Both landmark RCTs missed their primary endpoint (VDAART OR 0.80; COPSAC HR 0.76, each non-significant); a pooled analysis reached significance but VDAART's long-term follow-up was null — the effect, if any, is small.

    27. Deficiency may causally raise dementia risk — but on a now-contested method

    One nonlinear MR supports a causal deficiency→dementia threshold effect (~54% higher risk at 25 vs 50 nmol/L), but it uses the same residual nonlinear-MR method that failed for CVD/mortality (see #34) and is unreplicated. Hold lightly.

    28. Vitamin D cuts asthma and COPD exacerbations only when it corrects baseline deficiency

    IPD meta-analyses show benefit for asthma (RR 0.74) and COPD (RR ~0.55) exacerbations driven almost entirely by baseline <25 nmol/L, with replete-population trials null — a deficiency-correction effect, not a general one.

    29. Vitamin D as an adjunct in already-diagnosed depression shows a positive pooled effect — from weak trials

    A meta-analysis found ES 0.58 favoring vitamin D in diagnosed MDD, but from small, heterogeneous, low-quality trials; it is a treatment question, not prevention, and the huge VITAL-DEP is null for the general population.

    30. Correcting deficiency modestly improves fatigue and depressive symptoms — in the deficient/obese only

    High-dose D improved depression scores in overweight/deficient adults (Jorde) and fatigue in deficient adults (Nowak) — both confined to low-25(OH)D populations, not the general public.

    31. The 25(OH)D–mortality curve is reverse-J/U-shaped — harm appears at very HIGH serum too

    A 247,574-person cohort found lowest mortality at ~50–70 nmol/L and rising mortality above ~100–140 nmol/L — observational (reverse causation not excluded), but consistent with the trial evidence that more is not better.

    32. "Free 25(OH)D resolves the racial deficiency paradox" is assay-dependent and did not replicate

    Powe argued Black Americans' low total 25(OH)D is offset by lower binding protein so free D is normal; but that was largely a monoclonal-assay artifact (the GC1F variant), and polyclonal/LC-MS assays do not equalize free 25(OH)D. Unresolved.

    Tier 4 — Myths, misconceptions, and marketing (6 findings)

    33. "Everyone is deficient — test and megadose everyone" rests on a contested, industry-linked cutoff

    The 30 ng/mL "insufficiency" threshold was popularized by a guideline and review whose lead author has vitamin-D-industry ties; the IOM (20 ng/mL), the USPSTF, and the 2024 Endocrine Society all reject routine screening of healthy adults.

    34. The Mendelian-randomization "proof" that low vitamin D causally drives CVD/mortality does NOT hold

    The celebrated "L-shaped benefit at low 25(OH)D" was retracted twice — the Lancet DE nonlinear-MR paper (retracted + republished with NO causal effect at any level) and the European Heart Journal CVD paper (retracted 2025) — because the residual-stratification method was biased; the corrected doubly-ranked method dissolved the L-shapes. Robust MR is null for CVD, T2D and most cancers.

    35. "More is better — megadose 10,000 IU to be safe" is wrong

    Vitamin D is fat-soluble and stored, and the risk-benefit curve flattens then bends toward harm: high daily doses lose bone density, bolus megadoses increase falls, and even the pro-higher-intake safety case (Vieth) was partly undercut by those later RCTs.

    36. "Sunbathe to get your vitamin D" is a bad trade

    UVB drives BOTH cutaneous vitamin-D synthesis and skin carcinogenesis; an oral supplement achieves the same 25(OH)D with none of the DNA-damage, melanoma, or photoaging cost, and no trial shows sun-for-D beats a pill on hard outcomes.

    37. "Vitamin D cures seasonal affective disorder" rests on one tiny, uncontrolled trial

    The claim traces to a 1999 study of n=15 with NO placebo arm — far too small and uncontrolled to support it; the large depression RCTs are null.

    38. Vitamin D toxicity is not common at ordinary doses

    Frank intoxication (severe hypercalcemia, nephrocalcinosis) generally needs sustained very high intake or manufacturing/labeling errors — e.g. two men who took 186,400 and 970,000 IU/day from mislabeled products — not typical 1,000–4,000 IU use. (The sub-toxic harms in #14/#19/#20 are the real-world concern, not classic poisoning.)

    Practical — what to actually do (2 findings)

    39. What actually matters: correct a measured deficiency; don't chase a number in a well person

    For a healthy, replete adult, supplementation moves almost no hard outcome — the payoff is fixing genuine deficiency (rickets/osteomalacia, the truly deficient/elderly, MS-relevant lifelong sufficiency). Use daily (not mega-bolus) dosing, roughly 800–2,000 IU/day to replace a real deficit; test 25(OH)D only if at-risk (dark skin, housebound, malabsorption, obesity — which blunts the response); and stay well under the 4,000 IU upper limit.

    40. Is a 5,000 IU/day habit justified? Only on a measured low level

    5,000 IU/day sits above the IOM RDA (600–800 IU) and above what deficiency-correction needs, though below the 10,000 IU bone-loss signal. The evidence-based read: it is justified if a 25(OH)D test shows deficiency, or with obesity/malabsorption; otherwise about 1,000–2,000 IU/day daily matches the data, kept daily (not bolus), with K2 and a fatty meal.
