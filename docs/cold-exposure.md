# Deliberate Cold Exposure & Ice Baths: What It Actually Does vs What People Think

> A short, strong stress response with modest, mostly subjective benefits and a real risk in the first minutes. The "+250 % dopamine" is plasma dopamine after an hour in 14 °C water, not a brain measure; ice baths ease soreness a little, and straight after lifting they blunt muscle growth; brown fat burns about 6 kcal a day while people eat 230 to 270 kcal more afterwards; the one large shower trial cut sick-leave 29 % but not illness days; and "11 minutes a week" is a descriptive mean, not a tested dose.

*Literature reviewed to 15 November 2026. 274 sources, every identifier resolved against PubMed.*

## Start here

!!! quote "Bottom line"
    **Deliberate cold is a real stressor with a small, specific payoff: it reliably raises noradrenaline 2- to 6-fold, modestly eases soreness in low-quality trials and helps performance in the heat, but its brown-fat heat is about 6 kcal a day, it blunts muscle growth when used straight after lifting, the dopamine, immunity, testosterone and heart claims fail the evidence, and the real danger is cold shock and breath-holding in the first minutes, not hypothermia.**

**Myths vs. reality**

| The claim you've heard | What the evidence actually shows |
|---|---|
| "Cold water gives you +250 % dopamine for hours" | The figure is one plasma measurement during a 1-hour head-out immersion at 14 °C, with no brain measure, no mood outcome and no statement that levels stayed high for hours. The same laboratory, with the identical protocol, found dopamine "not increased significantly". Plasma dopamine does not reflect brain dopamine anyway: the blood-brain barrier blocks and degrades circulating monoamines, and the gut makes close to half of the body's dopamine. |
| "Ice baths speed recovery" | Against passive rest, cold water lowers soreness by SMD −0.55 at 24 hours and −0.93 at 72 hours in 17 small trials, and the evidence is low quality. Strength recovery does not change significantly. Thermoneutral 34.7 °C water presented as a recovery treatment was "as effective as CWI" at 10.3 °C, and after a marathon cryotherapy was "no more effective than a placebo intervention". |
| "A plunge after lifting is free recovery" | Cold straight after resistance training reduces strength gains by a small pooled ES −0.23 (whole-body immersion alone ES −0.08, not significant). A leg cooled in 8 °C water for 20 minutes after lifting made about 11 % less myofibrillar protein per day over two weeks, and an umbrella review concluded that cold "impaired strength and hypertrophic gains", at low to very low certainty. |
| "Cold plunges burn fat" | Directly measured brown-fat heat is 6.3 kcal a day during mild cold in people with the largest depots, about 1 % of the extra heat the body makes. People ate about 230 to 270 kcal more at the next meal after immersion. No study has shown weight loss from cold-induced brown-fat activation, and three 4-week blocks of post-exercise immersion left rugby players' fat mass unchanged (p = 0.801). |
| "11 minutes a week is the minimum effective dose" | 11 minutes is the self-reported mean weekly cold-water time of 7 or 8 Danish winter swimmers, who also took 57 minutes of sauna, in a cross-sectional study that tested no dose. The figure sits in a full-text table, not the abstract, and the "500 to 1,000 kcal/24 h" rise is a 30-minute reading expressed as a daily rate, about 12 to 21 kcal. |
| "Cold boosts your immune system" | No study found here measured laboratory-confirmed infections. Pooled immune markers showed no effect immediately (SMD −0.16) or at one hour (SMD −0.18). The one large shower trial cut self-reported sick leave 29 % but not illness days, and in the endotoxin trial cold alone changed nothing (P = .77): the breathing technique did (P = .002). |
| "Cryotherapy chambers beat ice baths" | Ten minutes in 8 °C water cooled deep leg muscle 2.9 °C, against 1.3 °C after two minutes of −110 °C air. The Cochrane review of whole-body cryotherapy found 4 RCTs in 64 people with very low quality evidence, a 13-RCT meta-analysis found cold water better for 24-hour soreness, and after a marathon chamber cold harmed muscle function compared with water. |
| "Contrast therapy flushes out lactic acid" | Two errors. Lactate does not cause next-day soreness: downhill running caused soreness without a lactate rise. And contrast cleared lactate only 0.28 against 0.22 mmol L⁻¹ min⁻¹; active recovery near the lactate threshold clears it fastest. |
| "Cold boosts testosterone" | Two hours at 10 °C left testosterone and LH unchanged in 20 men, and 15 minutes at 15 °C after squats left testosterone 10.4 % below baseline at 60 minutes. The one rise was salivary testosterone after chamber cold in 14 footballers, not seen in serum in another trial. |
| "Winter swimming protects the heart" | No outcome study exists. The Finnish HR 0.37 for sudden cardiac death is a sauna result, and 15 days of daily Wim Hof Method changed no blood-pressure, heart-rate-variability or arterial-stiffness measure. Cold constricts diseased coronary arteries, cutting flow 39 % across stenoses above 50 %. |

**What you actually learn**

- **The danger is the first minutes, not hypothermia:** at 10 °C the first 30 seconds gave about 47 breaths/min, 68 L/min of ventilation and 128 beats/min, and cold shock is the expert-estimated cause of most open-water immersion deaths. Lean swimmers lost only 1.57 °C/h of core temperature at 16 °C, and most people overestimate how fast hypothermia arrives.
- **Cold shock habituates, but only partly:** about four short immersions cut heart rate by a pooled 14 beats/min and ventilation by 21.3 L/min, and the effect lasts months. Anxiety can reverse it, most of the tachycardia remains, and 15 °C showers did nothing.
- **Breath-holding is where the heart goes wrong:** breath-holds in 5 to 10 °C water produced ectopic arrhythmias in 29 of 36 submersions in healthy subjects, mostly in the 10 seconds after the breath-hold ended. Hyperventilation first more than doubles breath-hold time and is the documented route to silent blackout: 23 deaths in 58 cases.
- **Noradrenaline is the reliable signal:** cold water raises it 2- to 6-fold, inside the 1.5- to more than 20-fold range of exercise, and it returns to baseline about 30 minutes after rewarming. No human study has measured brain dopamine during cold water.
- **Brown fat is real and small:** mild cold at 16 °C activated it in 23 of 24 healthy men, and detection falls with age, BMI and summer. Acclimation changes where heat comes from, not how much: shivering fell 21 to 36 % while total heat production stayed the same.
- **Recovery gains are soreness, not function:** soreness falls modestly in low-quality trials, strength recovery does not change, part of the felt benefit is expectation, and inflammatory markers rise for at least an hour after a plunge (pooled SMD 1.03 immediately, 1.26 at one hour).
- **Endurance adaptation is neutral:** after endurance or interval training, cold water neither clearly harms nor clearly improves aerobic adaptation (pooled SMD −0.07 for maximal aerobic power).
- **Heat is where cold clearly helps:** cold-water pre-cooling before exercise in the heat improved endurance performance (g = +0.53), and 15 minutes of cold water between two hot bouts prevented a 4.1 % fall in cycling work. Ice slurry showed no clear effect, and the trials studied mainly men.
- **Dose is depth and medium:** ten minutes at 10 °C cools the quadriceps 6.40 °C at 1 cm but only 1.61 °C at 3 cm, and body fat changes the dose. Water cools muscle more than colder air, and no optimal temperature-duration pair exists: trials span about 5 to 15 °C for 2.5 to 15 minutes.
- **Mood evidence leans favourable but does not establish a benefit:** controlled-only analyses attenuate, RCT-only pooling found no mood effect, and cold showers plus Wim Hof breathing matched warm showers plus slow breathing (both −24 % depressive symptoms). The menopause benefits come from self-selected surveys.
- **The metabolic headline is narrow:** the "+43 % insulin sensitivity" result came from 8 people with type 2 diabetes, worked through muscle and disappeared when shivering was prevented, while 16 daily 10-minute plunges at 14 °C temporarily decreased insulin sensitivity.
- **Long cold harms infection defence:** surgical patients held at 34.7 °C instead of 36.6 °C had 19 % against 6 % wound infections, and brief foot chilling was followed by more self-reported colds (13 of 90 against 5 of 90). A plunge followed by rewarming is a different exposure.

!!! tip "What this means for you"
    - **Never immerse alone in open water:** enter slowly, keep your head out and kick at entry, and expect the first minutes of gasping and a fast heart rate to be the risky part.
    - **Never breath-hold or hyperventilate in cold water:** that combination produced arrhythmias in healthy subjects and is the documented route to silent blackout.
    - **Do not trust comfort:** core temperature did not track thermal sensation, so set a time before you get in, and warm up afterwards rather than staying chilled.
    - **If muscle growth is your goal, keep cold away from the minutes after lifting:** every blunting trial applied cold within minutes, and whether a 4 to 6 hour gap removes the effect is untested.
    - **Use cold where the evidence is clearest:** pre-cooling or a cold-water break before or between bouts of exercise in the heat.
    - **Do not use cold as a fat-loss tool:** a 3-minute plunge plausibly adds about 10 to 15 kcal, less than the extra food people ate after immersion.
    - **If you enjoy cold showers, 30 seconds performed as well as 90 seconds** for sick-leave days; no morning-only or fasted-only rule has support, and evening post-exercise immersion left whole-night sleep unchanged.
    - **Schedule skilled work before, not straight after, a long or very cold exposure:** measured cognition falls about 14 % in cold of 10 °C or less and can stay impaired 60 minutes after rewarming.
    - **Get a medical assessment before regular plunging if you have:** cold urticaria (37 % of test-positive patients had anaphylaxis, most often from full immersion), coronary disease, heart failure, a channelopathy or unexplained fainting. Raynaud's and past non-freezing cold injury also have records of harm, and pregnancy has no data.

## 1. Executive Summary and the Chain of Claims

Deliberate cold exposure is one of the most promoted "biohacking" protocols, and exercise guides often file it among the add-ons with weak evidence. This review tests what cold plunges, ice baths, cold showers and cryotherapy chambers actually do in a healthy adult. The popular case runs as a chain of eight claims. First, that a cold plunge raises dopamine by 250 % for hours. Second, that ice baths speed recovery. Third, that cold switches on brown fat and burns serious calories. Fourth, that cold showers make you sick less often and "boost the immune system". Fifth, that eleven minutes a week is the minimum effective dose. Sixth, that a cryotherapy chamber at −110 °C beats a tub of cold water. Seventh, that contrast therapy flushes lactic acid. Eighth, that the practice is safe for anyone who can tolerate the discomfort.

The short version has eight parts.

**The dopamine figure is a plasma number from an hour in the water.** The "+250 %" comes from Šrámek 2000: young men sat head-out for one hour in 14 °C water; plasma noradrenaline rose 530 %, plasma dopamine 250 % and metabolic rate 350 % [^S94]. The same Prague laboratory, on the identical protocol, found dopamine "not increased significantly" beside a fourfold noradrenaline rise [^S8], and other laboratories found no dopamine change at 5 °C or on a cold pressor test [^S5] [^S3]. Circulating dopamine does not cross the blood-brain barrier in meaningful amounts and about half of it comes from the gut [^S2] [^S10]; no human study has measured brain dopamine during cold water. Noradrenaline does rise reliably, two- to sixfold, and returns to baseline about 30 minutes after rewarming [^S1] [^S20]. On mood, the best pooled evidence "does not establish" a benefit [^S43], and an RCT-only review found better sleep and quality of life but not mood [^S40].

**Ice baths ease soreness a little and, straight after lifting, cost some muscle.** Cochrane pools 17 small trials to soreness SMD −0.55 at 24 hours and −0.93 at 72 hours, from low-quality evidence [^S48]; across 52 studies, strength recovery did not improve [^S83]; and a thermoneutral bath presented as a recovery treatment worked as well as 10 °C water [^S52]. Taken within minutes of resistance training for 7 to 12 weeks, cold water reduced type II fibre growth [^S59] [^S73], a cooled leg made about 11 % less muscle protein a day [^S75], and pooled strength gains fell by SMD −0.60 [^S78]. No trial has tested whether waiting hours removes the effect.

**Brown fat is real and small.** Mild cold activates brown fat in most young adults [^S100] [^S98], but its own heat is about 6 kcal a day in mild cold [^S108] and about 1 % of the extra heat the body makes when cold [^S119]; most of the rest is muscle and shivering [^S114]. People ate 230 to 270 kcal more after immersion [^S140] [^S95], and no study has shown weight loss from cold-induced brown-fat activation [^S128].

**The immune case is one unblinded shower trial and a breathing technique.** In 3,018 Dutch adults, 30 to 90 seconds of cold at the end of a hot shower cut self-reported sickness absence 29 % (IRR 0.71) while illness days did not change significantly [^S29]. The famous endotoxin result came from the Wim Hof breathing technique; cold training alone did not change the inflammatory response [^S28] [^S34]. Pooled immune markers do not move, and inflammatory markers rise for at least an hour after a plunge [^S40].

**The danger is in the first minutes.** Cold shock drives a gasp, breathing at 47 breaths a minute and heart rate near 128 in the first 30 seconds at 10 °C [^S188]; breath-holding in cold water triggered ectopic beats in 29 of 36 submersions [^S185]; and 90 of 120 triathlon sudden deaths or arrests happened in the swim [^S202]. The cold shock response habituates after about four immersions [^S38] and the habituation lasts months [^S13].

**"Eleven minutes a week" is a descriptive mean, not a dose.** It is the self-reported weekly cold-water time of seven or eight Danish winter swimmers, who also took 57 minutes of sauna, in a cross-sectional study that assigned no dose [^S134].

**A colder chamber is not a colder muscle.** Ten minutes in 8 °C water cooled deep muscle 2.9 °C against 1.3 °C for two minutes of −110 °C air [^S228]; the Cochrane review of whole-body cryotherapy found 4 RCTs in 64 people and very low quality evidence [^S56]; and lactate does not cause next-day soreness, so "flushing" it is not a recovery mechanism [^S244].

**The rule.** For a healthy adult, deliberate cold exposure is an optional, short, strong sympathetic stress with a modest effect on soreness and on how people feel, a proven use in pre-cooling for exercise in the heat [^S230], and a real but small risk concentrated in the first minutes, in open water, in breath-holding and in people with heart disease or cold urticaria. It is not a fat-loss tool, an immune supplement, a dopamine treatment or a recovery aid for someone whose goal is muscle growth.

## 2. Neurochemistry, Mood and the "+250 % Dopamine" Claim

### 2.1 What the source study measured

The popular version runs: a cold plunge raises dopamine by 250 %, keeps it high for hours and rivals cocaine, so you get lasting focus, motivation and mood. The figure comes from a Charles University laboratory study in which "a group of young men" sat in head-out water immersion for one hour at 32 °C, 20 °C and 14 °C, against a control condition in ambient air, designed to separate the effect of cold from that of hydrostatic pressure [^S94]. At 14 °C for one hour, metabolic rate rose by 350 %, plasma noradrenaline by 530 % and plasma dopamine by 250 %, while plasma adrenaline stayed unchanged and cortisol "tended to decrease"; at 20 °C metabolic rate rose 93 % and the abstract reports no catecholamine increase [^S94]. The authors concluded that "responses induced by cold are mainly due to increased activity of the sympathetic nervous system" [^S94]. The abstract gives neither the number of men nor the blood-sampling times [full text, not verified here], and it contains no brain, cerebrospinal-fluid or imaging measure, no psychological outcome, no short plunge, no water below 14 °C and no statement that dopamine stayed high "for hours". "+250 %" is a 3.5-fold rise, not 2.5-fold; "+530 %" noradrenaline is 6.3-fold. A companion study from the same group gave 40 mg of propranolol before immersion at 12.5 °C and found that the beta-adrenergic route carried "about 80 %" of the metabolic increase in the first 20 minutes of cooling and about 20 % by the end [^S12], which frames this laboratory's cold response as peripheral, sympathetic and metabolic.

The dopamine number has not been reproduced, including by its own laboratory. Four years earlier the same Prague group, with Šrámek as second author, used the identical protocol (head-out, 14 °C, 1 h) in young athletes: one immersion produced "a four-fold increase (P < 0.05) in plasma noradrenaline", while "plasma adrenaline and dopamine concentrations were not increased significantly", and three immersions a week for six weeks gave "no clear change in reactivity of the sympathetic nervous system" [^S8]. In 16 US Navy divers in 5 °C water in dry suits, a military-funded study, noradrenaline rose more than three-fold and adrenaline rose significantly, with "no change in ... dopamine" [^S5]. In a US Public Health Service-funded study, basal plasma dopamine sat below the assay sensitivity of 42 pg/ml in 31 of 41 healthy volunteers, and a hand-in-ice-water cold pressor test in six people produced "no significant changes in DA or E concentrations" while noradrenaline rose by 212 ± 66 pg/ml; circulating dopamine joins the stress response "only when the adrenal component is maximally activated" [^S3]. A 3.5-fold rise from a baseline near an assay floor is a small absolute change, and the absolute values of the 2000 paper are not in its abstract [full text, not verified here]. The reproducible finding is the noradrenaline rise.

### 2.2 Plasma dopamine is not brain dopamine

The step from "plasma dopamine rose" to "your brain got more dopamine" fails on physiology that predates the claim. Brain-vessel endothelium keeps circulating monoamines out "primarily at the luminal membrane", and an enzymatic barrier in endothelial cells and pericytes degrades the fraction that gets through; this is why Parkinson's disease is treated with the precursor L-dopa, which crosses, and not with dopamine [^S2]. The review's one exception is that very high circulating amine levels can open the barrier, "probably only indirectly by inducing an acute rise in systemic blood pressure" [^S2]. Arterial, portal and hepatic venous sampling in eight subjects at the NIH showed that mesenteric organs produce close to half of the dopamine formed in the body, 12.0 nmol/min, mostly from non-neuronal cells [^S10], and plasma catechols "have related but distinct sources and therefore reflect different functions of catecholamine systems" [^S17]. Plasma dopamine reports on the gut, the kidneys and sympathetic nerves, not on reward circuits.

Animal brain data do not rescue the claim. In rat microdialysis after chronic cold (5 °C for 17 to 28 days), basal dopamine in prefrontal cortex, nucleus accumbens and striatum did not differ significantly from naive rats and trended lower (prefrontal 1.5 ± 0.2 vs 2.2 ± 0.3 pg/20 µl); what changed was a larger prefrontal response to a later tail-shock stressor [^S7]. There, cold is a stressor and prefrontal dopamine is part of the stress response. [11C]raclopride PET detected striatal dopamine release during a video game, so the tool for a human test exists, though that abstract gives no percentage [^S11] [full text, not verified here]; the cluster found no human PET, microdialysis or cerebrospinal-fluid study of brain dopamine during or after cold water. The cocaine comparison sets a human plasma value against rat brain dialysis, in which drugs of abuse raised extracellular dopamine in nucleus accumbens and caudate, with no percentages in the abstract [^S4] [full text, not verified here]. Different compartments, species and units cannot be ranked. The same paper found that the antidepressant imipramine "failed to modify synaptic dopamine", so a mood effect does not require a dopamine rise [^S4].

### 2.3 Noradrenaline, adrenaline, cortisol and beta-endorphin

Noradrenaline is the consistent signal, and its time course argues against "for hours". Six healthy men in 10 °C water for 60 minutes, a US Public Health Service-funded study, went from 359 ± 32 pg/ml at baseline to 642 ± 138 pg/ml after 2 minutes and 1,171 ± 226 pg/ml at 45 minutes, with metabolic rate up "approximately threefold"; after rewarming in 40 °C water there was a transient peak and then "a rapid decrease to basal levels after 30 min", while core temperature was still low [^S1]. In healthy women exposed three times a week for 12 weeks to 20-second winter swims at 0 to 2 °C (n = 10) or 2-minute whole-body cryotherapy at −110 °C (n = 10), noradrenaline showed "significant 2-fold to 3-fold increases each time for 12 weeks" at 5 and 35 minutes, adrenaline and IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α were unchanged, and ACTH and cortisol in weeks 4 to 12 were lower than in week 1, "probably due to habituation" [^S20]. No retrieved study measured catecholamines hours after a short plunge.

The rise also shrinks with practice in some cohorts. Finnish winter swimmers tested with roughly 36-second immersions in 10 °C and then 4 °C river water had lower post-immersion catecholamines after three months (p < 0.01), "cold adaptation induced by winter swimming attenuates the catecholamine responses" (n not in abstract) [^S14]. Over one winter, resting noradrenaline fell "more so in the WS-group, but no statistically significant difference was observed between the groups", homovanillic acid (the main dopamine metabolite) and beta-endorphin stayed level in both groups, serotonin fell about 50 % by spring in swimmers and controls alike, and the authors found "No clear effect of winter swimming as such" [^S15].

The large adrenaline surges of Wim Hof studies belong to the breathing. In a randomised trial of 12 trained against 12 untrained men, ten days of meditation, hyperventilation with breath retention and ice immersion, taught by the method's creator, produced "intermittent respiratory alkalosis and hypoxia resulting in profoundly increased plasma epinephrine levels" that correlated with higher IL-10 after endotoxin [^S28]. Two randomised pilots in 40 and 48 men separated the parts: "Both breathing exercises were associated with an increase in plasma epinephrine levels", and "Cold exposure training alone did not relevantly modulate the LPS-induced inflammatory response" [^S34]. Cortisol is not reliably raised: it tended to fall at 14 °C [^S94], fell with habituation in Oulu [^S20], rose with a time-of-day effect in the Navy divers [^S5] and "increased or decreased" in Finnish swimmers [^S14]. Beta-endorphin rose (P < 0.05) by 60 minutes in 21 men immersed for 120 minutes at 18, 22 or 26 °C, but was "not progressive with duration" and not proportional to heat loss [^S6]. For scale, exercise raises catecholamines "1.5 to >20 times basal concentrations" [^S23]; the cold-water noradrenaline rises of about 2- to 6.3-fold sit inside that range.

### 2.4 Habituation: the response gets smaller with practice

The cold shock response (gasp, hyperventilation and tachycardia in the first one to three minutes) is the part most clearly reduced by repetition. A 2024 meta-analysis of 17 groups found that it habituates "after ∼4 immersions", with pooled falls in heart rate of −14 (10) beats/min (d = −1.19), respiratory frequency of −8 (7) breaths/min (d = −0.78) and minute ventilation of −21.3 (9.8) L/min (d = −1.64); the authors declare no conflict [^S38]. In 12 healthy men (8 habituated, 4 controls), six 3-minute immersions at 15 °C cut the first-30-second response to 10 °C water from 47 to 24 breaths/min, 72.2 to 31.3 L/min and 128 to 109 beats/min, still reduced at 7 months and, for heart rate, at 14 months [^S13]. In 18 people, six cold showers over four days at 10 °C reduced respiratory frequency on later 10 °C immersion by 21 %, while 15 °C showers did not [^S19].

Habituation is component-specific. In 21 men in 12 °C water, 5-minute skin-only immersions habituated ventilation but not the metabolic response, which only repeated deep-body cooling of about 45 minutes per session blunted [^S26]. Young sportsmen immersed at 14 °C for one hour three times a week for four to six weeks developed a "hypothermic type of adaptation" with less shivering, lower cold sensation and about 20 % less heat production per immersion, appearing after four immersions [^S9]. The noradrenaline response to brief exposure persisted for 12 weeks in one study [^S20] and was smaller after three months in another [^S14]. A regular user should not expect the first-session arousal to stay the same size.

### 2.5 Mood, depression and anxiety: reviews and controlled trials

The reviews lean favourable on pre-post data and weaken when restricted to controlled comparisons. A 2026 Charité multilevel meta-analysis, PROSPERO-registered, pooled 13 studies and 4,105 participants across randomised, non-randomised, cohort, case-control and single-group designs; estimates "generally favored" cold water for depressive symptoms, stress, wellbeing and mood, "but confidence intervals were wide and several estimates attenuated in controlled-only analyses", "Anxiety showed little evidence of consistent benefit", and "Most studies had high, serious, or critical risk of bias" [^S43]. It concludes that the evidence "does not establish" benefit and that cold water "should not currently be regarded as a standalone treatment for psychiatric disorders" [^S43]. A 2025 review of randomised trials only, in healthy adults (≤ 15 °C for ≥ 30 seconds; 11 studies, 3,177 participants; no competing interests), found inflammation higher immediately (SMD 1.03, 95 % CI 0.37 to 1.68), stress lower at 12 hours only (SMD −1.00, −1.40 to −0.61), and better sleep quality and quality of life "but not mood" [^S40]. One shower trial supplies 3,018 of the 3,177 participants [^S40] [^S29]. The 2022 Norwegian review often cited as "Espeland 2022" (PubMed: Esperland D) is narrative, covers 104 studies "carried out in small groups, often of one gender", and states "Whether winter swimmers as a group are naturally healthier is unclear" [^S135].

For whole-body cryotherapy (cold air) as an add-on for mental-health problems, a meta-analysis of 10 studies, 6 controlled, 294 people treated, gave within-group g = 1.63 (CI 1.05 to 2.21, I² = 93 %) against between-group g = 0.76 (0.17 to 1.36): about half the apparent improvement is shared with controls [^S31]. Its largest controlled trial gave 15 daily cryochamber sessions of 2 to 3 minutes at −160 to −110 °C to 26 medicated outpatients against 34 controls; a ≥ 50 % fall in HDRS-17 occurred in 34.6 % vs 2.9 %, and in HARS in 46.2 % vs 0 % [^S21]. It is controlled, not randomised, small by its authors' account, with assessor blinding not stated [^S21].

Two randomised trials tested cold water directly. The Dutch trial randomised 3,018 adults aged 18 to 65 to a hot-to-cold shower ending in 30, 60 or 90 seconds of cold daily for 30 days, or control: sickness absence fell 29 % (incident rate ratio 0.71, P = 0.003) and illness days did not differ [^S29]. Its anxiety and quality-of-life results are not in the abstract [full text, not verified here]; 79 % completed the protocol, outcomes were self-reported and unblinded, one author was employed by the insurer Achmea, and PubMed lists a 2018 erratum not checked here [^S29]. The cleanest test of "cold plus breathing" randomised 84 midlife women with high stress and depressive symptoms to Wim Hof breathing plus cold showers (n = 41) or slow breathing plus warm showers (n = 43), 15 minutes daily for three weeks: both improved alike (depressive symptoms −24 %, anxiety −27 %, stress −20 %, held at three months), only post-stressor rumination fell more with Wim Hof, all six dropouts came from that arm, and "counter to the preregistered predictions" the arms were equivalent [^S39].

### 2.6 Single sessions, cohorts, surveys and a hypothesis

Much of what is cited as proof comes from designs that cannot isolate the cold. The "brain-scan proof" scanned healthy adults unhabituated to cold water with fMRI before and after one 5-minute head-out immersion at 20 °C (temperature from the protocol paper) [^S36] [^S37]. Positive affect rose, distress and nervousness fell, and the change tracked connectivity among default-mode, frontoparietal, salience and visual networks; there was no thermoneutral control, the n is not in the abstract [full text, not verified here], and dopamine was not measured [^S36]. Self-selected Finnish winter swimmers showed lower tension and fatigue and higher vigour than controls after four months (p < 0.05), although they had "more diseases (about 50 %) diagnosed by a physician" at the start [^S18]. A web survey of 722 outdoor swimmers found the odds of reporting a positive impact 44.32 times higher for mental health and "cannot provide causal relationships" [^S33].

Clinical depression rests on a case report and a pending trial. One 24-year-old woman whose depression had resisted fluoxetine and citalopram began weekly open cold-water swimming and was medication-free at one year [^S30]. The OUTSIDE feasibility trial of outdoor swimming for depression has a protocol and no indexed results [^S35]. A single-arm study of 13 young adults at 10.42 ± 0.59 °C three times a week for four weeks found no change in wellbeing, worry, GAD-7 anxiety or Stroop, faster trail making (TMT-B 39.68 → 26.18 s) and a lower sleep-disturbance score (7.85 → 5.75), with no control group to exclude practice effects [^S41]. The most-quoted "cold showers treat depression" paper is a Medical Hypotheses article proposing "adapted cold showers (20 °C, 2-3 min, preceded by a 5-min gradual adaptation)", supported by "practical testing by a statistically insignificant number of people"; it states that "wider and more rigorous studies would be needed" [^S22].

Supporters point to the consistent self-reported lift [^S36] [^S18] [^S33] [^S30], the noradrenaline rise [^S20], favourable pooled estimates [^S43] and large within-group effects [^S31], and note that blinding is impossible. Critics point to attenuation in controlled-only pooling [^S43] [^S31], no mood effect in RCT-only pooling [^S40], equivalence with a warm-shower control [^S39], seasonal changes that appear in controls [^S15], and the confounds of exercise, nature and company in outdoor swimming. Both sides call for larger controlled trials.

### 2.7 Cognitive performance in the cold

The "cold makes you sharp" claim joins a real feeling of alertness [^S36] to measured performance that is more often worse. A US-government-funded meta-analysis of 22 studies and 515 effect sizes found a 13.91 % decrement in cognitive-related performance in cold of 10 °C or less against neutral conditions, modified by duration and task type [^S16]. A systematic review of 18 studies (8 cold air, 10 cold water) found impairment in 15 of 18 settings, "even before accidental hypothermia was established", mostly in attention, processing speed, executive function and memory [^S32]. In ten young men in minimal clothing at 10 °C air, an NIH-funded study, working memory, choice reaction time and executive function declined during cold and stayed impaired 60 minutes into rewarming, after physiology had returned to baseline [^S24].

A randomised crossover in 24 healthy adults aged 18 to 60, dressed for 15 minutes at −10 °C, 5 °C and 20 °C, found slower attention and more lapses on the psychomotor vigilance test, and less risk-taking, at −10 °C, with no difference in core temperature [^S42]. Forty people split into fast and slow coolers, immersed intermittently at 14 °C until rectal temperature reached 35.5 °C or 170 minutes, had similar motor, cognitive and stress-marker responses [^S27]. The only repeated short-plunge data are the uncontrolled four-week 10 °C study, with no Stroop decrement [^S41]. Long or deep cooling impairs attention and executive function for up to an hour after rewarming; short plunges have little controlled cognitive data.

### 2.8 What this means in practice

For a healthy adult, the dependable acute effect of cold water is a 2- to 6-fold rise in plasma noradrenaline plus a cold shock in the first minutes, within the range of ordinary exercise and back to baseline about 30 minutes after rewarming [^S1] [^S20] [^S23]. The "+250 % dopamine for hours" claim rests on one plasma measurement during an hour at 14 °C that its own laboratory did not reproduce, and plasma dopamine does not report on the brain [^S94] [^S8] [^S2]. A mood lift after a plunge is commonly reported, but controlled evidence for lasting effects on mood, depression or anxiety is weak, and cold water has no established role as a psychiatric treatment [^S43] [^S39]. Keep skilled or safety-critical work away from the hour after a long or very cold exposure, expect the first-session rush to shrink within about four sessions, never immerse alone in open water, and avoid breath-holding in cold water, where cold shock plus the dive reflex can trigger arrhythmias [^S24] [^S38] [^S25]. People with heart disease, uncontrolled high blood pressure, Raynaud's phenomenon, cold urticaria or pregnancy were not studied here and should take medical advice first.


## 3. Muscle Hypertrophy, Strength Blunting and Exercise Recovery

### 3.1 What people claim and what the trials tested

Four claims circulate: "ice baths speed recovery" after any session; "cold reduces inflammation, so you recover and grow faster"; the opposite camp's "cold kills your gains"; and the compromise that waiting 4-6 hours, or plunging before training, removes the problem. The trials behind all four are about post-exercise cooling: whole-body or lower-body cold water immersion (CWI) at 8-15 °C for 10-20 minutes, started within minutes of the session, sometimes with one limb in the water [^S59] [^S73] [^S75]. Participants are mostly young, healthy, active or trained men; pooled analyses report 92 % [^S85] and 85 % [^S93] male samples, and there is almost no direct evidence in women, older adults or patients. A morning plunge on a rest day or a cold shower far from training is not what these studies tested. Reviews now frame the field as a "recovery-adaptation paradox" [^S92] and separate adaptation (does regular post-exercise cold change what weeks of training give you), acute recovery (do you feel or perform better 1-96 hours after one session) and context (when fast recovery matters more than adaptation) [^S67] [^S79] [^S77].

### 3.2 Hypertrophy and strength: the training trials

The landmark trial put 21 physically active men through 12 weeks of strength training twice a week, each session followed by 10 minutes of CWI or 10 minutes of low-intensity cycling: strength and muscle mass rose more with active recovery (P < 0.05), where isokinetic work rose 19 %, type II fibre cross-sectional area 17 % and myonuclei per fibre 26 %, while none of these rose significantly with CWI [^S59]. The water temperature is not in the abstract (10 °C [full text, not verified here]), and the comparator is active recovery, not passive rest, so part of the gap could come from the cycling. Biopsies from the same cohort showed no group difference in fibre-type shift, but capillaries per fibre increased with CWI (P = 0.004) and not with active recovery (P = 0.955), a change in angiogenesis that is not negative [^S69].

A seven-week trial in 16 men training three days a week compared 15 minutes at 10 °C (n = 8) with 15 minutes of passive recovery at 23 °C (n = 8): 1-RM leg press improved similarly (130 ± 69 kg, pooled ES 1.53 ± 90 % CI 0.49), while the type II fibre CSA gain was attenuated with CWI (−1,959 ± 1,675 µm², ES −1.37 ± 0.99) [^S73]. The fibre grew less; the strength did not differ.

Limb-cooling trials point the same way. In the oldest, with legs trained by cycling or forearm flexors by handgrip three to four times a week for 4-6 weeks, "significant training effects were three times more frequent in the control than in the cold group"; the abstract gives no sample size, temperature or effect sizes [^S44]. In 14 men doing six weeks of wrist-flexion training, seven immersed the trained forearm at 10 ± 1 °C for 20 minutes after each session: muscle thickness rose less with cooling, and strength rose only in the uncooled group [^S60]. In 17 trained male students over five weeks, one randomly chosen leg was cooled after each session (3 × 4 minutes; temperature not in the abstract); both legs improved 1RM (p < 0.001), the uncooled leg tended to do better (interaction p = 0.11) and gained 1.6-2.0 % more 1RM [^S53]. A randomised crossover in 11 participants (two eight-week periods, 10 minutes of whole-body CWI against passive sitting) found non-significant strength and jump effects (1-RM g = 0.42, 95 % CI −0.42 to 1.26) but a significant condition × time effect on muscle thickness (P = .01) with a "large negative effect of cooling" (g = 1.20); the printed CI ("−0.65 to 1.20") looks like a typo in the record [^S80].

Three trials found no blunting. In 18 male academy Super Rugby players during a 12-week in-season programme, a randomised crossover of three four-week blocks (no immersion, whole-body CWI, hot water) found no difference in DXA lean mass (p = 0.960) or fat mass (p = 0.801) [^S86]; four-week blocks are short, carry-over is possible and in-season athletes may gain little muscle under any condition. In 13 trained men, 10 minutes at 10 °C after each of 16 lower-body sessions gave no benefit over a sham and no detrimental effect on morphology [^S81], but 13 people give little power. Whole-body cryotherapy differs: in 16 men doing six weeks of concurrent training, two three-minute sessions a week (30 s at −60 °C, then 150 s at −120 °C; n = 7) left torque and 3RM squat gains equal to control (n = 9), but only control increased jump height, and the authors noted "there may be an interference effect in the development of explosive power"; WBC timing relative to training is not stated, and funding was internal (Moulton College) [^S82].

### 3.3 The mechanism: signalling, protein synthesis and muscle blood flow

In the landmark paper's acute arm, nine men did single-leg strength exercise followed by CWI or active recovery: Pax7+ satellite cells rose 20-50 % at 24-48 hours only with active recovery, and p70S6K phosphorylation rose more after active recovery (P < 0.05) [^S59]. In nine men doing two high-load bouts, the p38-MNK1-eIF4E axis, c-Myc and pre-rRNAs rose only with active recovery, and CWI "markedly blunted" ribosome biogenesis; funding came from the ACSM Foundation, ESSA, QUT and UQ [^S62]. In the seven-week trial, rpS6 phosphorylation was blunted at +1 hour (−0.4-fold) and +48 hours (−0.2-fold) and FOXO1, a breakdown marker, was 1.3-fold higher [^S73].

The Maastricht group measured synthesis directly. In 12 healthy males, after one resistance session one leg sat in 8 °C water and the other in 30 °C water for 20 minutes, followed by 20 g of labelled milk protein with 45 g of carbohydrate: five-hour myofibrillar protein synthesis was 0.058 ± 0.011 against 0.072 ± 0.017 %/h (P = 0.024), dietary protein incorporation 0.016 ± 0.006 against 0.021 ± 0.007 MPE (P = 0.016), and daily synthesis over two weeks of training with the same cooling 1.48 ± 0.17 against 1.67 ± 0.36 %/day (P = 0.042); funding was internal to Maastricht UMC+ [^S75]. That is about 19-21 % lower five-hour synthesis and 11 % lower daily synthesis; breakdown was not measured, so this is not net growth. The follow-up in 12 young males with the same design found microvascular blood volume 1.24 ± 0.82 against 3.13 ± 1.64 video intensity right after immersion (P < 0.001), about 60 % lower, still lower 180 minutes after the drink, with amino-acid incorporation 0.011 ± 0.004 against 0.016 ± 0.005 MPE and a between-leg correlation of r = 0.65 between the two deficits [^S89]. One author reports unrelated FrieslandCampina funding. Both tracer studies set a cold limb against a warm one, the sharpest local contrast in the literature.

### 3.4 The pooled evidence and how the two camps argue

A meta-analysis of eight articles with CWI at ≤ 15 °C during regular training found a harmful effect on strength after resistance training (SMD −0.60; 95 % CI −0.87 to −0.33) and on ballistic performance (SMD −0.61; −1.11 to −0.11) [^S78]. A strength meta-analysis of 10 studies and 170 participants found an overall attenuation (ES −0.23; 95 % CI −0.45 to −0.01), a similar effect for trained-limb cooling (ES −0.31) and no significant difference for whole-body CWI (ES −0.08; −0.53 to 0.38; p = 0.743) [^S85]. The Bayesian hypertrophy meta-analysis by Piñero and colleagues (Eur J Sport Sci 2024, not a PubMed record) pooled eight CWI studies: hypertrophy SMD 0.36 with training alone against 0.14 with CWI, comparative cSMD −0.22 (95 % CrI −0.47 to 0.04), probability below zero 0.957, study quality "relatively fair to poor".

A 2026 network meta-analysis of 87 RCTs and 2,313 athletes ranked passive recovery highest for resistance-training strength (moderate certainty), with medium-duration, moderate-temperature CWI at g = 0.00 (95 % CrI −0.38 to 0.38); 69-85 % of comparisons were indirect, and acute and training outcomes are not separated [^S93]. An umbrella review of 15 meta-analyses (161 studies) rated quality "low to critically low", concluded that "CWI did not improve muscular strength performance, and impaired strength and hypertrophic gains", and recommended a "periodised, context-driven approach" [^S90].

The blunting camp cites convergent mechanism [^S59] [^S62] [^S75] [^S89], a consistent direction across training trials even when single results miss significance [^S44] [^S60] [^S53] [^S80] and negative pooled effects [^S78] [^S90]. The other camp replies that the strength effect is small [^S85] [^S53], that strength did not differ despite less fibre growth [^S73], that whole-body CWI did not change lean mass in rugby players [^S86], that many blunting trials cooled one limb [^S75] [^S89] and that the longest trial used active recovery as the comparator [^S59]. The blunting camp answers that DXA is less sensitive than fibre CSA and that four-week blocks and 13 participants are too little [^S86] [^S81]. Samples of 8-21, almost all male and mostly 4-8 weeks long, limit both readings; a year of CWI after three weekly sessions is about 156 exposures, and the longest trial covered 24.

### 3.5 Endurance and interval training adaptation

Early work suggested cold might help aerobic adaptation. In nine active males, one leg immersed to the gluteal fold at 10 °C for 15 minutes after running reached 28.9 against 37.0 °C muscle temperature and had higher PGC-1α mRNA at 3 hours (P = 0.014) [^S54]. Over four weeks in eight males with the same one-leg protocol, the cooled leg had higher AMPK, β-HAD and complex I and III subunits (P < 0.05), but PGC-1α protein missed significance (d = 0.99, P = 0.079), and the 5.9 % VO2peak gain is a whole-body number that cannot be split by leg [^S58].

Whole-person trials did not carry the signal through. In 19 men, 15 minutes at 10 °C after sprint intervals left AMPK, p38 MAPK and PGC-1α signalling unchanged against a 23 °C control, and in 16 who trained for six weeks it left VO2max, mitochondrial respiration and 2-km time trial unchanged [^S64]. In 19 recreationally active men over six weeks of sprint interval cycling, 15 minutes at 10 °C "neither impairs nor improves" Na+,K+-ATPase adaptations [^S68]. In 17 healthy subjects doing 12 HIIT sessions, lower-limb CWI at 10 °C for 15 minutes (n = 8) raised Tfam and PDK4 mRNA but not PGC-1α mRNA, and the 15-km time-trial gain did not differ (p = 0.33) [^S61]. A 2026 RCT in 16 males doing five weeks of HIIT with 15 minutes at 11.2 ± 0.2 °C (n = 9) against control (n = 7) found no effect of condition on VO2max, satellite cells or PGC-1α (p ≥ 0.098); it was funded by FAPESP, CNPq and CAPES [^S91]. The pooled endurance outcome was null (SMD −0.07; 95 % CI −0.54 to 0.53) [^S78]. In current trials, CWI after endurance sessions neither clearly harms nor clearly improves aerobic adaptation.

### 3.6 Acute recovery: soreness, performance, inflammation and placebo

Soreness is the most consistent benefit. The Cochrane review of 17 small, low-quality trials (366 participants) found CWI against passive rest reduced soreness by SMD −0.55 (95 % CI −0.84 to −0.27) at 24 hours and −0.93 (−1.36 to −0.51) at 72 hours, with heterogeneous results, no difference against contrast or warm water and little monitoring of adverse events [^S48]. A nine-RCT meta-analysis found CWI "can be slightly better than passive recovery" and stated a dose-response with 11-15 °C and 11-15 minutes [^S63]; the same group's 44-RCT update found that water temperature and protocol "do not influence this result", with long immersions losing the benefit [^S84], and its RCT in 60 men (15 minutes at 9 °C, 15 minutes at 14 °C, or control) found "no evidence … to suggest dose-response relationship" [^S65]. The 2016 dose-response claim did not survive its own group's later work. Across 36 articles of all cooling methods, DOMS fell by g −0.75 at 24 hours, but there was "no evidence, that cooling affects any objective recovery variable in a significant way during a 96 hrs recovery period", with high risk of performance and detection bias [^S57].

Objective outcomes move less. Across 52 studies of one strenuous bout, CWI improved 24-hour power after eccentric exercise (SMD 0.34; 95 % CI 0.06-0.62), lowered CK (SMD −0.85) and soreness (SMD −0.89) after high-intensity exercise, but had no significant effect on strength recovery; funding was a government PhD scholarship, and one author holds a football-club grant to evaluate CWI [^S83]. In 21 RCTs of trained athletes, cooling improved performance recovery by an average 2.4 % (g = 0.28), about 43 seconds over a 30-minute time trial, and whole-body immersion (5.1 %) beat limb-only immersion (1.1 %) [^S50]. In 10 active men, 10 minutes at 10 °C after resistance exercise did not improve maximal function at 2 and 4 hours but raised later submaximal squat load at 6 hours by 38 % (ES 1.3) [^S55]; the authors' idea that this "could enhance long-term training adaptations" was not borne out by their own 12-week trial [^S59]. A 99-study review, often called a network meta-analysis, used pairwise meta-analyses and found that "massage was found to be the most powerful technique for recovering from DOMS and fatigue", with massage and cold the most powerful for inflammation markers (CRP SMD −0.38 overall) [^S70].

The "cold reduces inflammation in muscle" mechanism has weak support. In nine active men, 10 minutes waist-deep at 10 °C after resistance exercise left intramuscular neutrophils, macrophages and cytokine mRNA no different from 10 minutes of easy cycling [^S66], though nothing was compared with rest. In the rugby crossover, CWI reduced CK trivially (g = 0.05) and hot water more (g = 0.62), suggesting part of the effect is water pressure [^S88]. The umbrella review found no significant effect on CRP or IL-6 up to 72 hours [^S90].

Blinding is the central weakness. In 30 males after sprints, 15 minutes at 10.3 °C was matched by 34.7 °C water presented as a recovery treatment; the placebo was "as effective as CWI" [^S52]. In 31 endurance-trained males after a marathon, "cryotherapy is no more effective than a placebo intervention" for functional recovery [^S72]. In 24 resistance-trained males, CWI (10 minutes at 10 °C) and WBC (−85 °C) were not more effective than placebo overall [^S74].

### 3.7 Chambers versus water, heat, tournaments and timing

The Cochrane review of whole-body cryotherapy (below −100 °C, 2-4 minutes) found four RCTs in 64 mostly male participants: soreness fell at 1 hour (SMD −0.77; 95 % CI −1.42 to −0.12) but not significantly at 24-72 hours, all evidence was "very low" quality, and no trial compared WBC with water [^S56]. Head-to-head data disagree: pooled comparisons found air cryotherapy better than CWI for strength recovery and CWI best for soreness [^S87]; WBC beat CWI for some outcomes after resistance exercise [^S74] and harmed muscle function against CWI after a marathon [^S72]; chambers averaged 3.8 % and CWI 2.9 % performance recovery [^S50]. "Chambers beat water" has no consistent support.

The clearest performance benefit is a second bout in the heat. In 10 trained cyclists, CWI at 10-20 °C for 15 minutes between two 30-minute tasks prevented the 4.1 % fall in work seen after active recovery [^S45]. Between 35-minute heat bouts, performance fell 1.81 % after active recovery and held after CWI, with rectal temperature 36.8 against 38.3 °C [^S47]. In 157 pooled subjects, each 1 °C colder lowers the core-temperature change by 0.03 °C, each extra minute by 0.02 °C, and the peak difference comes 60 minutes after immersion [^S71]; a review puts 10-15 °C for 5-15 minutes as most effective and notes that the gap to the next bout matters [^S51]. In tournaments, 20 junior soccer players with CWI at 10 °C after four matches in four days had lower soreness (P = 0.004) and fatigue but no performance difference [^S46], and an under-18 hockey squad (n = 18) with daily five minutes at about 6 °C found no differences in match running, DOMS or CK [^S76].

No study tested whether delaying CWI 4-6 hours after lifting, or plunging before training, prevents blunting; every training trial cooled within minutes. Indirect data show the first hours are affected: perfusion stayed low for 180 minutes [^S89], the synthesis deficit spanned five hours [^S75], muscle temperature stayed below baseline for 35 minutes [^S55], jump performance was impaired "in the initial hours" [^S90] and satellite-cell differences appeared at 24-48 hours [^S59]. A long gap is a plausible, untested precaution. Pre-cooling helped acute performance more in heat (+6.6 %) than in moderate conditions (+1.4 %), with water application giving small effects (+1.2 %) [^S49], but no trial measured adaptation. The "11 minutes a week" figure comes from a study of winter swimmers [^S134] and was never tested for interaction with training. Where funding is recorded, it is public, university or professional-society money; no record names a plunge, chamber or recovery-product maker, though many state no funder.

### 3.8 What this means in practice

If your goal is muscle growth, avoid a plunge in the minutes after lifting: pooled strength effects run from ES −0.23 to SMD −0.60, and cooled legs synthesise about 11-21 % less protein with about 60 % less microvascular blood volume [^S85] [^S78] [^S75] [^S89]. The cost is small and uncertain for whole-body immersion (ES −0.08), and the evidence is low certainty, short and almost all male [^S85] [^S90]. After endurance sessions, trials show neither harm nor benefit to adaptation [^S78] [^S64] [^S91]. Cold reliably reduces felt soreness but does not clearly speed strength recovery, and part of the effect is expectation [^S48] [^S83] [^S52]. It earns its place when you must perform again within about an hour, especially in heat, at 10-15 °C for 10-15 minutes [^S45] [^S47] [^S84]. If you plunge for other reasons, a rest day or a gap of several hours from lifting is reasonable, but no trial has tested it [^S90].


## 4. Brown Adipose Tissue, Thermogenesis and Body Weight

### 4.1 The claim and the 2009 rediscovery

The popular version runs: cold activates brown fat, brown fat burns serious calories, so cold plunges help you lose fat, and more brown fat means less diabetes and heart disease. The tissue is real. Brown adipose tissue (BAT) carries uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1), which releases the energy of fuel oxidation as heat, and three NEJM papers in 2009 established that adults have it. A retrospective review of 3,640 unstimulated clinical FDG-PET/CT scans in 1,972 patients, with UCP1-positive adipocytes confirmed in neck biopsies, found BAT in 76 of 1,013 women (7.5 %) and 30 of 959 men (3.1 %), with detection inversely related to age (P<0.001), outdoor temperature (P=0.02), beta-blocker use (P<0.001) and, in older patients, BMI (P=0.007) [^S97]. A prospective Maastricht study of 24 healthy men saw BAT activity in 23 of 24 (96 %) at 16 °C and none at 22 °C, lower in the 14 overweight or obese men (P=0.007) [^S100]. In five Turku subjects, cold raised supraclavicular glucose uptake by a factor of 15, and biopsies showed UCP1 mRNA and protein [^S99]. In Sapporo, 2 h at 19 °C produced substantial FDG uptake in 17 of 32 subjects aged 23 to 35 and 2 of 24 aged 38 to 65, more in winter than summer (P<0.001) and inversely related to BMI and visceral fat (P<0.001) [^S98].

Apparent prevalence depends on the stimulus. Unstimulated scans showed BAT in 250 of 2,934 Sydney patients (8.5 %), but repeat scans of 145 BAT-positive patients put true prevalence at an estimated 64 %; carriers were younger (36 ± 1 vs 52 ± 1 years) and leaner (BMI 20.1 vs 24.9 kg/m²) [^S101]. In Québec, 328 of 4,842 patients (6.8 %) had detectable BAT, with prevalence falling with outdoor temperature, age, BMI and diabetes [^S103]. Among 162 volunteers aged 20 to 73 given 2 h at 19 °C, BAT appeared in 41 %, in more than 50 % of people in their twenties and under 10 % in their fifties and sixties, a cross-sectional trend that cannot separate cause from effect [^S104]. Measured volume is method-dependent: after 5 h of cold, 12 lean men averaged 334 mL of activated BAT against 130 mL in 8 obese men, but the figure shifted with region-of-interest method, PET threshold and resolution [^S126]. In a randomised crossover of season order in 45 men, cold-induced thermogenesis (CIT) in the High-BAT group was 185.6 kcal/d in winter and 18.3 kcal/d in summer, daily-rate equivalents of a 2-hour reading [^S120].

The female excess belongs to unstimulated oncology scans. Under controlled cold, an NIH study found a cooler lower critical temperature in women (21.9 ± 1.3 vs 22.9 ± 1.2 °C, P=0.047), driven mainly by insulation from body fat, and no sex difference in BAT glucose uptake, muscle activity or CIT; the abstract gives no sample size [^S137]. The 2009 papers show that functional BAT exists in most young adults and that cold recruits it. They do not show how much heat it makes, because FDG measures glucose transport.

### 4.2 How much heat brown fat actually makes

Direct measures of BAT oxygen use give small numbers. In six men, multi-tracer PET showed that cold activated oxidative metabolism in BAT but not in adjacent muscle or fat, inversely to shivering, with BAT burning its own triglyceride; the abstract gives no kcal figure [^S106]. Triple-oxygen [15O] PET with calorimetry in 25 adults at 15.5 °C found 59.1 ± 17.5 g of activated BAT in the High-BAT group (8 women, 1 man) and 2.2 ± 3.6 g in the 16 Low-BAT subjects; the energy attributable to BAT was 3.2 ± 2.4 kcal/d at rest and 6.3 ± 3.5 kcal/d (range 4.0 to 9.9) in mild cold [^S108]. The same abstract concludes that BAT "accounts for 15-25 kcal/d in subjects with relatively large BAT depots" and "is a minor source of thermogenesis in humans"; it does not reconcile 15 to 25 with the measured 6.3, so the larger figure reads as an upper scenario (NIH funded) [^S108]. In seven Turku subjects, BAT accounted for "almost 1 % of total whole-body elevation in EE", while whole-body expenditure rose 2 to 47 % and deep cervico-thoracic muscles contributed heavily without perceived shivering [^S119]. A carbohydrate-rich meal raised BAT oxygen use "to the same extent as in cold stress", so BAT is not cold-specific [^S129].

Two Sherbrooke reviews frame the dispute. The first places BAT's contribution "at the lower end of what would be potentially clinically relevant if chronically sustained" and says BAT glucose uptake "can therefore be disconnected from thermogenesis", but also that true volume "may be much larger than the typically observed 50-150 ml", so estimates "may underestimate the true contribution" [^S127]. The second adds that BAT "has yet to be fully and selectively activated to increase the body's thermogenic response and shift energy balance" [^S136]. The FDG problem runs both ways. UCP1-knockout mice lost BAT thermogenesis "but BAT 18F-FDG uptake was fully retained" (animal evidence) [^S124], while men with type 2 diabetes had low FDG uptake yet normal BAT oxidative metabolism, so FDG can also underestimate function [^S115]. The NIDDK BARCIST 1.0 panel wrote reporting criteria because "diverse experimental practices" made laboratories hard to compare, and stated that "there are no fully validated best practices at this time" [^S117]. A headline such as "cold doubles your brown fat" usually means doubled FDG signal; oxygen-based heat estimates are far smaller.

### 4.3 Muscle, shivering and what acclimation changes

Most of the extra heat in cold comes from muscle. In 12 lean men under mild cold designed to minimise shivering, whole-body oxygen consumption depended on the sum of BAT and muscle responses, and glucose uptake was more than an order of magnitude greater in muscle than in BAT (674 ± 124 vs 12 ± 8 µmol/min, P<0.001) [^S114]. When nicotinic acid blocked BAT lipolysis in cold-exposed men, shivering rose by a commensurate amount and total heat production did not change (PubMed-tagged RCT, n not in the abstract) [^S121]. In a respiration chamber, mild cold raised 24-h expenditure in 11 lean men by 2.8 % (0.32 MJ/day), and individual rises correlated with muscle mitochondrial uncoupling (R² = 0.50) [^S96].

Whole-body CIT varies widely. A 13-day NIH room-calorimeter protocol measured capacity at the coldest temperature before overt shivering at 300 ± 218 kcal/d (17 ± 11 %) in 12 lean men and 125 ± 146 kcal/d (6 ± 7 %) in 9 men with obesity (P=0.01), with muscle recruited "in parallel with brown fat, not sequentially" [^S130]. In a single-blind randomised crossover with 24 completers, an overnight stay at 19 °C against 24 °C raised expenditure 5.3 ± 5.9 % and BAT activity 10.5 ± 11.1 % [^S107]. Two hours at 19 °C raised expenditure by 410 ± 293 kcal/day-rate in 6 BAT-positive men and 42 ± 114 in 7 BAT-negative men, with a standard deviation about 70 % of the mean [^S105]. A review of two centuries of data puts reported CIT at 0 to 280 % above basal metabolism, the spread coming from method as much as biology [^S123]. Water is a larger stimulus while it lasts: 1 h of head-out immersion at 14 °C raised metabolic rate 350 %, with plasma noradrenaline +530 % and plasma dopamine +250 %, and 20 °C raised metabolic rate 93 %; these were blood levels during the hour in young men whose number the abstract does not give [^S94].

Acclimation changes the source of heat rather than the total. Six men wearing a suit circulating 10 °C water for 2 h a day, 5 days a week for 4 weeks, increased BAT volume of activity 45 % (66 ± 30 to 95 ± 28 mL) and BAT oxidative metabolism 2.2-fold, with shivering unchanged [^S111]. In nine men on the same protocol, shivering fell 21 % "with no net difference in whole-body thermogenesis" [^S122]. Seven men immersed at 14 °C for 1 h on 7 consecutive days cut shivering 36 % (P=0.003) "without affecting whole body heat production" [^S131]. The result is comfort, not a larger burn per session.

### 4.4 Acclimation trials, body weight and compensatory eating

The trials that recruit BAT use mild cold air or water-perfused suits for hours. Daily 2 h at 17 °C for 6 weeks increased BAT activity and CIT and decreased body fat mass, with BAT change and fat change negatively correlated [^S109]. The abstract gives no sample size, no magnitude for the fat change and no described control arm for the cold intervention, although PubMed tags it as randomised, and one co-author is affiliated with Ajinomoto, which markets capsinoids, the paper's second intervention (PMC full text) [^S109]. A 10-day Maastricht protocol raised BAT activity and non-shivering thermogenesis with no muscle-uncoupling contribution and no "browning" of white fat; participants felt more comfortable, and body weight was not an abstract endpoint [^S110]. Ten days in 10 obese men raised BAT glucose uptake and muscle GLUT4, with no weight result [^S118]. In the four-month ICEMAN protocol, 5 men slept at 24, 19, 24 and 27 °C for a month each; BAT rose and fell reversibly, but "BAT acclimation did not alter CIT" [^S113].

Trials of repeated immersion are few. In a 12-week randomised crossover of 18 academy rugby players, 4-week blocks of post-exercise cold-water immersion made no difference to DXA lean mass (p=0.960) or fat mass (p=0.801) [^S86]. Sixteen daily 10-min immersions at 14 °C in 12 young adults left resting catecholamines and expenditure unchanged (single-arm) [^S142]. A narrative review of 104 studies wrote that immersion "seems to reduce and/or transform body adipose tissue", while stating that small, often single-sex groups prevented clear conclusions and that winter swimmers may be healthier to begin with [^S135]. A Pennington and NIH review states that "No studies have shown a decrease in body weight with cold-induced BAT activation" and that seasonal BAT variation "does not seem to be translated into weight changes" [^S128].

The arithmetic explains the null weight data. Even 20 kcal/d of BAT heat, sustained for 365 days, is 7,300 kcal a year, under 1 kg by the conventional 7,700 kcal per kg rule (a convention, not a cited source) [^S108]. Energy-imbalance modelling shows that weight responds "with half times of about 1 year" and that a persistent gap of "about 30 kJ per day" underlies average US adult gain, so small differences count only if sustained daily and uncompensated; PubMed also tags this record as a Comment [^S102]. Per session, winter CIT of 185.6 kcal/d-rate is about 15 kcal per 2 h at 19 °C [^S120], 300 kcal/d about 25 kcal per 2 h in lean men [^S130], and 0.32 MJ/day about 76 kcal for a full day of continuous mild cold [^S96]. Applying the 14 °C rise of 350 % to an assumed resting rate of 1,700 kcal/day (not from the paper) gives about 12 kcal for a 3-minute plunge and about 45 kcal for 11 minutes a week, a generous estimate that excludes rewarming [^S94].

Appetite can cancel that several times over. In 11 men, 45 min of cycling in 20 °C water cost about the same as in 33 °C water (505 ± 22 vs 517 ± 42 kcal), but subsequent intake was 877 ± 457 kcal, 44 % above neutral water (P<0.05), an excess of about 268 kcal [^S95]. In 15 active adults, intake after 30 min at 16 °C was 2,783 ± 909 kJ against 1,817 ± 862 kJ after 35 °C water, about 231 kcal more, with unchanged appetite ratings; Lazy Spa supplied the tub but was not involved in the design [^S140]. Cooling without a core-temperature drop behaved differently: 90 min in a 10 °C suit raised lunch intake 10 % in 47 adults with obesity, with lower intake later (one author supported by Bausch Health Canada) [^S141], and 24 h at 16 °C did not change total intake in 23 men at the French military biomedical research institute (P=0.120) [^S138]. All four are single sessions.

### 4.5 Søberg 2021 and the "11 minutes a week" figure

The minimum-dose claim traces to one cross-sectional case-control study: young male winter swimmers (n = 8, with 7 in Table 1) and 8 controls matched for age, BMI and VO2max, tested with water-perfused cooling blankets rather than immersion [^S134]. Swimmers showed greater rises in CIT and supraclavicular skin temperature, while BAT glucose uptake and muscle activity rose similarly to controls; tissue fat was 12.0 % against 18.2 % (p=0.01) [^S134]. Table 1 lists 2.6 swims a week, 1.4 (SD 2.6) min per immersion, "Total time immersed in cold water/week (min): 11" and "Total time in sauna heat/week (min): 57" (PMC full text) [^S134]. The 11 minutes is the mean of what these men reported, beside five times as much sauna; no dose was assigned or compared, so it cannot be a minimum or effective dose, and the authors did not claim it was [^S134].

The authors write that "we cannot conclude whether winter swimming per se results in increased cold-induced thermogenesis", because "people with a certain thermophenotype could be more prone to take up winter swimming", and that "the low sample size provides a risk for type 1 and type 2 errors" [^S134]. The discussion's "500–1,000 kcal/24 h" is a 30-min reading of 3,044 against 2,038 kcal/24 h expressed as a daily rate, an excess of about 21 kcal in the 30 minutes for swimmers and about 12 kcal for controls [^S134]. Funding came from TrygFonden and the Novo Nordisk Foundation centre; one author consults for Novo Nordisk on BAT biology and another works for Embark Biotech, an obesity-therapeutics developer; no plunge or sauna maker is listed [^S134].

### 4.6 Insulin sensitivity and the cardiometabolic association

The quoted "+43 %" comes from a single-arm study: 10 days of acclimation at 14 to 15 °C raised insulin sensitivity by "∼43 % in eight type 2 diabetes subjects", with marked muscle GLUT4 translocation and "only a minor increase in BAT glucose uptake"; the abstract gives neither sex nor hours per day [^S116]. The same group's study in 9 patients at 16 to 17 °C with shivering prevented found no improvement in insulin sensitivity, glucose or lipid metabolism, and concluded that "some form of muscle contraction is needed" [^S133]. Fifteen adults with overweight shivering 1 h a day for 10 days improved glucose tolerance, triglycerides, NEFA and blood pressure, with no control arm described (NCT04516018) [^S139]. The benefit appears when muscles shiver and vanishes when shivering is blocked, closer to exercise than to BAT [^S116] [^S133] [^S139].

Acute studies point the same way. In 7 BAT-positive and 5 BAT-negative men, 5 to 8 h of cold raised glucose disposal and insulin sensitivity only in the BAT-positive group [^S112]. Cooling 15 men to 18.06 °C in a suit with shivering excluded raised insulin sensitivity ∼20 % without changing insulin secretion [^S125], and the ICEMAN cold month raised postprandial insulin sensitivity in 5 men [^S113]. The protocol closest to a home plunge pointed the other way: 16 daily 10-min immersions at 14 °C temporarily decreased glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity at 24 h, returning to baseline after one week [^S142].

The cardiometabolic claim rests on one large association. Propensity-matched analysis of 134,529 unstimulated scans from 52,487 cancer patients linked detectable BAT with lower odds of type 2 diabetes, dyslipidaemia, coronary disease, heart failure and hypertension, more strongly in people with overweight (NIH funded) [^S132]. From the PMC full text, BAT appeared in 9.7 % of patients, 13.8 % of women against 4.9 % of men; type 2 diabetes prevalence was 4.6 % against 9.5 %; and beta-blockers (OR 0.61, 95 % CI 0.53 to 0.71) were associated with lower odds of detected BAT [^S132]. The study cannot show that cold-recruited BAT produces health: insulin resistance lowers FDG uptake [^S115], beta-blockers suppress detection [^S97] [^S132], and the authors acknowledge limits of "the retrospective design and analysis of data from a cancer population" [^S132].

### 4.7 What this means in practice

For a healthy adult, cold recruits brown fat, but BAT's own heat is single-digit to about 25 kcal a day, around 1 % of the extra heat made in cold, with muscle supplying most of the rest [^S108] [^S119] [^S114]. A 3-minute plunge plausibly adds about 10 to 15 kcal, and after cold-water immersion people ate about 230 to 270 kcal more at the next meal [^S94] [^S140] [^S95]. No trial shows weight loss from cold-induced BAT activation, the one randomised trial of repeated immersion on body composition was null, and acclimation makes cold more comfortable without raising total heat output [^S128] [^S86] [^S131]. The insulin-sensitivity gains came from long daily mild cold with shivering in people with diabetes or overweight, while short plunges briefly worsened glucose tolerance in one trial [^S139] [^S133] [^S142]. "11 minutes a week" describes what a few Danish winter swimmers reported, beside 57 minutes of sauna, and was never tested as a dose [^S134]. If fat loss is the goal, the evidence does not support cold as the tool; if you enjoy cold for other reasons, brown fat and body weight are not a reason to keep it or to stop it.


## 5. Immune Function, Infection Resistance and Inflammation

### 5.1 Cold showers and sick days: the one large trial

The claim "cold showers make you sick less often", usually quoted as "29 % fewer sick days", comes from one Dutch pragmatic trial. It randomised 3,018 adults aged 18 to 65, without severe comorbidity and with no routine experience of cold showering, 1:1:1:1 to end their usual hot shower with 30, 60 or 90 seconds of cold water, or to a control group, for 30 consecutive days in January 2015, after which the cold groups showered cold at their own discretion for 60 more days [^S29]. The water temperature was not measured (the authors estimated 10 to 12 °C from ground temperature), compliance could not be verified, the trial was unblinded and every outcome was self-reported [^S29]. Sickness absence from work fell 29 % in the pooled cold groups (incidence rate ratio 0.71, 95 % CI 0.56 to 0.89, P = 0.003), but illness days, the days on which participants felt ill, showed "no significant group effect" (IRR 0.89, 95 % CI 0.77 to 1.01, P = 0.073) [^S29]. The 30, 60 and 90 second arms did not differ (P = 0.992), and regular physical activity was independently associated with 35 % less sickness absence (IRR 0.65, P = 0.003), a non-randomised association [^S29]. Every arm had a median of zero days of absence over 90 days, 29.4 to 34.0 % of the cold arms and 34.8 % of controls took any sick leave, and median illness days were 2 in every arm; applying the IRR to the model's intercept of 2.23 days gives about 0.65 days less absence per person over the season, a model estimate rather than an observed mean [^S29]. Funding was "no specific funding", one author was employed by the insurer Achmea without support in time or salary, the introduction cites the Wim Hof Method as rationale, and a 2018 erratum exists whose content was not checked [^S29].

For a real effect: the trial is large, randomised and registered (NTR5183), and work absence is more objective than a symptom diary. Against over-reading it: volunteers in a "cold challenge" who knew their group may have been more motivated to go to work, the outcome closer to "did you get sick" was not significant, and there was no dose-response and no laboratory confirmation [^S29]. A 2025 meta-analysis of cold-water immersion in healthy adults (11 studies, 3,177 participants, water at or below 15 °C for at least 30 s) repeats the 29 % figure by narrative synthesis, and 3,018 of its participants are this one trial [^S40]. On blood immune measures it found "no significant effects on immune function immediately (SMD −0.16, 95 % CI −0.82 to 0.51) or 1 hour (SMD −0.18, 95 % CI −1.09 to 0.74) post-CWI", an estimate resting on roughly 159 people [^S40]. No study found for this dossier measured laboratory-confirmed infections as the outcome of a cold habit in healthy adults. A Portsmouth short report notes that habitual cold-water swimmers "anecdotally" claim fewer and milder infections, "though this was not directly measured"; its own data were not verified here [^S169].

### 5.2 The Wim Hof endotoxin experiments: was it the cold or the breathing?

"Cold boosts the immune system" is usually told through the Radboud University Medical Center programme, in which healthy volunteers receive intravenous E. coli lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 2 ng/kg), a laboratory model that produces a few hours of fever and flu-like symptoms. It began with one man who held "several world records with regard to tolerating extreme cold": the ex vivo cytokine response of his blood cells was "greatly attenuated" after an 80-minute ice immersion during which he practised his concentration technique, and during endotoxaemia his cortisol was higher than in any earlier participant and his cytokines and symptoms were "remarkably low" against a historical cohort of 112 [^S159]. That is n = 1, an unblinded believer and a historical comparison.

The randomised trial that followed allocated 24 healthy men to 10 days of training or none (12 each); training combined meditation, "cyclic hyperventilation followed by breath retention" and "immersions in ice cold water", and the trained men did the breathing during the LPS challenge [^S28]. The technique caused "intermittent respiratory alkalosis and hypoxia" and "profoundly increased plasma epinephrine levels"; IL-10 rose faster and higher and tracked adrenaline, TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-8 were lower, and flu-like symptoms were milder [^S28]. The abstract gives no fold changes and the full text was not retrievable here, the control group had no sham, and all components were given together. Inside the same project, generalised optimism correlated with the adrenaline response (rho = 0.76, P < .01) and IL-10 (rho = 0.60, P < .05), and specific expectations with symptom reports (rho = −0.71, P < .01), in a proof-of-principle analysis whose abstract gives no n [^S164]. A metabolomic re-analysis of the 24 men placed the main training effects "shortly after initiation of the acquired breathing exercises but prior to LPS administration", with lactate and pyruvate tracking IL-10 and, in vitro, raising IL-10 and lowering IL-1β and IL-6; four co-authors were employees of Metabolomic Discoveries GmbH [^S168].

The component question was answered in 2022 by two pilot studies in healthy young men [^S34]. In the first (n = 40), both breathing exercises raised adrenaline regardless of training length (F(4,152) = 0.53, P = .71) or of whether the method's creator taught it (F(4,152) = 0.92, P = .46) [^S34]. In the second (n = 48, 12 per group), men had cold training only, breathing only, both, or nothing, taught by one independent trainer, then received 2 ng/kg LPS; the cold programme was four days of standing barefoot in snow for up to 30 minutes, lying in snow in shorts for up to 20 minutes and ice-water immersion for up to 3 minutes, plus 60 seconds of cold at the end of each daily shower [^S34]. Cold training alone "did not relevantly modulate the LPS-induced inflammatory response" (F(8,37) = 0.60, P = .77), breathing produced "significantly enhanced anti-inflammatory and attenuated proinflammatory cytokine levels" (F(8,37) = 3.80, P = .002), and cold "significantly enhanced the immunomodulatory effects of the breathing exercise" (F(8,37) = 2.57, P = .02) [^S34]. In the full text the combined group against control showed IL-10 +44 % and TNF-α −32 %, IL-6 −35 % and IL-8 −30 % by area under the curve, cold alone lowered only MCP-1 (−25 %), and adrenaline in the breathing groups roughly doubled, from a median 0.51 to 1.01 nmol/L [^S34]. Symptoms ran the other way: peak scores were 9.3 ± 1.3 in controls, 9.4 ± 1.5 with breathing and 7.04 ± 1.2 with both, and only the cold-only group was significantly lower at 5.5 ± 0.8 (P = .017), a self-reported outcome in an unblinded trial; funding was internal to the Radboud intensive-care department with no conflicts reported [^S34].

The patient trials are small. An eight-week add-on of breathing, cold and meditation in 24 patients with axial spondyloarthritis (open-label randomised one-way crossover, 13 against 11) was safe and lowered ESR from a median 16 to 9 mm/hr (P = 0.040) and ASDAS-CRP from 3.1 to 2.3 (P = 0.044), while hs-CRP did not change [^S166]. In 60 people with multiple sclerosis randomised to 12 weeks of the Wim Hof Method, a lifestyle programme of structured activity and nutrition counselling, or control (43 analysed), both active programmes lowered IL-17A and IL-18 (P < 0.001), with "comparable short-term anti-inflammatory effects" [^S177]. The systematic review of the method (searches to 4 July 2022, nine papers from eight trials) concluded narratively that it "may reduce inflammation" through adrenaline and IL-10, did no meta-analysis and did not separate cold from breathing [^S174].

### 5.3 Blood immune markers after acute and repeated cold

Most of the older "cold activates immunity" evidence measures blood cells and cytokines, not infections. Athletic young men immersed head-out in 14 °C water for one hour showed a "minimal" effect after one bout; after three immersions a week for six weeks there was "a small, but significant, increase" in monocytes, CD25-positive lymphocytes, plasma TNF-α, haptoglobin and haemopexin, with no change in IL-1β, CRP, immunoglobulins, complement or total leukocytes, and the authors called the activation "slight" with significance that "remains to be elucidated" (no n in the abstract) [^S144]. The same protocol raised noradrenaline four-fold without a significant rise in adrenaline [^S8]. One hour at 14 °C is a far larger stimulus than a two to three minute plunge.

In a Canadian defence laboratory with US Army co-authors, seven men sat for 2 h in 5 °C air, which "induced a leukocytosis and granulocytosis, an increase in natural killer cell count and activity, and a rise in circulating levels of interleukin-6", attributed to noradrenaline [^S147]. Habitual Finnish winter swimmers had higher resting IL-6, leukocytes and monocytes than inexperienced subjects, and a "dramatically larger" cortisol change after a sauna plus a short ice-cold swim, in a cross-sectional design that cannot separate cold from heat [^S149]. Over 12 weeks of three weekly exposures, healthy women doing winter swimming (0 to 2 °C, 20 s; n = 10) or −110 °C cryotherapy (2 min; n = 10) raised noradrenaline 2- to 3-fold each time, and "Plasma IL-1-beta, IL-6 or TNF alpha did not show any changes after cold exposure" [^S20]. An exploratory RCT in 12 men, 12 minutes at 7 °C four times a week for three weeks, found "no relevant effects of 3-week repeated CWI on leukocyte counts in healthy men" [^S173].

The German winter-swimming work often cited for "hardening" concerns oxidative stress. A German-language report links a dose-dependent fall in uric acid after cold exposure to oxygen-radical formation and proposes antioxidant adaptation as the mechanism of "body hardening" [^S143], and a cross-sectional comparison found higher erythrocyte glutathione, superoxide dismutase and catalase in winter swimmers [^S148]. Two older reviews disagree on direction: one, mostly from small-mammal data, describes severe chilling as immunosuppressive with adaptation "over the course of 2-3 weeks" [^S146], and one from the US Army Research Institute of Environmental Medicine finds "no support for the concept that cold exposure depresses immune function" [^S150].

### 5.4 Inflammation after a plunge and after exercise

"Ice baths reduce inflammation" is the claim; the pooled healthy-adult data show a rise in the first hour. The 2025 meta-analysis found inflammatory markers increased immediately after immersion (SMD 1.03, 95 % CI 0.37 to 1.68) and at one hour (SMD 1.26, 95 % CI 0.59 to 1.94), "an acute inflammatory response" [^S40]. Inside the muscle, nine active men did unilateral resistance exercise and then sat waist-deep in 10 °C water or cycled lightly for 10 minutes, in a crossover with biopsies to 48 h; "changes in inflammatory cells, cytokines, neurotrophins and HSPs did not differ significantly between the recovery treatments" [^S66]. The comparator was active recovery, so this tests cold against light cycling, not against nothing. Reviews from cold-water laboratories find that for some claims "the supporting data remain at the level of anecdotal speculation" [^S165], that benefits may exist for experienced swimmers in a "regular, graded and adjusted mode" while unfamiliar people risk death from cold shock [^S167], and that "whether winter swimmers as a group are naturally healthier is unclear" [^S135].

### 5.5 Whole-body cryotherapy in healthy people and in inflammatory disease

Whole-body cryotherapy (WBC) uses air at −110 to −140 °C for two to three minutes, and its inflammation data are more favourable than those for water, though from small trials. Forty-five healthy men given 5, 10 or 20 daily three-minute sessions at −130 °C showed higher IL-6 and IL-10 and lower IL-1α, with no cold-free control; the abstract calls IL-6 "anti-inflammatory", an interpretation for a cytokine with both roles [^S157]. In a crossover in 11 endurance-trained men at INSEP, daily WBC for 96 h after a trail run lowered IL-1β and CRP and raised IL-1ra against passive recovery, while TNF-α, IL-10 and IL-6 did not differ [^S158]. A meta-analysis of 11 RCTs with 274 participants found lower IL-1β (the abstract reports "SMD value was −2.08pg/mL", mixing units) and higher IL-10 (SMD 0.78), with no heterogeneity or certainty grading in the abstract [^S175]. A consortium scoping review found 16 documented adverse events and judged the risk "within acceptable limits" when contraindications are respected; several authors are WBC researchers [^S172].

In rheumatic disease, cold improves symptoms more consistently than systemic inflammation. A systematic review pooling 6 studies and 257 rheumatoid arthritis patients found lower pain VAS and DAS28 after chronic cryotherapy and asked for RCTs "with stronger methodology" [^S160]. In a single-blinded RCT of 60 patients with active RA, WBC at −110 °C was "not superior to local cryotherapy commonly used in RA patients for pain relief", disease activity did not differ, and 6 of 40 (15 %) stopped WBC [^S154]. Six WBC sessions at −130 °C added to inpatient care (56 completed) gave a baseline-adjusted pain difference of −1.31 NRS points (95 % CI −2.1 to −0.53, P = 0.002), no difference in disease activity at 12 weeks, and unblinded patients [^S170]. The Cochrane review of thermotherapy in RA (7 studies, 328 subjects) found no effect on objective disease measures and predates most WBC trials [^S152]. In ankylosing spondylitis, a meta-analysis of 5 studies and 310 patients found better BASDAI, ASDAS, BASFI and pain but "no significant impact" on CRP (P = 0.684) [^S176], the same pattern as the spondyloarthritis pilot [^S166].

### 5.6 Does cold cause colds?

The opposite folk belief, "you catch a cold with wet hair", has a small evidence base. At the Cardiff Common Cold Centre, 180 healthy people were randomised to a foot chill or a control procedure: 13 of 90 chilled subjects reported a cold in the next four to five days against 5 of 90 controls (P = 0.047), with no acute symptom change (P = 0.62) and higher symptom scores over days 1 to 4 (5.16 against 2.89; P = 0.013) [^S153]. That is 14.4 % against 5.6 %, resting on 8 extra self-reported colds with no virological testing [^S153]. The group's hypothesis paper notes that nasal inoculation during cold "failed to demonstrate any effect of cold exposure on susceptibility to infection" and proposes nasal vasoconstriction as the route by which chilling unmasks a subclinical infection [^S151].

Cell studies point to airway temperature. Rhinovirus replicated more at 33 °C than at 37 °C because interferon defence was weaker in mouse airway cells [^S161], and apoptosis and RNase L restricted it better at 37 °C in human bronchial cells [^S162]. In human nasal tissue, about 5 °C of cooling cut antiviral extracellular-vesicle release by 41.9 % per the full text, and lowering room air from 23.3 °C to 4.4 °C dropped intranasal temperature by up to 6.4 °C (NIH-supported) [^S171]. The stimulus is cold inhaled air, not water on the skin. Among 892 Finnish conscripts, each 1 °C fall in outdoor temperature raised upper respiratory infection risk by 4.3 % (P < 0.0001) [^S156], and in 147 PCR-confirmed rhinovirus cases a fall over three days raised risk about 8 % per 1 °C (OR 1.07 to 1.08) [^S163]. A narrative review concludes that cold exposure increases respiratory infection risk, "although not all studies agree" [^S155].

Cold clearly harms immune defence in one setting. Among 200 colorectal surgery patients, routine care left core temperature at 34.7 ± 0.6 °C against 36.6 ± 0.5 °C with extra warming, and wound infection occurred in 18 of 96 (19 %) against 6 of 104 (6 %, P = 0.009), with a 2.6-day longer stay [^S145]. That is about 2 °C of core cooling for hours under anaesthesia, which no plunge study above measured, but it marks where cold becomes immunologically harmful.

### 5.7 What this means in practice

If you already take cold showers and like them, the one large trial suggests about 29 % less self-reported sick leave, roughly 0.65 days per person over a winter on its model, with 30 seconds doing as well as 90 [^S29]. The same trial found no significant change in days spent ill, and no study has shown fewer laboratory-confirmed infections from a cold habit [^S29] [^S40]. The dampened response to injected endotoxin came mainly from the breathing technique, with cold training alone leaving cytokines unchanged, in small trials of healthy young men from one group [^S34] [^S28]. A plunge produces a short rise in inflammatory markers, and after resistance training it did not lower muscle inflammation compared with light cycling [^S40] [^S66]. The symptom benefits of cryotherapy belong to patients with rheumatic disease under medical care [^S170] [^S176]. Rewarm after cold exposure rather than staying chilled, because brief chilling was followed by more self-reported colds and hours of lowered core temperature raise infection risk [^S153] [^S145].


## 6. Cardiovascular Physiology, Cold Shock and Safety Risks

### 6.1 The cold shock response in the first minutes

The popular framing treats cold water as a hypothermia problem, so a few minutes look harmless. The classic review of the initial responses to immersion gives no numbers in its abstract; it concludes that cold shock "can result in the death or serious incapacitation of an individual long before general hypothermia develops" and is "probably responsible for the majority of annual open-water immersion deaths", an expert estimate rather than a measured fraction [^S183]. The 1-3 minute time course usually attributed to it is a full-text figure not verified here. The same laboratory's later "kill or cure?" review concedes that for some claimed benefits of cold water "the supporting data remain at the level of anecdotal speculation" [^S165].

The primary trials give the size of the response in healthy young men seated head-out in stirred water for 3 minutes. In 13 men at 10 °C, the first 30 s produced a respiratory frequency of 47.3 breaths/min, an inspiratory minute volume of 67.6 L/min and a heart rate of 128 beats/min; at 15 °C the values were 33.3 breaths/min, 50.5 L/min and 114 beats/min [^S188]. A second cohort of 12 men gave 47 breaths/min, 72.2 L/min and 128 beats/min at 10 °C, "an inspiratory gasp response followed by uncontrollable hyperventilation and tachycardia" [^S13]. A 2026 review co-authored by Tipton lists the mechanisms of cold-shock death as "aspiration, hypocapnia-induced cerebral hypoperfusion, and cardiac arrhythmia before core temperature begins to fall" [^S218]. In 17 people at 12 °C, middle cerebral artery flow velocity fell 6 (9) % in the first minute of resting immersion, and leg-only kicking offset the fall (−3 (16) % against +5 (4) % in 35 °C control water) without worsening other responses, the basis of the proposed advice "Float first – and kick for your life" [^S199]. The RNLI campaign of the same name is not a PubMed record.

The response is not fixed. In two crossover studies (n = 11 and n = 10) at 15 °C for 7 minutes, participants told falsely that the water would be 5 °C colder reached a peak heart rate of 136.4 (15.0) against 124.0 (17.6) beats/min, and anxiety erased the habituation of five earlier immersions [^S195]. Alcohol changed less than often assumed: at 105 mg/100 ml in 16 subjects at 15 °C, respiratory frequency in the first 20 s fell 10 % ("a total of 2-3 breaths") with heart rate unchanged [^S186]. The 2026 review lists alcohol among risk modifiers without figures [^S218].

### 6.2 Habituation: how many dips, how much protection

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 17 groups places habituation "after ∼4 immersions, with variation between studies": heart rate −14 (10) beats/min (d = −1.19; n = 145), respiratory frequency −8 (7) breaths/min (d = −0.78; n = 73) and minute ventilation −21.3 (9.8) L/min (d = −1.64; n = 106) [^S38]. Most source studies came from the same UK laboratories with small samples of young healthy men [^S38]. In the primary trial, six 3-minute immersions at 15 °C over four days cut the 10 °C first-30-s responses from 47.3 to 24.0 breaths/min, from 67.6 to 29.5 L/min and from 128 to 109 beats/min while controls did not change, so habituation in warmer water carried over to colder water [^S188]. The reduction was still significant seven months later; at 14 months heart rate stayed lower but breathing had returned "towards pre-habituation levels" [^S13].

The claim that cold showers train the same response is half right. In 18 subjects given six showers over four days, a 3-minute 10 °C shower on the back cut the first-30-s respiratory frequency of a later 10 °C immersion by 21 %, from 54 (14) to 44 (16) breaths/min; a 3-minute 15 °C shower changed nothing, and no shower reduced the first-minute tachycardia [^S19]. Some habituation starts within one session: in 26 men doing five helicopter-escape submersions in 29.5 °C water, the heart-rate response was lower by the fourth run [^S194]. By this report's arithmetic, the 18 minutes of total exposure in the primary trial cut breathing frequency 49 %, ventilation 56 % and heart rate 15 %, and the pooled −14 beats/min is about 11 % of a 128 beats/min surge [^S188] [^S38]. Habituation removes most of the hyperventilation and leaves most of the tachycardia, and no abstract in this set shows that it lowers the arrhythmia rate.

### 6.3 Breath-holds, the diving response and arrhythmias

Breath-hold time collapses in cold water. In 160 people submerged at 0 to 35 °C, maximum breath-hold duration on sudden submersion followed BHD = 15.01 + 0.92 × water temperature (°C), 25-50 % of the pre-submersion value in 0-15 °C water, which gives about 15 s at 0 °C and 24 s at 10 °C [^S181]. After brief habituation and mild voluntary hyperventilation it more than doubled, BHD = 38.90 + 1.70 × water temperature, about 56 s at 10 °C, while the diving bradycardia did not depend on temperature [^S181].

The rhythm data come from whole-body breath-hold submersions, not from face-only immersion as the claim is often stated. Twelve subjects in wet or dry suits made one submersion at 5 °C and two at 10 °C, held their breath as long as possible and then breathed through a tube for 10 s still submerged: bradycardia below 60 beats/min occurred in 28 of 36 submersions, and ectopic arrhythmias in 11 subjects in 29 of 36, "predominantly in the 10-s period of submersion after the cessation of breath holding" [^S185]. In 26 men doing five 10-s helicopter-escape submersions in 29.5 °C water, 32 arrhythmias occurred in 22 participants (25 %), mostly just after submersion; the authors called them "probably of little clinical significance in young, fit individuals" while noting that older, less fit people and colder water remain untested [^S194]. A hypothesis review proposes that the simultaneous sympathetic cold shock and vagal diving response, "autonomic conflict", explain these arrhythmias and may cause some deaths ascribed to drowning or hypothermia; it states a mechanism and gives no count of deaths [^S25].

Vulnerability is partly genetic. Of 388 consecutive referrals for long-QT genetic testing, 43 (about 11 %) had a personal or family history of near-drowning or drowning; of the 33 with high clinical probability of LQTS, 28 (85 %) were LQT1, and 9 of the 10 with low probability carried putative CPVT1-causing RyR2 mutations [^S190]. That NIH-funded study states no water temperature, so it concerns swimming in general. Long cold swims also lengthen repolarisation in trained people: in 20 cold-water swimmers (47.3 ± 8.6 years) swimming laps at 15 °C for a mean 214 ± 115 min, QTc rose from 437.7 ± 27.7 to 457.2 ± 35.9 ms and 5 exceeded 500 ms, with systolic function unchanged and no correlation between ΔQTc and Δcore temperature [^S215].

### 6.4 How people die in cold water

The current model has four stages: cold shock, swimming failure, hypothermia and circum-rescue collapse. Its 2026 review states that "epidemiological data challenge the prevailing assumption that hypothermia is the primary mechanism of death", that in most cold-water drownings hypothermia "occurs after aspiration-induced hypoxic arrest rather than as the primary cause of death", and that "fewer than 10% of drowning victims present in a shockable rhythm" [^S218]. In a flume trial of 10 volunteers swimming breaststroke for up to 90 minutes, all 10 completed at 25 °C, 8 at 18 °C and 5 at 10 °C; at 10 °C one reached swim failure at 61 minutes and four were withdrawn with rectal temperature 35 °C, "close to swim failure" [^S189].

The public gets the clock wrong. Among 661 seminar attendees, 84 % thought ice water makes a person hypothermic within 15 minutes or less (the authors' answer: more than 30) [^S191]. An expansion of the 1946 US Navy survival dataset from 23 to 122 points recorded immersions of up to 75 h below 20 °C [^S205]. Accidental hypothermia begins below 35 °C core, and arrest risk rises below 30 °C in young people and below 32 °C in the elderly [^S211]. In 12 lean competitive swimmers in costumes and hats for up to 2 h, half failed to complete 2 h at 16 °C and three-quarters completed at 18 °C; rectal temperature fell 1.57 °C/h at 16 °C and 1.07 °C/h at 18 °C, and it did not correlate with thermal sensation (r = 0.25) [^S207]. At 1.57 °C/h a linear fall from 37 to 35 °C takes about 1.3 h, so a 2-5 minute plunge sits inside the cold-shock window and far outside the hypothermia window.

Mass-participation swims show where cardiac events cluster. In US triathlons from 1985 to 2016, 90 of the non-trauma sudden deaths and resuscitated arrests occurred in the swim, 7 on the bike, 15 on the run and 8 in recovery; incidence in USA Triathlon races was 1.74 per 100,000 participants and 18.6 per 100,000 in men aged 60 and older, and 27 of 61 decedents (44 %) had clinically relevant cardiovascular abnormalities at autopsy [^S202]. That abstract gives no water temperatures, so cold cannot be separated from crowding, exertion and anxiety; the funder was the Minneapolis Heart Institute Foundation [^S202]. In 58 cases of loss of consciousness during underwater swimming after hyperventilation, mostly in good swimmers aged 16 to 20 and about 80 % in guarded pools, 23 died, and the time from loss of consciousness to death "may be no longer than 2.5 minutes" [^S179]. A CDC and New York review of 1988-2011 found 16 cases of "dangerous underwater breath-holding behaviors", and practising more than one such behaviour raised the risk of death [^S196]. No PubMed-indexed case report links Wim Hof breathing specifically to a drowning; the hazard rests on this blackout literature and on the doubling of breath-hold time after hyperventilation [^S181].

### 6.5 Blood pressure and the heart in healthy adults and in heart disease

The claim that a plunge trains blood pressure starts from a real but modest pressor effect. In 1-h head-out immersions in young men (n not given in the abstract), 14 °C raised metabolic rate 350 %, heart rate 5 %, systolic pressure 7 %, diastolic pressure 8 %, plasma noradrenaline 530 % and plasma dopamine 250 %, with adrenaline unchanged; at 32 °C heart rate fell 15 % and pressure 11-12 % [^S94]. The much-quoted "dopamine 250 %" is a plasma value after 1 h at 14 °C, not a brain measurement and not a 2-3 minute plunge. In 28 habitual ice-water winter swimmers, systolic pressure rose while they waited undressed in cold air, immersion and swimming added no further systolic rise, pressures returned to control 4 minutes later and the ECG did not change, although "very high pressures were recorded in several subjects" without values [^S180].

Diseased coronary arteries respond the wrong way. During a hand cold pressor test with angiography, normal arteries in 8 patients dilated 12 ± 1 % with flow up 65 ± 4 %, while stenoses above 50 % in 13 patients constricted 24 ± 6 % with flow down 39 ± 8 %, [^S182]. In 22 patients with left anterior descending stenosis, a 45-s "dive" raised coronary vascular resistance by +44 ± 3.1 % in severe stenosis against +19 ± 1.6 % for a 2-minute cold pressor test; the abstract states neither water temperature nor manoeuvre, and PubMed's randomised-trial tag covers a within-patient comparison of two stimuli [^S184]. Breathing −20 °C air for four minutes provoked angina in 4 of 17 coronary patients at rest and in 4 of 7 paced at a subanginal rate [^S178].

The patient immersion trials reassure only within their protocols. In 12 men with compensated heart failure (EF 32 %, NYHA II) and 10 with stable coronary disease, chest-deep immersion at 22 °C gave a higher rate-pressure product than at 32 °C, and premature ventricular contractions in heart failure rose from 15 ± 41 to 76 ± 163 per 30 minutes (P = 0.013), about fivefold, without ventricular tachycardia; the authors wrote that "the increase in PVCs raises concerns", and 22 °C is far warmer than a typical plunge [^S192]. In 37 men around 61 years old (12 CHF, 13 CAD, 12 controls), two 80 °C sauna sessions followed by head-out immersion at 12 °C raised cardiac output, heart rate and systolic pressure in all groups but "did not provoke an excessive increase in adrenergic activity or complex arrhythmias"; immersion duration is not in the abstract, and the patients were stable and supervised [^S200]. Neither trial shows that unsupervised near-freezing plunges are safe in coronary disease or heart failure.

For healthy adults, a cold dip lowered the load of a sauna session. In a crossover of 16 healthy adults doing two 15-minute 85 °C sauna bouts, a 90-s whole-body dip at 20 °C in each cooling break left core temperature at 37.55 against 38.03 °C and heart rate at 108 against 125 bpm at the end of the second bout, with systolic pressure unchanged; the authors declared an unnamed industry partner that "did not provide a financial contribution" [^S219]. No trial in this set measured arrhythmias during sauna-to-ice transitions in people with uncontrolled hypertension.

### 6.6 Cold weather, myocardial infarction and the long-term record

The epidemiology of cold and heart attacks measures weather, not voluntary immersion. Across 84,010 MI admissions in England and Wales in 2003-6, each 1 °C fall in daily mean temperature was associated with a 2.0 % (95 % CI 1.1 to 2.9 %) cumulative rise in MI risk over 28 days, strongest at lags of 2-14 days, with people aged 75-84 and those with previous coronary heart disease more vulnerable; the funders were the British Heart Foundation, the Garfield Weston Trust and the Wellcome Trust [^S193]. In SWEDEHEART (274,029 patients, mean age 71.7 years), a 7.4 °C warmer day was associated with a 2.8 % lower MI risk (incidence ratio 0.972, 0.967 to 0.977) [^S203]. A Swedish case-crossover of 120,380 cold-season MIs gave OR 1.077 (1.037 to 1.120) for cold spells at lag 2-6 days, while at lag 0-1 days cold was associated with lower risk [^S216]. In 456,051 AMI patients in 323 Chinese cities, cold spells raised onset risk by 4.24 % (2.31 to 6.20 %), significant without central heating and null or much weaker with it [^S213]. A meta-analysis of 23 studies gave RR 1.014 (1.004 to 1.024) per 1 °C fall [^S204]. The signal works over days in older and already-ill people, and applying it to a 2-minute plunge is an inference.

The claim that winter swimmers have healthier hearts has no outcome study behind it: the searches found no PubMed-indexed cohort with myocardial infarction, stroke or cardiovascular death as outcomes in winter swimmers or cold plungers. A narrative review of 104 studies found conclusions "hampered by the fact that they were carried out in small groups, often of one gender", and judged that "whether winter swimmers as a group are naturally healthier is unclear" [^S135]. Another narrative review holds that graded winter swimming "seems to confer cardiovascular (CV), and other health benefits" while "unaccustomed individuals are at risk of death" [^S206]. The cross-sectional support is 10 cold-adapted swimmers against 16 controls with a lower ApoB/ApoA1 ratio and homocysteine, a design that cannot separate cold from self-selection [^S197]. The one randomised trial, 42 participants doing 15 days of daily Wim Hof Method, found no time × group interaction for resting heart rate, heart-rate variability, blood pressure or pulse wave velocity [^S214]. The Finnish evidence quoted for "sauna plus plunge" is the KIHD cohort of 2,315 men followed a median 20.7 years, in which 4-7 sauna sessions a week against one gave HR 0.37 (0.18 to 0.75) for sudden cardiac death; its exposure was sauna, and its abstract does not mention cold water [^S198].

The acclimatisation camp argues from mechanism and cross-sectional risk factors that graded exposure helps [^S206] [^S197]. The water-safety camp answers that those data come from self-selected healthy swimmers without hard outcomes, and that the measurable facts are the acute surge, the submersion arrhythmias and the deaths of the unhabituated [^S183] [^S218]. The reply is that those hazard data come from sudden immersion of unhabituated volunteers, not from the graded practice of experienced swimmers.

### 6.7 Allergic, vascular, cryotherapy and other risks

Cold urticaria is the contraindication with the clearest numbers. In COLD-CE, 551 patients at 32 specialist centres, 412 had a positive cold stimulation test and 151 of them (37 %) had cold-induced anaphylaxis, "most commonly provoked by complete cold water immersion" [^S209]. For Raynaud's phenomenon the indexed record is a NEJM review without a PubMed abstract, so no figure is quoted from it, and no trial tested plunges in Raynaud's [^S201]. People with a past non-freezing cold injury are more sensitive: in 16 participants, toe skin after a 2-minute 15 °C foot immersion rewarmed to 27.4 (2.3) °C at 10 minutes in NFCI against 31.7 (3.9) °C in controls, with greater cold intolerance (P < 0.0001) [^S212]. Swimming-induced pulmonary oedema presents as dyspnoea, cough and sometimes haemoptysis "particularly in cold water", with hypertension, female sex, tight wetsuits and prior episodes as listed risk factors [^S220]. In pregnancy no human trial or cohort exists; the only record is a Medical Hypotheses paper proposing, without data, that cold-water swimming might improve obstetric outcomes [^S208].

Cryotherapy chambers have a small record. A consortium scoping review found 16 documented adverse events from "true" whole-body cryotherapy, said part of the FDA and INSERM safety data came from partial-body liquid-nitrogen devices "often erroneously mistaken to be whole-body cryotherapy", and judged the risks "within acceptable limits" when contraindications are obeyed; several authors work in WBC research and application, and without a denominator no incidence rate exists [^S172]. A Delphi of 28 experts from the WBC Working Group of the International Institute of Refrigeration produced a list of contraindications that the abstract does not reproduce, from a group inside a refrigeration-sector body [^S217]. Three patients had frostbite after single sessions below −100 °C, one a full-thickness injury that needed grafting [^S210]. Local ice can injure nerves: a 22-year-old footballer who iced his distal hamstring for 20 minutes developed peroneal nerve palsy and recovered over a year [^S187].

### 6.8 What this means in practice

For a healthy adult, the cardiovascular risk of a plunge is concentrated in the first 1-3 minutes, not in hypothermia, which takes tens of minutes even in 10-16 °C water [^S188] [^S207] [^S189]. About four to six short immersions blunt the gasp and hyperventilation but leave most of the tachycardia, and anxiety can undo the gain [^S38] [^S195]. Keeping the head out, avoiding breath-holds at entry and never pairing hyperventilation with underwater breath-holding address the documented arrhythmia and blackout routes [^S185] [^S181] [^S179]. Coronary disease, heart failure, a known or suspected channelopathy, unexplained fainting, uncontrolled hypertension and cold urticaria are the settings in which the measured responses turn adverse, and the supervised patient trials do not cover unsupervised near-freezing plunges [^S182] [^S192] [^S190] [^S209]. No outcome study shows that regular plunging lowers cardiovascular events, and the Finnish mortality data concern sauna [^S198] [^S214]. Entering slowly, staying within reach of an exit and having another person present follow directly from these data [^S199] [^S218].


## 7. Protocol Parameters: Temperature, Duration, Frequency and Timing

### 7.1 Where the popular protocol numbers come from

Four numbers dominate the protocol advice: 10 to 15 °C for 11 to 15 minutes, "11 minutes a week", "2 to 3 minutes at 10 to 15 °C", and a cooling "dose" in which colder and longer are better. Each comes from a different literature. Recovery studies measure soreness, creatine kinase (CK) and performance in athletes; thermoregulation studies, often from military laboratories, measure core and muscle temperature, shivering and habituation [^S227]; brown adipose tissue (BAT) studies use hours of mild cold, not short plunges [^S109]. A number from one does not transfer to the others without evidence.

The 10 to 15 °C window comes from a 2016 meta-analysis of nine randomised trials of cold-water immersion (CWI) against passive recovery, with soreness as its only outcome: immediate soreness MD 0.290 (95 % CI 0.037 to 0.543), delayed soreness MD 0.315 (95 % CI 0.048 to 0.581), "slightly better than passive recovery" [^S63]. The abstract does not name the soreness scale, so the clinical size of an MD of about 0.3 cannot be judged. Its Results give "between 10 and 15 °C" and "between 10 and 15 min"; its Conclusions give "between 11 and 15 °C" and "11-15 min" [^S63]. The popular version mixes the two.

The "11 minutes a week" figure comes from a cross-sectional comparison of young Danish winter-swimming men with matched controls during 195 minutes of laboratory cooling; 8 swimmers were recruited and Table 1 lists 7 [^S134]. That table, in the full text rather than the abstract, gives self-reported habits: 2.6 swims a week, 1.4 minutes per immersion, "Total time immersed in cold water/week (min) 11" and 57 minutes a week of sauna, in water "between 9°C to 1°C" [^S134]. No group had less or more cold, and the authors note that "people with a certain thermophenotype could be more prone to take up winter swimming as a hobby" [^S134]. Funders included the Novo Nordisk Foundation, and one author consults for Novo Nordisk on BAT biology [^S134]. Calling 11 minutes "the minimum effective dose" adds a claim the paper does not make.

The "2 to 3 minutes at 10 to 15 °C" advice has no PubMed-indexed trial behind it in this search. The nearest tested protocols are 2.5 minutes at 10 °C for sprint and jump recovery [^S242], 5 minutes at 14 °C for core cooling after exercise in the heat [^S222], 20 seconds at 0 to 2 °C three times a week [^S20], and cold showers of 30 to 90 seconds at about 10 to 12 °C [^S29]. None tested 2 to 3 minutes at 10 to 15 °C as an optimum for mood, metabolism or longevity.

### 7.2 Temperature and duration against recovery outcomes

The meta-analyses point in different directions. The 2023 update from the Machado group, with 44 randomised trials, concluded that "water temperature and CWI protocol do not influence this result, but only short and medium immersions times presented positive effects", without stating the duration cut-offs in the abstract [^S84]. The group's own trial gave 60 men 15 minutes at 9 °C, 15 minutes at 14 °C or no cooling after eccentric exercise: both CWI arms lowered soreness right after recovery, the 14 °C arm recovered maximal voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC) earlier (P < 0.05), and "no evidence was found to suggest dose-response relationship" [^S65].

A 2022 meta-analysis of 52 studies found that 24 hours after high-intensity exercise, CWI improved power (SMD 0.22), CK (SMD −0.85), soreness (SMD −0.89) and perceived recovery (SMD 0.66), not strength recovery [^S83]. Its meta-regression found that "shorter time and lower temperatures were related to the largest beneficial effects on serum CK ... and endurance performance (duration dose effects only)", the opposite direction on duration to the 2016 window; one co-author holds a Norwood Football Club grant to study CWI [^S83]. A 2026 umbrella review of 15 meta-analyses (161 studies) rated quality "low to critically low" and certainty "low to very low", and stated that "protocols of 10-15 min at 10-15 or 5-10 °C appear to be most effective" [^S90].

The one head-to-head test is a randomised crossover in 15 male athletes: 15 °C for 11 minutes, 10 °C for 2.5 minutes, or seated rest [^S242]. The 2.5-minute protocol "maintained T20 performance during the acute phase" and alone improved countermovement jump (CMJ) at 72 hours; the 11-minute protocol "facilitated later recovery"; soreness "showed minimal differences" [^S242]. One author is affiliated with the football club Gamba Osaka [^S242].

The tested range is therefore about 5 to 15 °C for about 2.5 to 15 minutes, pooled from small trials with subjective or biochemical outcomes at low to very low certainty [^S63] [^S84] [^S90]. No single optimal pair of numbers has strong support.

### 7.3 Tissue-cooling kinetics and individual variation

The "cooling dose" concept comes from a 2019 regression of muscle tissue temperature, not of recovery or health outcomes: 10 studies, 104 participants, 26 protocols [^S232]. Twenty-four of 26 protocols reduced muscle temperature significantly (overall r = 0.51), the best fit was full-body immersion at 30 mm depth (r = 0.87, "y = 4.051 x + 0.535"), and the authors proposed "a minimum CWI dose of 1.1 ... corresponding with an immersion of 11 minutes with a water temperature of 10 °C" [^S232]. The example implies minutes divided by degrees; the abstract does not state the formula, so this is an inference. Two of four authors list the company ProCcare, one lists Manchester United FC, and funding is not stated [^S232]. On the implied index, 2.5 minutes at 10 °C scores 0.25 and 3 minutes at 15 °C scores 0.20; the index also grows without limit as water nears 0 °C, so it describes only the tested range.

Muscle cooling falls steeply with depth. Six pooled studies of 10 °C for 10 minutes on the quadriceps found maximum falls of 6.40 °C at 1 cm, 4.50 °C at 2 cm and 1.61 °C at 3 cm, the last not significant, and skinfold (about 6 to 11 mm) did not correlate with cooling [^S235]. In 16 young adults immersed to the iliac crest at 10 °C, by contrast, body fat percentage "significantly predicted the rectus femoris cooling magnitude and rate" (R² = 0.58 to 0.67) [^S236]. The pooled samples were thin; the single study spanned a wider range of bodies.

Duration trades core cooling against muscle cooling. Twelve cyclists exhausted at 40 °C received 5, 10 or 20 minutes at 14 °C, or 20 minutes at 24 °C: every CWI duration cooled rectal temperature faster than control, muscle stayed colder after 10 and 20 minutes than after 5, and the authors chose 5 minutes "as the most appropriate duration for reducing rectal temperature but limiting decreases in muscle temperature" [^S222]. Core temperature responds weakly to the dial: pooled data from 157 subjects give 0.03 °C (SE 0.01) of extra fall per 1 °C colder water and 0.02 °C (SE 0.01) per extra minute, with the peak difference 60 minutes after immersion [^S71].

Blood flow does not follow a "colder is stronger" rule. In 10 men after exercise, leg CWI at 8 °C for 10 minutes lowered deep muscle temperature 2.9 ± 0.8 °C against 1.3 ± 0.6 °C for −110 °C cryotherapy for 2 minutes, and femoral artery conductance fell 84 % against 59 % [^S228]. A PET study in 30 men then found that 22 °C water reduced global quadriceps perfusion beyond the clinical threshold (−2.5 mL per 100 g·min⁻¹) while 8 °C water had "no effect" [^S237]. In 9 men given 5 minutes at 8.6, 14.6 or 35.0 °C after exercise in the heat, both cold conditions lowered rectal temperature 1.0 °C at 60 minutes against 0.6 °C for control, only 8.6 °C kept muscle perfusion lower at 30 minutes, and the authors advise that "CWI below 10°C should not be used for short-term (i.e. <60 min) recovery after exercise" [^S229].

The same water and minutes give different bodies different internal doses. In 110 young adults (81 women), mean time to shivering was 116.1 minutes in men and 125.8 minutes in women; in women it rose with BMI, lean and fat mass (R² 0.12 to 0.14), in men no association reached significance (all p ≥ 0.409), in both it rose with body surface area, and it was unrelated to BAT volume or [18F]FDG uptake [^S238]. In a Canadian defence-institute comparison of 11 women and 14 men immersed to the neck at 18 °C for up to 90 minutes, the women's rectal cooling rate (0.47 °C/h) was "about one-half that in men", and sex differences disappeared when the groups were matched for fatness; "no gender adjustments are necessary ... if body fatness and the ratio of body surface area to size are taken into account" [^S221].

### 7.4 Depth, hydrostatic pressure and cold showers

Immersion acts before any cold is added. Head-out for one hour at thermoneutral 32 °C lowered heart rate 15 %, plasma renin activity 46 % and cortisol 34 % and raised diuresis 107 %; at 14 °C, metabolic rate rose 350 %, noradrenaline 530 % and plasma dopamine 250 %, while adrenaline did not change; the abstract does not give the group size [^S94]. The 250 % dopamine figure behind the popular claim therefore belongs to an hour at 14 °C, not to a 2- to 3-minute plunge.

More hydrostatic pressure did not improve recovery. Twenty-four games players given seated or standing CWI (14 minutes at 14 °C) or control showed no CWI effect on strength, CMJ, CK, CRP, IL-6 or soreness, and seated CWI gave lower soreness than standing (effect size 1.86) [^S226]. A 2026 meta-analysis of 30 randomised trials found that CWI reduced CK (g = −0.24) and soreness (g = −0.40), not CMJ or MVIC, with "no significant difference between whole-body and partial immersion at any follow-up time point (0-72 h)"; partial CWI impaired immediate CMJ (g = −0.94) [^S243]. Depth matters for the core: in 12 trained male runners given 10 minutes at 13.3 °C after an evening run, only head-in whole-body immersion lowered core temperature against control [^S234].

A cold shower is not the thermal equivalent of a tub. A 15 °C shower for 15 minutes after cycling at 35 °C "was not effective in reducing Tc or salivary cortisol" in 9 men, though heart rate fell faster (−18.3 % against −7.0 % at 30 minutes) [^S231], while 5 minutes of immersion at 14.6 °C lowered rectal temperature 1.0 °C at 60 minutes [^S229]. No study found here measured heat extraction for both directly. In the large shower trial, 3,018 adults ended a warm shower with 30, 60 or 90 seconds of cold water averaging "10–12°C"; sickness absence fell 29 % (IRR 0.71) with "no significant difference between the intervention groups (P = 0.992)", so 90 seconds gave no more than 30 [^S29]. The trial was unblinded with self-reported outcomes; one author worked for the insurer Achmea, and Wim Hof is thanked for "inspiration and contribution" [^S29].

### 7.5 Frequency: habituation counts and BAT recruitment

The cold-shock response, the gasp and hyperventilation of the first minutes, habituates fast. A 2024 meta-analysis of 17 groups found habituation "after ∼4 immersions, with variation between studies", with pooled falls in heart rate of 14 bt·min⁻¹ (d = −1.19, n = 145), respiratory frequency of 8 br·min⁻¹ and minute ventilation of 21.3 L·min⁻¹ [^S38]. In 8 men who did six extra 3-minute immersions at 15 °C over four days, first-30-second respiratory frequency fell from 47 to 24 breaths·min⁻¹ and heart rate from 128 to 109 beats·min⁻¹ at 10 °C; the reductions were still significant at 7 months, and at 14 months heart rate was still lower while breathing had returned towards baseline [^S13]. Habituating at 15 °C reduced the response at 10 °C, so the training water need not be as cold as the target.

What habituates depends on the dose. Fourteen men given six 10-minute immersions at 12 °C over three days, against thermoneutral controls at 35 °C, cut ventilation after three immersions (22.12 against 30.90 L·min⁻¹), habituated shivering sensation after 2 immersions and thermal comfort after 4, while oxygen consumption, heart rate, blood pressure and core-temperature change "did not demonstrate evidence of cold-water habituation"; the authors are US Army researchers and funding is not stated [^S241]. In 21 men, the metabolic response shrank only in the group whose core fell about 1.18 °C at each immersion (about 45 minutes), while 5-minute skin-only immersions habituated ventilation but not metabolism [^S26]. Brief plunges deliver habituation, the first of three adaptation patterns, not metabolic or insulative acclimatisation, which depend on "changes in skin and core temperature and the exposure duration" [^S227].

The human BAT-recruitment trials all used long exposures: two hours a day at 17 °C for six weeks [^S109], a 10-day mild-cold acclimation whose temperature and hours are not in the abstract [^S110], and one hour a day at 14 °C for seven days, which cut shivering 36 % with unchanged heat production [^S131]. The nearest test of a brief daily plunge, 16 daily 10-minute sessions at 14 °C in 12 non-obese adults, "did not affect adrenaline, noradrenaline, REE, and substrate oxidation rate" [^S142]. Very brief, very cold exposures keep their catecholamine response: 20 seconds at 0 to 2 °C, or 2 minutes at −110 °C, three times a week for 12 weeks, gave "2-fold to 3-fold" noradrenaline rises each time throughout [^S20].

### 7.6 Timing around training and sleep

CWI straight after resistance training reduces the growth the training was meant to produce; the trials are covered in depth in § 3, and the timing detail is what matters here. Ten minutes after each session for 12 weeks gave smaller strength and muscle-mass gains than active recovery [^S59]; 15 minutes at 10 °C after each session for seven weeks attenuated type II fibre hypertrophy (−1,959 ± 1,675 µm², ES −1.37) [^S73]; and 20 minutes at 8 °C on one leg lowered myofibrillar synthesis over the next 5 hours (0.058 against 0.072 %·h⁻¹) and over two weeks (1.48 against 1.67 %·day⁻¹) [^S75]. Pooled across 8 studies at ≤ 15 °C, strength gains fell (SMD −0.60, 95 % CI −0.87 to −0.33) [^S78].

The popular fix, "wait 4 to 6 hours" or "plunge on rest days", is an extrapolation. Every hypertrophy trial applied CWI within minutes of the session, and this search found no human trial comparing immediate with delayed CWI for muscle growth [^S59] [^S73] [^S75]. The time courses behind the mechanism are muscle perfusion held lower for at least 30 minutes after 8.6 °C water [^S229] and lower protein synthesis across 5 hours after an 8 °C leg immersion [^S75]. Cooling before power work can cost output: torque was lower 55 minutes after every cooling duration in the cyclists' study [^S222], and partial CWI cut immediate CMJ [^S243]. Endurance training shows neither harm nor gain: maximal aerobic power SMD −0.07 and time trial SMD 0.00 [^S78], and "no later benefits or positive effects on adaptations" [^S90].

Evening cold water did not disturb sleep in the two trials found, both in trained men after exercise. Eleven male cyclists exercising at about 19:00, with or without CWI, showed "no differences ... between conditions for any whole night sleep measures" on polysomnography; core temperature stayed lower for 90 minutes after bedtime and melatonin did not differ [^S224]. In the runners' study, head-in whole-body CWI at 13.3 °C increased slow-wave sleep in the first 180 minutes of the night against waist-deep immersion [^S234]. This search found no randomised trial of evening cold showers on objective sleep in healthy adults.

### 7.7 Heat, sauna, time of day, meals and pre-cooling

Alternating heat and cold has no special advantage. A meta-analysis of 18 contrast-water-therapy trials, all at "a high risk of bias", found less soreness and strength loss than passive recovery from under 6 hours to 96 hours, but "little evidence for a superior treatment intervention" against CWI, warm water, compression, active recovery or stretching [^S223]. Within a sauna session, a brief mild dip serves heat relief, not a cold dose. Sixteen adults (8 women) did two 15-minute bouts at 85 °C with 10-minute breaks in 23 °C air, with or without a 90-second immersion at 20 °C: at the end of the second bout, core temperature was 37.55 against 38.03 °C and heart rate 108 against 125 bpm with the dip; funding came from Bangor University and UKRI [^S219]. Because the winter swimmers behind "11 minutes" also averaged 57 minutes a week of sauna, their phenotype cannot be assigned to cold alone [^S134].

Time of day made no measurable difference to thermogenesis. In a randomised crossover of 14 adults, cold-induced thermogenesis was 16.8 % above resting energy expenditure at 08:00 and 15.7 % at 18:00 (P = 0.72), and in 133 others BAT [18F]FDG uptake was similar across the day [^S233]. No trial found here compared morning and evening plunges for mood, alertness or recovery. The only fasted-versus-fed trial randomised 12 non-obese women to two days of fasting with or without two 10-minute immersions, and to the same on a usual diet: cold "had no effect on REE" and, when fasted, reduced fasting-induced glucose intolerance [^S239]. It was short, small, female-only and measured glucose markers, not body fat.

Pre-cooling before exercise in the heat is where purpose and outcome are most directly linked. Across 28 studies in men exercising above 30 °C, cooling improved performance by 6.7 ± 0.9 % (ES 0.43), pre-cooling by 5.7 % and per-cooling by 9.9 %, and finishing core temperature did not correlate with performance [^S225]. Across 22 crossover studies at ambient temperatures of at least 26 °C, CWI pre-cooling improved endurance performance (g = +0.53, 95 % CI 0.28 to 0.77), while ice slurry or crushed ice showed "no clear effect" (g = +0.2, 95 % CI −0.07 to 0.46) [^S230]. In 59 studies of 563 male athletes at an average 34.0 °C, pre-cooling helped constant-workload tests more than self-paced tests (ES 0.74 against 0.29); two authors sit on the advisory board of Inuteq, a cooling-garment maker [^S240]. All three meta-analyses studied mainly or only men.

### 7.8 What this means in practice

No trial validates a minimum effective dose: "11 minutes a week" is the self-reported habit of seven winter swimmers who also used the sauna [^S134], and "2 to 3 minutes at 10 to 15 °C" has no trial behind it in this search. For next-day soreness, the tested range is about 5 to 15 °C for 2.5 to 15 minutes at low certainty, with mostly subjective benefit [^S83] [^S90]. If muscle growth is the goal, skip cold water straight after lifting; no trial says how long a delay removes the effect [^S73] [^S78]. Expect the first-minute gasp to settle after about four to six short immersions, while heart rate and heat production keep responding [^S38] [^S241]. Deep thigh muscle barely cools in 10 minutes at 10 °C, and a cold shower will not cool your core the way a tub does [^S235] [^S231]. The best-supported performance use is pre-cooling by immersion before hard work in the heat [^S230].


## 8. Whole-Body Cryotherapy, Contrast Therapy, Local Cooling and the Remaining Claims

### 8.1 The chamber and what the systematic evidence says

Whole-body cryotherapy (WBC) is sold as the faster, drier alternative to an ice bath: two to four minutes in very cold air, marketed for recovery, inflammation, sleep, testosterone, skin and longevity. The definition moves with the author. The Cochrane review uses "extremely cold dry air (below -100 °C) in a specialised chamber or cabin for two to four minutes per exposure" [^S56]; a review with a systematic search uses −110 to −190 °C [^S259]; the consensus paper of the International Institute of Refrigeration's working group widens it to "an extreme cold stimulation lasting 1-4 min and given in a cold room at a temperature comprised from -60 to -195°C" [^S265]; and one head-to-head trial ran at −85 °C [^S74]. Many commercial "cryotherapy" devices are not walk-in chambers but nitrogen-vapour partial-body cabins (PBC) with the head outside [^S172].

The Cochrane review, searched to August 2015, found four laboratory RCTs with 64 physically active participants (mean age 23 years, all but four male), every trial at high risk of bias, and GRADE rated every outcome "very low" quality [^S56]. Soreness against rest was SMD −0.77 (95 % CI −1.42 to −0.12) at 1 hour, from 20 participants in two crossover trials, then −0.57 (−1.48 to 0.33) at 24 hours, −0.58 (−1.37 to 0.21) at 48 hours and −0.65 (−2.54 to 1.24) at 72 hours, intervals that include no difference or a benefit for control [^S56]. No trial compared WBC with cold water, none actively surveyed adverse events, and the conclusion was "insufficient evidence to determine whether whole-body cryotherapy (WBC) reduces self-reported muscle soreness, or improves subjective recovery", with no data in women or elite athletes; two review authors had co-authored included trials, which other authors assessed [^S56]. A narrative review by the same group found ten mostly small reports in athletes under 35, "weak evidence" for antioxidant, parasympathetic and inflammatory changes, and better subjective recovery "but little benefit towards functional recovery" [^S257].

The proponent reading is a vote count. Of 16 eligible articles, "muscle pain was reduced in 80% of studies following WBC" and performance improved in 71 % of controlled-exercise studies, so WBC "may improve recovery", more consistently with repeated exposures, with recommendations called "preliminary" [^S259]. Counting positive studies gives no weight to size or bias, the sceptics' main criticism. The consensus paper acknowledges "controversies about the benefits", is a position paper rather than a systematic review, and declares authors employed by ProCcare BVBA, Mobilito Sport, Krioplanet Ltd and the industrial-gas company Air Liquide [^S265]. Pooling all post-exercise cooling (mostly water) across 36 articles gives DOMS g −0.75 (−1.20 to −0.30) at 24 hours but "no evidence, that cooling affects any objective recovery variable in a significant way during a 96 hrs recovery period", with high performance and detection bias [^S57]. WBC against partial-body cabins, six studies with 120 participants and a mean PEDro of 4.3/10, gave "similar effects without difference between them" [^S266].

### 8.2 Air against water: the physics, the head-to-head trials and the biomarkers

"The relatively poor thermal conductivity of air prevents significant subcutaneous and core body cooling" [^S257]. (Water conducts heat roughly 20 to 25 times better than air, about 0.6 against 0.025 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹; textbook physics, not a PubMed record.) In a randomised crossover of 20 active men given 4 minutes at −110 °C air or in 8 °C water, vastus lateralis temperature (n = 10) fell most 60 minutes later, by 1.6 ± 1.2 against 2.0 ± 1.0 °C at 1 cm and 1.6 ± 0.6 against 1.7 ± 0.5 °C at 3 cm; rectal temperature fell 0.3 ± 0.2 against 0.4 ± 0.2 °C; and skin fell 12.1 ± 1.0 °C in air against 8.4 ± 0.7 °C in water [^S252]. The chamber cools the skin hard, which is what the user feels, and the muscle by a similar small amount.

With the protocols people actually use, water wins. Ten men heated by cycling to a rectal temperature of 38 °C were crossed over between 10 minutes of lower-body immersion at 8 °C and 2 minutes of WBC at −110 °C: deep muscle fell 2.9 ± 0.8 against 1.3 ± 0.6 °C, superficial muscle 4.4 ± 1.3 against 1.8 ± 1.1 °C and femoral artery conductance 84 % ± 11 % against 59 % ± 21 %, while rectal temperature fell 0.6 °C in both, with non-US government funding, source not stated [^S228]. The deep-muscle drop is about 2.2 times larger in water.

"Cryotherapy chambers beat water" is tested directly by two placebo-controlled trials. Thirty-one endurance-trained men randomised after a marathon to cold water, WBC or placebo showed that "WBC had a harmful effect on muscle function compared to CWI", with WBC better only on perceived training stress; apart from CRP at 24 and 48 hours neither beat placebo on blood markers, and "treatment belief and the placebo effect may be largely responsible" [^S72]. In 24 resistance-trained men matched (not described as randomised) to water at 10 °C for 10 minutes, WBC at −85 °C for 3 and 4 minutes or placebo, WBC eased 24-hour soreness and helped 48-hour peak force, but "many of the remaining outcomes were trivial, unclear or favoured the PL condition" and neither treatment beat placebo [^S74]. The syntheses disagree. Thirteen RCTs with 214 participants found cold water "significantly more effective" than body cryotherapy for 24-hour DOMS (MD 1.07, 95 % CI 0.70 to 1.43), no difference at 48 hours (MD 0.47, −0.04 to 0.98) and "a small and fragile effect" for cryotherapy on 24-hour jump height (SMD 0.52, 0.03 to 1.02), with no funding or conflicts [^S274]. A network meta-analysis of 57 studies and 1,220 participants ranked "cryotherapy" first for DOMS (SUCRA 88.3 %) and jump recovery (83.7 %) and contrast water first for creatine kinase (79.9 %); what its cryotherapy node contains is in the full text [full text, not verified here], and SUCRA ranks order, not the size of a gap [^S271].

The anti-inflammatory claim has its own null. Three minutes at −110 °C after high-intensity running in 11 endurance-trained men "did not attenuate exercise- induced changes in IL-6, IL-10, sICAM-1, myoglobin, cortisol, testosterone or their ratio" [^S263]. In 14 Premier League academy players given 30 seconds at −60 °C then 120 seconds at −135 °C, salivary testosterone was 21 % higher at 2 hours and 28 % at 24 hours, while cortisol, lactate, creatine kinase, peak power, perceived recovery and soreness did not differ [^S260].

### 8.3 Safety of chambers and nitrogen cabins

A scoping review by an international consortium of WBC researchers found five case reports and two RCTs, "for a total of 16 documented adverse events", noted safety concerns from the FDA and INSERM, argued that part of the underlying data came from nitrogen partial-body cabins "often erroneously mistaken to be whole-body cryotherapy", and judged WBC risks "within acceptable limits" with adherence to contraindications; an erratum, not a retraction, exists [^S172]. A burn-centre series describes frostbite after single sessions in a "liquid nitrogen cryochamber": a 26-year-old man with full-thickness injury that "eventually required excision and grafting", and two people aged 17 and 28 with partial-thickness injuries [^S210]. Its line that "there is no evidence that cryotherapy provides any benefits" is stronger than the Cochrane wording of insufficient evidence [^S210] [^S56].

A retrospective survey of Division I athletes at one university reached 51 of 457 (11.2 %), of whom 29 qualified: within an hour of WBC 27.6 % (n = 8) reported a rash, 13.8 % (n = 4) itching and 6.9 % (n = 2) fatigue, and 58.6 % none [^S267]. An 11 % response rate makes these shares unreliable as population rates. Both Cochrane reviews note that most trials did not actively survey adverse events [^S56] [^S48], and no indexed paper gives a denominator of sessions, so no risk per session can be calculated. The FDA consumer statement that WBC is not cleared for any medical condition and news reports of at least one death in a nitrogen-cooled cabin are not PubMed records; no indexed case report of a death was found.

### 8.4 Contrast water therapy and the "flushing lactic acid" claim

Contrast water therapy (CWT) alternates hot and cold water and is sold as a pump that "flushes lactic acid". Against doing nothing it helps: a meta-analysis of 18 trials, "all had a high risk of bias", pooled 13 and found "significantly greater improvements in muscle soreness at the five follow-up time points (<6, 24, 48, 72 and 96 hours) in comparison to passive recovery" and less strength loss, but against cold water, warm water, compression, active recovery and stretching "there was little evidence for a superior treatment intervention"; its effect sizes are in the full text [full text, not verified here] [^S223]. The Cochrane review of cold water immersion pooled five studies against contrast immersion and found "no evidence of differences between the two groups" for pain to 72 hours [^S48]. A performance review suggests equal hot and cold time, bouts of about 1 minute and up to about 15 minutes in total, and states that a dose-response relationship "is unlikely to exist" [^S51].

The lactate premise has two parts, and the first fails. Forty-five minutes of level running raised lactic acid without significant soreness, while downhill running at −10 % never raised it yet produced delayed-onset soreness, so "lactic acid is not related to exercise-induced delayed-onset muscle soreness" [^S244].

The second part is true but small. After four 30-second Wingate tests in 11 subjects, alternating partial immersion at 36 °C and 12 °C cleared plasma lactate at 0.28 (±0.02) against 0.22 (±0.02) mmol L⁻¹ min⁻¹ with passive rest (p < 0.001), "An approximately 1.8 mmol L(-1) difference" after 30 minutes [^S249]. In 20 junior rugby players, 6 minutes of contrast (three 1-minute hip-height immersions at 8 to 10 °C alternated with three 1-minute showers at 38 °C) lowered lactate by 2.1 mmol L⁻¹ (95 % confidence limits ±1.8) against slow jogging, but effects on repeated sprints an hour later were "small to trivial and unclear" [^S248]. In 10 men, active recovery cleared lactate faster than rest, fastest "close to the lactate threshold" [^S250]. Easy movement is the reference method, a faster fall has not improved the next performance, and lactate is back at baseline long before DOMS peaks at 24 to 72 hours; contrast may genuinely ease soreness against rest, but "lactate flushing" is not a supported mechanism [^S223] [^S244].

### 8.5 Ice for acute soft-tissue injury: RICE, its reversal and PEACE and LOVE

RICE (rest, ice, compression, elevation) is commonly credited to Gabe Mirkin's 1978 *Sportsmedicine Book*, and his later statement that ice may delay healing appears only in non-indexed sources; neither is a PubMed record. A systematic review of 22 RCTs to April 2002 gave "a mean PEDro score of 3.4 out of of 10", "marginal evidence that ice plus exercise is most effective, after ankle sprain and postsurgery", little effect of ice added to compression (in inpatients only) and "no evidence of an optimal mode or duration of treatment" [^S246]. A review of 11 trials with 868 adults found that "Insufficient evidence is available from randomized controlled trials to determine the relative effectiveness of RICE therapy for acute ankle sprains in adults" [^S253]. The Dutch ankle-sprain guideline update prefers supervised exercise-based programmes over passive modalities and warns that NSAIDs "may suppress the natural healing process"; its abstract gives no ice recommendation, which sits in the full text [full text, not verified here] [^S261].

PEACE and LOVE, which drops ice from acute care, was introduced in a BJSM editorial, expert opinion with no abstract in PubMed and not a study; its components are in the full text [full text, not verified here] [^S264]. The "ice delays healing" evidence is animal plus one small human trial. In rats with a crush injury, 20 minutes of ice packs retarded necrotic-fibre degeneration and satellite-cell differentiation "by ∼1 day", delayed macrophage and TGF-β1/IGF-I changes by 1 to 2 days, and left a smaller fibre cross-sectional area at 28 days with more collagen at 14 and 28 days [^S251]. In 11 men in a randomised crossover after eccentric elbow work, cold packs for 15 minutes at 0, 3, 24, 48 and 72 hours gave "greater elevations in circulating CK-MB and myoglobin" at 48 to 72 hours and more fatigue at 72 hours, with strength unaffected; the authors wrote that icing "seems to not improve but rather delay recovery", but these are blood markers after exercise, not healing of an injury [^S256]. No human trial shows that ice improves long-term healing, and none shows that it harms it; the move from RICE to PEACE and LOVE rests on that gap and on expert opinion [^S246] [^S264].

### 8.6 Testosterone, fertility, sleep, skin and longevity

"Cold boosts testosterone" has no controlled human support at rest. Twenty healthy men in shorts spent 2 hours at 10 °C with "no significant changes in the serum concentration of adrenalin, T3, T4, testosterone, TSH or LH", while noradrenaline rose from 4.5 to 6.3 nmol/L and cortisol, GH and prolactin fell by 20, 87 and 48 % [^S245]. One immersion below 4 °C at −15 °C air in 13 young men lowered testosterone (p = 0.000037; size not in the abstract), within "non-pathological limits", in an uncontrolled pre-post design that carries an erratum [^S273]. After squats in 11 resistance-trained men, 15 minutes at 15 °C turned the 9.2 % free-testosterone rise at 30 minutes into −0.5 %, and at 60 minutes testosterone sat 10.4 % below baseline against 1.6 % after passive recovery, with IL-6 and TNF-α also suppressed [^S262]. After WBC, salivary testosterone rose in one trial and serum testosterone did not change in another [^S260] [^S263]. Every change, −10.4 % to +28 %, was transient, inside the normal range and unlinked to muscle, performance or fertility.

The fertility idea comes from scrotal temperature, not whole-body cold. Eight articles on scrotal-cooling devices worn for weeks, mostly by subfertile men, gave "insufficient evidence to draw any firm conclusions about the impact of scrotal cooling on male fertility", with "a positive trend" [^S255]. No study links cold plunges or showers to semen quality or pregnancy in healthy men.

The sleep claims rest on small trials, mostly from one French network. In 10 elite synchronised swimmers crossed over between two 2-week intensified blocks, sleep duration fell 21 ± 7 minutes, efficiency 1.9 % ± 0.8 % and latency rose 11 ± 5 minutes without daily WBC, and these "did not change" with it, on a qualitative chances-of-benefit analysis [^S258]. In 20 healthy young adults (9 women) randomised to 5 days with or without WBC, subjective sleep quality rose from 3.6 ± 0.5 to 3.9 ± 0.3 and slow-wave sleep by 7.3 ± 16.8 minutes, with no change in other parameters or nocturnal HRV [^S269]. In 22 well-trained male athletes, "WBC after training did not modify subjective or objective sleep parameters compared to the control condition" [^S270]. Some authors of these trials co-authored the industry-linked position paper [^S265], a fact about the network, not evidence of bias in a given trial.

Skin has no human outcome data, and the longevity citation is mice engineered to run a warmer hypothalamus, whose core temperature fell 0.3 to 0.5 °C and median lifespan rose 12 % in males and 20 % in females [^S247]. That is a genetically lowered set point, not intermittent external cold; a human in cold water defends core temperature, which fell only 0.3 to 0.6 °C transiently in the tissue-temperature trials [^S252] [^S228]. Hormesis is a framework, not an outcome, and no human trial has measured disease, mortality or functional ageing after repeated cold in healthy adults.

### 8.7 Cold-water swimming surveys, self-selection and placebo

The menopause claim comes from Pound et al. 2024 (Massey is the second author): an online survey "advertised for 2 months on social media" to 1,114 women who already swim in cold water [^S272]. Perimenopausal respondents reported better anxiety (46.9 %), mood swings (34.5 %), low mood (31.1 %) and hot flushes (30.3 %); 56.4 % with period symptoms and 63.3 % with perimenopausal symptoms swam specifically to relieve them; free text named "companionship and community"; the authors note that other forms of exercise "may show similar findings"; and one author gives paid talks on menopause [^S272]. A survey of 717 Scottish open-water swimmers rated mental well-being the main perceived benefit, cross-sectionally [^S268]. Winter swimmers followed from October to January reported less tension, fatigue and negative mood and more vigour than controls (p < 0.05), but allocation was by choice and the swimmers "had more diseases (about 50%) diagnosed by a physician" [^S18].

These designs share predictable biases: people who felt worse or got hurt stop, so surveys sample those who stayed; 63.3 % swam in order to relieve the symptom they then rated [^S272]; outdoor swimming bundles exercise, daylight, nature, a group and a ritual; and two of the three have no comparison group [^S272] [^S268]. The meta-analysis shows the predicted pattern: 13 studies with 4,105 participants "generally favored" cold water for depressive symptoms, stress, well-being and mood, "but confidence intervals were wide and several estimates attenuated in controlled-only analyses", most studies had high, serious or critical risk of bias, and "The available evidence does not establish that CWE improves mood" [^S43]. The Portsmouth review finds some claims "at the level of anecdotal speculation" and treats cold water as a drowning and cardiac hazard [^S165].

Cold cannot be blinded, so the strongest designs give a credible placebo. Thirty men after four 30-second sprints spent 15 minutes in water at 10.3 °C, thermoneutral water at 34.7 °C presented as a recovery aid, or the same water as plain control; strength and readiness, pain and vigour were impaired only in the plain control, so the placebo "is as effective as CWI" and the benefits are "at least partly placebo related" [^S52]. In 12 rugby players crossed over between 15 minutes at 14 °C, 30 °C water and passive rest, the subjective rating of the treatment correlated with next-day sprint performance (r = 0.5886, p = 0.0441), and temperature drop plus rating more strongly (r = 0.7743) [^S254]. One camp says a belief-driven gain in strength and readiness is still a gain; the other says the same gain comes from a cheaper, safer ritual and cannot justify a premium chamber or an anti-inflammatory claim [^S52] [^S72].

### 8.8 What this means in practice

For a healthy adult paying for a cryotherapy chamber to recover from training, the evidence is four small trials of very low certainty, the effect is on felt soreness for hours to a day, and neither the chamber nor cold water beat a placebo for functional recovery [^S56] [^S72] [^S74]. If the aim is to cool muscle, 8 to 10 °C water for about 10 minutes does it better than 2 minutes of −110 °C air, and chambers and nitrogen cabins carry documented frostbite at an unknown rate per session [^S228] [^S210] [^S172]. Contrast therapy is a reasonable choice for how it feels, but easy movement clears lactate as well and lactate does not cause next-day soreness [^S250] [^S244]. For a fresh sprain, ice can ease pain but has no proven effect on healing either way, and supervised exercise has the strongest guideline support [^S246] [^S261]. Cold is not a testosterone or fertility tool, skin and longevity claims have no human data, and the mood and menopause benefits swimmers report cannot be separated from exercise, community and expectation [^S262] [^S255] [^S43].

## 10. The Cold Habit, Worked Through

This section turns the evidence into the decisions a healthy adult actually makes. None of it is a prescription.

**Whether to start.** A healthy adult who enjoys a cold shower or plunge gets a reliable noradrenaline surge [^S1] [^S20], a small and expectation-sensitive soreness benefit [^S48] [^S52] and, in the one large trial, fewer self-reported sick days [^S29]. There is no metabolic, immune or dopamine reason to start, and no evidence that a person who dislikes cold misses a health benefit by skipping it [^S43] [^S128].

**How cold and how long.** No optimal temperature-duration pair exists: recovery trials span roughly 5 to 15 °C for 2.5 to 15 minutes, and the largest update found that water temperature did not change the soreness benefit [^S84]. Brief dips habituate the cold shock but not heat production [^S241]. Thirty seconds of cold at the end of a shower performed as well as 90 seconds in the shower trial [^S29].

**When, relative to training.** If muscle growth is the goal, keep cold water away from the minutes after lifting [^S75] [^S59]. Endurance adaptation was not clearly harmed [^S64] [^S78]. When the next performance is within about an hour, especially in the heat, cold water helps [^S45] [^S47], and pre-cooling before a hot endurance event is the best-supported performance use [^S230] [^S225].

**How to stay safe.** Enter slowly, keep the head out, do not breath-hold or hyperventilate in water, never immerse alone in open water, and treat the first minute as the risk window [^S188] [^S185] [^S181] [^S199]. Anxiety enlarges the cold shock response [^S195]. People with coronary disease, arrhythmia, heart failure, uncontrolled hypertension or cold urticaria have specific mechanisms of harm [^S182] [^S192] [^S209], and cognition can stay impaired for an hour after a long or very cold exposure [^S24].

## 11. Funding and the Shape of the Evidence Base

Most of the physiology comes from military and government laboratories (US Army, Canadian defence research, the UK Institute of Naval Medicine tradition around Tipton) and university sports-science groups, many without stated industry funding. The commercial links in this evidence base are specific and named in sources: the cryostimulation consensus paper's authors are employed by Air Liquide, Krioplanet and ProCcare [^S265]; the "cooling dose" index comes from authors linked to ProCcare [^S232]; a pre-cooling meta-analysis has authors who advise the cooling-garment maker Inuteq [^S240]; Søberg 2021 was funded in part by the Novo Nordisk Foundation, with an author consulting for Novo Nordisk [^S134]; the capsinoid co-intervention paper has an Ajinomoto-affiliated author [^S109]; and the Dutch shower trial had a co-author from the insurer Achmea and acknowledged Wim Hof [^S29].

Three structural features shape the evidence more than any funder. First, almost every trial is small and male: the strength meta-analysis was 92 % male [^S85], and the Cochrane cryotherapy review had 60 men of 64 participants [^S56]. Second, blinding is nearly impossible, so subjective outcomes such as soreness, mood and perceived recovery carry expectation effects that the placebo-controlled trials expose [^S52] [^S72] [^S74]. Third, the winter-swimming literature is cross-sectional and self-selected, so swimmers' physiology cannot be separated from who chooses to swim [^S134] [^S272].

## 12. Method and Limits of This Review

Seven schema-free cluster researchers each searched PubMed through NCBI E-utilities during this run (15 November 2026), read the abstracts they cite and ended with a structured identifiers table. The tables were parsed programmatically; 274 unique PMIDs were resolved against PubMed esummary and checked against each agent's claimed first author, year and journal; 272 matched, 0 were wrong, 0 missing and 0 retracted, and two records (an editorial and a modelling paper that PubMed also types Comment) are cited only as what they are. The bibliography is generated from those records and every citation in this report was converted from a PMID token against it.

Limits. Numbers come from abstracts; where a figure lives only in a full text (Søberg 2021's Table 1, Becher 2021, the Tipton 1989 timing figures) the cluster reports marked it, and this report quotes it as such. Piñero 2024, a hypertrophy meta-analysis with a PMC record but no PubMed record found by E-utilities, the RNLI "float first" campaign, the FDA position on whole-body cryotherapy and any reported chamber death are not PubMed records and are named only in plain text. No human study of brain dopamine during cold water, no trial of evening cold showers and sleep, no trial of a delay between lifting and cold, and no randomised trial of laboratory-confirmed infection were found. Absence of evidence here is stated as absence, not as proof of no effect.

## The full findings — all 45

??? note "Tier 1 — Settled (High Confidence) (7 findings)"

    **1. Adults carry functional brown fat that mild cold recruits: 2 h at 16 °C activated it in 23 of 24 healthy men, and detection falls with age, BMI and summer.**

    UCP1-positive brown adipocytes were confirmed by biopsy, and cold raised supraclavicular glucose uptake 15-fold. BAT activity appeared in 23 of 24 men at 16 °C and none at 22 °C, and after 2 h at 19 °C in 17 of 32 adults aged 23 to 35 against 2 of 24 aged 38 to 65, more in winter. Detection was over 50 % in people in their twenties and under 10 % in their fifties and sixties, and unstimulated clinical scans show only 6.8 to 8.5 % prevalence against an estimated true 64 %.

    **2. Plasma dopamine does not reflect brain dopamine: the blood-brain barrier blocks and degrades circulating monoamines, and the gut makes close to half of the body's dopamine.**

    Brain-vessel endothelium excludes circulating monoamines and enzymatically breaks down the fraction that passes, which is why Parkinson's disease is treated with L-dopa rather than dopamine. Mesenteric organs produce close to half of body dopamine, 12.0 nmol/min, mostly from non-neuronal cells, and plasma catechols reflect distinct peripheral sources. Chronic cold did not raise basal brain dopamine in rats and trended lower, and no human brain-dopamine study of cold water was found, although PET can detect dopamine release during a video game.

    **3. Cold water reliably raises plasma noradrenaline 2- to 6-fold, inside the 1.5- to >20-fold range of exercise, and it returns to baseline about 30 minutes after rewarming.**

    Six men in 10 °C water went from 359 ± 32 to a peak of 1,171 ± 226 pg/ml at 45 minutes, then fell to basal within 30 minutes of warm rewarming while core temperature was still low. Women doing 20-second winter swims at 0 to 2 °C or 2-minute cryotherapy at −110 °C showed 2- to 3-fold rises every time for 12 weeks with adrenaline unchanged. Exercise raises catecholamines 1.5 to more than 20 times basal.

    **4. The acute danger of cold water is cold shock in the first minutes, with about 47 breaths/min, 68 L/min of ventilation and 128 beats/min in the first 30 s at 10 °C, long before hypothermia.**

    In 13 healthy men seated head-out at 10 °C for 3 minutes, the first 30 s gave 47.3 breaths/min, 67.6 L/min and 128 beats/min, and a second cohort gave 47 breaths/min, 72.2 L/min and 128 beats/min. The four-stage model lists aspiration, hypocapnic cerebral hypoperfusion and arrhythmia as cold-shock mechanisms of death before core temperature falls, and the classic review calls cold shock "probably responsible for the majority" of open-water immersion deaths, an expert estimate. Lean swimmers lost 1.57 °C/h of core temperature at 16 °C, and most people overestimate how fast hypothermia arrives.

    **5. About four short immersions habituate cold shock, cutting heart rate by a pooled 14 beats/min and ventilation by 21.3 L/min; the habituation lasts months, but anxiety can reverse it, most of the tachycardia remains, and brief dips do not habituate heat production.**

    A meta-analysis of 17 groups places habituation "after ∼4 immersions" with heart rate −14 (10) beats/min and minute ventilation −21.3 (9.8) L/min. Six 3-minute dips at 15 °C cut the 10 °C heart rate only from 128 to 109 beats/min while breathing fell from 47.3 to 24.0 breaths/min, and the effect lasted 7 to 14 months. Telling people the water would be 5 °C colder raised peak heart rate to 136.4 against 124.0 beats/min and erased five immersions of habituation. Six 10 °C showers cut breathing frequency 21 % but not the first-minute heart-rate surge, and 15 °C showers did nothing. Six 10-minute immersions at 12 °C habituated ventilation and sensation but not oxygen consumption, heart rate or blood pressure, and the metabolic response shrank only with repeated core cooling of about 1.18 °C.

    **6. Breath-holding in cold water produced ectopic arrhythmias in 29 of 36 submersions in healthy subjects, mostly in the 10 s after the breath-hold ended.**

    Twelve subjects submerged at 5 and 10 °C had ectopic arrhythmias in 29 of 36 submersions, "predominantly in the 10-s period of submersion after the cessation of breath holding". Even in 29.5 °C water, 25 % of helicopter-escape submersions in 26 men produced arrhythmias. The "autonomic conflict" between cold shock and the diving response is the proposed mechanism, stated as a hypothesis without a death count. Swimming is a known trigger in LQT1 and CPVT, with 43 of 388 long-QT referrals carrying a drowning or near-drowning history, and long 15 °C swims raised QTc above 500 ms in 5 of 20 trained swimmers.

    **7. Hours of lowered core temperature clearly impair infection defence: surgical patients held at 34.7 °C instead of 36.6 °C had 19 % versus 6 % wound infections (P = 0.009) and stayed 2.6 days longer.**

    This randomised trial of 200 colorectal surgery patients marks where cold becomes immunologically harmful. A plunge followed by rewarming is a different exposure, and a narrative review links longer cold exposure with more respiratory infection, "although not all studies agree".


??? note "Tier 2 — Well Supported (Moderate Confidence) (16 findings)"

    **8. Cold water immersion after a hard session reduces muscle soreness by SMD −0.55 at 24 hours and −0.93 at 72 hours against passive rest, but the evidence is low quality.**

    The Cochrane review of 17 small trials and 366 participants reported SMD −0.55 (95 % CI −0.84 to −0.27) at 24 hours and −0.93 (−1.36 to −0.51) at 72 hours, with heterogeneous results. Across 52 studies, CWI lowered 24-hour soreness after high-intensity exercise by SMD −0.89 but had no significant effect on strength recovery. A 36-article review found "no evidence, that cooling affects any objective recovery variable in a significant way" over 96 hours.

    **9. Cold water immersion straight after resistance training reduces strength gains by a small pooled effect of ES −0.23, and whole-body immersion alone was not significant (ES −0.08).**

    A meta-analysis of 10 studies and 170 participants (92 % male) found CWI attenuated strength gains overall (ES −0.23; 95 % CI −0.45 to −0.01) and with trained-limb cooling (ES −0.31), but not with whole-body CWI (ES −0.08; 95 % CI −0.53 to 0.38). An eight-article meta-analysis restricted to CWI at ≤ 15 °C found SMD −0.60 (95 % CI −0.87 to −0.33) for strength after resistance training. An umbrella review of 15 meta-analyses rated certainty "low to very low" and concluded that CWI "impaired strength and hypertrophic gains".

    **10. A leg cooled in 8 °C water for 20 minutes after lifting made about 11 % less myofibrillar protein per day over two weeks (1.48 vs 1.67 %/day) than the other leg at 30 °C.**

    In 12 healthy males, five-hour post-exercise synthesis was 0.058 ± 0.011 against 0.072 ± 0.017 %/h (P = 0.024) and dietary protein incorporation 0.016 ± 0.006 against 0.021 ± 0.007 MPE (P = 0.016). The same two-leg design found microvascular blood volume 1.24 ± 0.82 against 3.13 ± 1.64 video intensity right after immersion (P < 0.001), with amino-acid incorporation 0.011 against 0.016 MPE and a correlation of r = 0.65 between the two deficits. In a seven-week trial, 15 minutes at 10 °C attenuated type II fibre growth by −1,959 ± 1,675 µm² while 1-RM gains did not differ.

    **11. Cold water immersion after endurance or interval training neither clearly harms nor clearly improves aerobic adaptation (pooled SMD −0.07 for maximal aerobic power).**

    The pooled endurance outcome was SMD −0.07 (95 % CI −0.54 to 0.53). Six weeks of sprint interval training with 15 minutes at 10 °C left VO2max, mitochondrial respiration and 2-km time trial unchanged in 16 men, and five weeks of HIIT with 15 minutes at 11.2 °C showed no condition effect in 16 males. Higher PGC-1α mRNA in a cooled leg did not reach significance as protein (P = 0.079).

    **12. Directly measured brown-fat heat is small: 6.3 kcal/d during mild cold in people with the largest depots, and about 1 % of the extra heat the body makes in the cold.**

    [15O] PET with calorimetry in 25 adults measured BAT heat at 3.2 kcal/d at rest and 6.3 ± 3.5 kcal/d in mild cold, with 15 to 25 kcal/d stated only as a scenario for large depots. In seven subjects BAT accounted for "almost 1 %" of the cold-induced rise in whole-body expenditure, with deep muscles a major contributor, and muscle glucose uptake exceeded BAT uptake 674 against 12 µmol/min. Reviews note that FDG uptake can be disconnected from heat and that BAT volume may be underestimated.

    **13. Cold acclimation changes where heat comes from, not how much: shivering fell 21 to 36 % after 7 days to 4 weeks while total heat production stayed the same.**

    Four weeks of a 10 °C suit for 2 h a day raised BAT volume 45 % and BAT oxidative metabolism 2.2-fold, yet the same protocol cut shivering 21 % "with no net difference in whole-body thermogenesis". Seven days of 1 h immersion at 14 °C cut shivering 36 % "without affecting whole body heat production", blocking BAT lipolysis raised shivering with no change in total heat, and a month of overnight 19 °C raised BAT without altering cold-induced thermogenesis.

    **14. After cold-water immersion, people ate about 230 to 270 kcal more at the next meal, more than the session burned.**

    In 11 men, intake after 45 min of cycling in 20 °C water was 877 ± 457 kcal, 44 % above neutral water, and in 15 adults intake after 30 min at 16 °C was 2,783 against 1,817 kJ, about 231 kcal more. Suit cooling at 10 °C in 47 adults with obesity raised lunch intake 10 % with lower intake later in the day, and 24 h of passive 16 °C air did not change total intake.

    **15. In the only large trial, 30 to 90 seconds of cold at the end of a hot shower cut self-reported sickness absence by 29 % (IRR 0.71, 95 % CI 0.56 to 0.89) in 3,018 adults, but illness days did not change significantly (IRR 0.89, P = 0.073).**

    The trial was randomised but unblinded, with self-reported outcomes, unverified compliance and an estimated water temperature of 10 to 12 °C. The 30, 60 and 90 second arms did not differ (P = 0.992), every arm had a median of zero absence days, and the model gain is about 0.65 days per person over 90 days. A 2025 meta-analysis repeats the 29 % figure, and 3,018 of its 3,177 participants come from this one trial.

    **16. Cold training alone did not change the inflammatory response to injected endotoxin (P =.77); the Wim Hof breathing technique did (P =.002), and cold plus breathing gave the largest effect, with IL-10 +44 % and TNF-α −32 %.**

    In 48 healthy young men given 2 ng/kg LPS after four days of snow and ice-water training, breathing alone, both or nothing, cold "significantly enhanced" the breathing effect (P =.02) and cold alone lowered only MCP-1 (−25 %). The breathing technique roughly doubled adrenaline (0.51 to 1.01 nmol/L), and the 2014 trial that started the claim gave all components together against an untrained control. A metabolomic re-analysis places the mechanism in the breathing-driven lactate and pyruvate rise. Cold alone left adrenaline unchanged in the Prague and Oulu immersion studies.

    **17. A cold-water plunge raises inflammatory markers for at least an hour (pooled SMD 1.03 immediately and 1.26 at one hour), and 10 minutes at 10 °C after resistance exercise did not lower muscle inflammation compared with light cycling.**

    The 2025 meta-analysis describes the rise as "an acute inflammatory response". In nine men with muscle biopsies at 2, 24 and 48 h, neutrophils, macrophages and cytokine mRNA did not differ between cold immersion and active recovery. Reviews from cold-water laboratories find that some claims remain "anecdotal speculation".

    **18. Cold constricts diseased coronary arteries, cutting flow 39 % across stenoses above 50 %, and moderately cold 22 °C water raised ventricular ectopy about fivefold in heart failure.**

    A hand cold pressor test raised flow 65 % in normal coronary arteries and cut it 39 % across advanced stenoses; a 45-s dive raised coronary resistance 44 % in severe stenosis, and −20 °C air provoked angina in 4 of 17 coronary patients at rest. In compensated heart failure, PVCs rose from 15 to 76 per 30 minutes at 22 °C. A supervised sauna-plus-12 °C protocol was tolerated by stable patients, so the trials cover stable, supervised people and not unsupervised near-freezing plunges.

    **19. Each 1 °C fall in outdoor temperature is associated with a 2.0 % rise in heart-attack risk over the next 28 days, concentrated in older and already-ill people, which is weather evidence and not a test of plunging.**

    Across 84,010 MI admissions in England and Wales, the cumulative rise was 2.0 % (95 % CI 1.1 to 2.9 %) per 1 °C, strongest at 2 to 14 days. SWEDEHEART linked a 7.4 °C warmer day to 2.8 % lower risk, a Swedish case-crossover found cold spells raised risk at lag 2-6 days but lowered it at lag 0-1, Chinese cold spells raised onset risk 4.24 % with little effect where homes were centrally heated, and a 23-study meta-analysis gave RR 1.014 per 1 °C.

    **20. Hyperventilation before a cold breath-hold more than doubles breath-hold time, and that is the documented route to silent blackout, with 23 deaths in 58 cases.**

    Breath-hold time on sudden submersion followed 15.01 + 0.92 × water temperature, rising to 38.90 + 1.70 × temperature after habituation and mild hyperventilation. In 58 underwater blackout cases after hyperventilation, 23 died, and the interval from unconsciousness to death "may be no longer than 2.5 minutes". A CDC review found 16 cases of dangerous underwater breath-holding behaviours, with risk rising when more than one was practised, and no PubMed-indexed case links Wim Hof breathing specifically to a drowning.

    **21. Cold water helps when heat is the limit: pre-cooling before exercise in the heat improved endurance performance (g = +0.53), and 15 minutes of cold water between two hot bouts prevented a 4.1 % fall in cycling work.**

    Across 22 crossover studies at ambient temperatures of at least 26 °C, CWI pre-cooling gave g = +0.53 (95 % CI 0.28 to 0.77), while ice slurry showed "no clear effect". Across 28 studies, pre-cooling improved performance by 5.7 % and all cooling by 6.7 %. The benefit was larger in constant-workload than self-paced tests (ES 0.74 against 0.29), and two authors of that analysis advise the cooling-garment maker Inuteq. All three meta-analyses studied mainly or only men. Between two 30-minute heat tasks in 10 trained cyclists, CWI at 10-20 °C kept total work stable, and between 35-minute heat bouts performance held after CWI but fell 1.81 % after active recovery, with rectal temperature 36.8 against 38.3 °C; across trained athletes whole-body immersion (5.1 %) beat limb-only immersion (1.1 %).

    **22. Ten minutes at 10 °C cools the quadriceps 6.40 °C at 1 cm but only 1.61 °C, a non-significant fall, at 3 cm, and body fat changes the dose.**

    Six pooled studies gave falls of 6.40, 4.50 and 1.61 °C at 1, 2 and 3 cm, and body fat percentage predicted rectus femoris cooling in 16 adults (R² = 0.58 to 0.67). Women cooled at about half the rate of men at 18 °C, a difference that disappeared when fatness was matched. The "cooling dose" index of 1.1 (11 minutes at 10 °C) is a muscle-temperature regression, not a recovery dose, from authors linked to the company ProCcare.

    **23. Ten minutes in 8 °C water cooled deep leg muscle by 2.9 °C, against 1.3 °C after two minutes of −110 °C air, so a colder chamber is not a colder muscle.**

    In a crossover of 10 heated men, water also cut femoral artery conductance 84 % against 59 %, while rectal temperature fell 0.6 °C in both. At matched 4-minute exposures in 20 men, air at −110 °C cooled the skin 12.1 °C against 8.4 °C in 8 °C water, but muscle and core fell by a similar small amount. The poor thermal conductivity of air prevents significant subcutaneous and core cooling.


??? note "Tier 3 — Contested or Emerging (Low Confidence) (8 findings)"

    **24. Pooled evidence leans favourable for mood but "does not establish" a benefit: controlled-only analyses attenuate, RCT-only pooling found no mood effect, and cold showers plus Wim Hof breathing matched warm showers plus slow breathing (both −24 % depressive symptoms).**

    A 2026 meta-analysis of 13 studies and 4,105 participants found wide confidence intervals, attenuation in controlled-only analyses and mostly high or critical risk of bias; an RCT-only review of 11 studies and 3,177 participants found better sleep quality and quality of life "but not mood", with one shower trial supplying 3,018 of the participants. In 84 women the three-week randomised trial found the same 24 % fall in depressive symptoms in both arms, "counter to the preregistered predictions"; the positive cryotherapy trial in medicated patients was controlled but not randomised, and the 5-minute 20 °C fMRI study had no control condition. The rest of the depression evidence is a case report, a survey, a self-selected cohort, an uncontrolled 13-person study, a trial protocol and a hypothesis paper, and the Norwegian review of 104 studies is narrative.

    **25. The swimmers' mood and menopause benefits come from self-selected surveys, in which 63.3 % of perimenopausal women swam to relieve the very symptoms they then rated.**

    Of 1,114 women recruited through social media, 30.3 % reported fewer hot flushes, with no comparison group. A meta-analysis of 13 studies with 4,105 people found that estimates shrank in controlled-only analyses and that the evidence "does not establish" a mood benefit. A thermoneutral-water placebo matched cold water for strength and readiness, and WBC sleep gains were small or absent.

    **26. Part of the felt recovery from an ice bath is expectation: 34.7 °C water presented as a recovery treatment was "as effective as CWI" at 10.3 °C in 30 males.**

    After 4 × 30-second sprints, strength and readiness ratings were similar after CWI and the thermoneutral placebo, and worse only after the same water presented as a control. After a marathon in 31 trained males, "cryotherapy is no more effective than a placebo intervention" for functional recovery, and in 24 resistance-trained males neither CWI nor WBC beat placebo overall. Intramuscular inflammation after 10 minutes at 10 °C did not differ from easy cycling in 9 men.

    **27. The "+43 % insulin sensitivity" result came from 8 people with type 2 diabetes in 10 days of mild cold, worked through muscle, and disappeared when shivering was prevented.**

    The gain went with muscle GLUT4 translocation and "only a minor increase in BAT glucose uptake"; the replication with shivering prevented in 9 patients found no improvement, while 1 h of shivering a day for 10 days improved glucose tolerance in 15 adults with overweight. Sixteen daily 10-min plunges at 14 °C temporarily decreased insulin sensitivity, and people with detectable BAT on 52,487 patients' scans had less diabetes and heart disease, an association open to reverse causation.

    **28. Whole-body cryotherapy lowers some inflammatory markers in small trials (11 RCTs, 274 people: lower IL-1β, IL-10 SMD 0.78), and in rheumatic disease it improves pain without a consistent fall in CRP.**

    Daily WBC after a trail run lowered IL-1β and CRP versus passive recovery in 11 men, but TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-10 did not differ. In RA, WBC at −130 °C reduced pain by 1.31 NRS points versus control (95 % CI −2.1 to −0.53), and −110 °C was not superior to local cold for pain. In ankylosing spondylitis, disease-activity scores improved but CRP did not (P = 0.684).

    **29. Brief body-surface chilling was followed by more self-reported colds (13 of 90 versus 5 of 90, P = 0.047), and cold air lowers nasal temperature and weakens antiviral defences in cell studies.**

    The foot-chill trial had no virological testing and rests on 8 extra colds, and its authors frame nasal vasoconstriction as a hypothesis. Rhinovirus replicated more at 33 °C than 37 °C in airway cells, and about 5 °C of cooling cut antiviral vesicle release by 41.9 %. In Finnish conscripts each 1 °C fall in outdoor temperature raised respiratory infection risk by 4.3 %.

    **30. No optimal temperature-duration pair exists: recovery trials span about 5 to 15 °C for 2.5 to 15 minutes, and the meta-analyses disagree on whether colder or shorter is better.**

    The 2016 "10-15 °C" window rests on 9 trials with soreness MD 0.290 on an unnamed scale, while the 44-trial update found that water temperature did "not influence this result". A 52-study meta-regression linked shorter and colder immersion to larger CK effects, and an umbrella review rated certainty "low to very low". A crossover of 10 °C for 2.5 minutes against 15 °C for 11 minutes found different recovery time courses and "minimal differences" in soreness.

    **31. Ice for an acute soft-tissue injury rests on 22 RCTs with a mean PEDro score of 3.4 out of 10, and no human trial shows that ice speeds or harms healing.**

    The review found "marginal evidence that ice plus exercise is most effective", and 11 trials with 868 adults gave insufficient evidence for RICE in ankle sprains. Ice delayed regeneration by about 1 day in crushed rat muscle, and in 11 men icing raised CK-MB and myoglobin at 48 to 72 hours without changing strength. The guideline favours supervised exercise, and PEACE and LOVE is an editorial, not a study.


??? note "Tier 4 — Claims That Fail the Evidence (8 findings)"

    **32. The "+250 % dopamine" figure is one plasma measurement during a 1-hour head-out immersion at 14 °C, and the same laboratory did not reproduce it.**

    In the source study plasma dopamine rose 250 % (a 3.5-fold rise) and noradrenaline 530 % during one hour at 14 °C, with no brain measure, no mood outcome and no statement that levels stayed high for hours. The same Prague group, using the identical 14 °C, 1-hour protocol, found dopamine "not increased significantly" beside a four-fold noradrenaline rise. Navy divers at 5 °C showed "no change in... dopamine", and a cold pressor test produced no significant dopamine change, with basal plasma dopamine below 42 pg/ml in 31 of 41 people.

    **33. "Cold plunges burn fat" fails the evidence: no study has shown weight loss from cold-induced brown-fat activation, and a randomised crossover of three 4-week blocks of post-exercise immersion in 18 rugby players found no change in fat mass (p=0.801).**

    A Pennington and NIH review states that no study has shown a decrease in body weight with cold-induced BAT activation. In 18 rugby players, post-exercise cold-water immersion blocks changed neither lean (p=0.960) nor fat mass (p=0.801), and 16 daily 10-min immersions at 14 °C did not change resting expenditure. The one positive fat-mass result (2 h/day at 17 °C for 6 weeks) gives no magnitude or n in its abstract and has an Ajinomoto-affiliated co-author, and weight responds to small energy gaps only over years and only when they are sustained.

    **34. "11 minutes a week is the minimum effective dose" fails the evidence: 11 minutes is the self-reported mean weekly cold-water time of 7 or 8 Danish winter swimmers, who also took 57 minutes of sauna, in a cross-sectional study that tested no dose.**

    The source compares 8 swimmers with 8 controls, tested with cooling blankets, in which BAT glucose uptake rose similarly in both groups; the swimmers averaged 1.4 minutes per immersion in water between 1 and 9 °C, the figure sits in the full text's Table 1 and not the abstract, and the authors state that self-selection by thermophenotype cannot be excluded. The "500–1,000 kcal/24 h" rise is a 30-minute reading expressed as a daily rate, about 12 to 21 kcal in the 30 minutes; funders included the Novo Nordisk Foundation, and one author consults for Novo Nordisk on BAT biology.

    **35. "Cold boosts the immune system" is not supported by infection data: no study found here measured laboratory-confirmed infections, and pooled immune markers showed no effect immediately (SMD −0.16) or at one hour (SMD −0.18) after immersion.**

    Six weeks of one-hour immersions at 14 °C gave a "slight" immune activation of unknown significance. Twelve weeks of winter swimming or −110 °C cryotherapy left IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α unchanged, and three weeks at 7 °C for 12 minutes gave "no relevant effects" on leukocytes. The pooled immune estimate rests on about 159 people, and infection claims in habitual swimmers were "not directly measured".

    **36. "Cryotherapy chambers beat ice baths" fails: the Cochrane review found 4 RCTs in 64 people with very low quality evidence, a 13-RCT meta-analysis found cold water better for 24-hour soreness (MD 1.07), and after a marathon WBC harmed muscle function compared with cold water.**

    In the Cochrane review the one clear effect, soreness SMD −0.77 at 1 hour, came from 20 people, every 24 to 72 hour interval crossed zero and no trial compared WBC with water; the later meta-analysis found only "a small and fragile effect" of body cryotherapy on 24-hour jump height. Head-to-head data split: pooled air cryotherapy beat CWI for strength recovery and a 57-study network meta-analysis ranked cryotherapy first for soreness (SUCRA 88.3 %), an order and not a size, while the marathon trial favoured water. The positive reading of WBC is a vote count, the consensus that widens WBC to −60 to −195 °C declares authors employed by cryotherapy and industrial-gas companies, and WBC and partial-body cabins give similar effects.

    **37. "Contrast therapy flushes lactic acid" carries two errors: lactate does not cause next-day soreness, and contrast cleared lactate only 0.28 against 0.22 mmol L⁻¹ min⁻¹, about 1.8 mmol/L after 30 minutes.**

    Downhill running produced delayed soreness without any rise in lactic acid, while level running raised lactate without soreness. Contrast lowered lactate 2.1 mmol/L more than jogging in 20 rugby players, but repeated sprints an hour later changed "small to trivial and unclear". Active recovery near the lactate threshold clears lactate fastest, and contrast beats passive rest for soreness but no other active method.

    **38. "Cold boosts testosterone" fails: two hours at 10 °C left testosterone and LH unchanged in 20 men, and 15 minutes at 15 °C after squats left testosterone 10.4 % below baseline at 60 minutes.**

    Mild cold for 2 hours raised noradrenaline but not testosterone, cold water after squats also turned a 9.2 % free-testosterone rise into −0.5 %, and one immersion below 4 °C lowered testosterone in 13 young men within normal limits. The one rise, salivary testosterone +21 to +28 % after WBC in 14 footballers, was not seen in serum in another WBC trial. The fertility version rests on scrotal-cooling devices for subfertile men, with "insufficient evidence" and "a positive trend" across 8 articles.

    **39. "Winter swimming protects the heart" rests on no outcome study: the Finnish HR 0.37 for sudden cardiac death is a sauna result, and the one Wim Hof trial changed no cardiovascular measure.**

    The KIHD cohort of 2,315 men tied 4-7 sauna sessions a week to HR 0.37 (0.18 to 0.75) for sudden cardiac death, with no cold-water exposure in its abstract. Fifteen days of daily Wim Hof Method in 42 people left blood pressure, heart-rate variability and pulse wave velocity unchanged. The supporting data are 10 swimmers against 16 controls with a better ApoB/ApoA1 ratio and narrative reviews that call the question unresolved. In habitual ice swimmers the systolic rise came while waiting in cold air, and immersion added none.


??? note "Practical — what to actually do (6 findings)"

    **40. Measured cognition falls about 14 % in cold of 10 °C or less and can stay impaired 60 minutes after rewarming, so schedule skilled work before, not straight after, a long or very cold exposure.**

    A meta-analysis of 22 studies found a 13.91 % decrement, and a review found impairment in 15 of 18 settings before hypothermia. Working memory and executive function stayed impaired 60 minutes into rewarming in 10 men at 10 °C air, and 15 minutes at −10 °C slowed attention in a 24-person crossover; fast and slow coolers responded alike at 14 °C. Never immerse alone in open water and avoid breath-holding in cold water, where cold shock plus the dive reflex may trigger arrhythmias.

    **41. If muscle growth is your goal, keep cold away from the minutes after lifting; no trial has tested whether a 4-6 hour gap removes the blunting.**

    Every training trial applied cold within minutes, and muscle perfusion stayed lower for 180 minutes after a 20-minute, 8 °C immersion. Satellite-cell responses differed at 24-48 hours after one bout followed by CWI, and over 12 weeks type II fibre area rose 17 % only with active recovery. The umbrella review recommends a "periodised, context-driven approach" rather than cold after every session, as do narrative reviews. A cooled leg made 1.48 against 1.67 %·day⁻¹ of myofibrillar protein over two weeks, so "wait 4 to 6 hours" is an extrapolation, not a tested rule.

    **42. Do not use cold exposure as a fat-loss tool: a 3-minute plunge plausibly adds about 10 to 15 kcal, less than the extra food people ate after immersion.**

    Brown-fat heat is single-digit kcal per day, whole-body cold-induced thermogenesis is about 15 to 25 kcal per 2-h session of mild cold, and 14 °C water raised metabolic rate 350 % for the hour it lasted. Next-meal intake rose about 230 to 270 kcal after immersion; if you plunge for other reasons, brown fat and weight are not a reason to keep or stop.

    **43. If you enjoy cold showers, 30 seconds performed as well as 90 seconds for sick-leave days, but a 15 °C shower does not cool the body the way immersion does, and regular physical activity carried a similar association (IRR 0.65) in the same trial.**

    The 30, 60 and 90 second arms did not differ (P = 0.992), and the physical-activity association was observational while the cold-shower effect was randomised but unblinded. A 15 °C shower for 15 minutes did not lower core temperature after exercise in the heat in 9 men, while 5 minutes of 14.6 °C immersion lowered rectal temperature 1.0 °C at 60 minutes. For healthy users the immune case alone is weak, so warm up afterwards rather than staying chilled; cold-and-breathing programmes in spondyloarthritis or multiple sclerosis were small add-on trials, not evidence for healthy adults.

    **44. Neither a morning-only nor a fasted-only rule has support: cold-induced thermogenesis was 16.8 % at 08:00 and 15.7 % at 18:00, and evening post-exercise immersion left whole-night sleep unchanged.**

    The time-of-day crossover in 14 adults found no difference (P = 0.72). Brief cold during a two-day fast had "no effect on REE" in 12 women. After evening exercise, CWI did not change any whole-night sleep measure in 11 cyclists, and head-in whole-body CWI at 13.3 °C increased early-night slow-wave sleep against waist-deep immersion in 12 runners.

    **45. Before regular plunging, people with cold urticaria, coronary disease, heart failure, a channelopathy or unexplained fainting need a medical assessment, because 37 % of test-positive cold-urticaria patients had anaphylaxis, most often from full immersion.**

    In COLD-CE, 151 of 412 test-positive patients had cold-induced anaphylaxis, "most commonly provoked by complete cold water immersion". Healthy users can enter slowly, keep the head out and kick at entry, which offset the fall in cerebral blood flow, and should not trust comfort, because core temperature did not track thermal sensation. Cryotherapy chambers carry few documented harms but a formal contraindication list, with frostbite after single sessions. Other conditions with records are past non-freezing cold injury, swimming-induced pulmonary oedema and Raynaud's, and pregnancy has no data. A burn-centre series reports three frostbite cases after single liquid-nitrogen sessions, one needing grafts, but no source counts sessions, so the risk per session is unknown.
