# Electrolytes & Morning Hydration: What They Actually Do vs What People Think

> Hypotonic salt water with a small, real magnesium top-up, sold on a rationale the physiology does not support. A 24-hour fast lowers urinary sodium rather than raising it; the sodium J-curve comes from spot-urine formulas that over-estimate intake by 1,297 mg a day; neither salt nor magnesium prevents cramps; a temperate hour of exercise costs half a gram of sodium; and "hydrates better than water" means an oral rehydration solution left 300 mL less urine from a litre you did not need.

*Literature reviewed to 1 November 2026. 341 sources, every identifier resolved against PubMed.*

## Start here

!!! quote "Bottom line"
    **A fasted morning electrolyte drink is hypotonic salt water with a small, real magnesium top-up, sold on a rationale the physiology does not support: a 24-hour fast lowers urinary sodium rather than raising it, concentrated morning urine is the kidney working rather than failing, the sodium "J-curve" behind the high-salt fad comes from spot-urine formulas that over-estimate intake by 1,297 mg a day, and neither salt nor magnesium prevents cramps.**

**Myths vs. reality**

| The claim you've heard | What the evidence actually shows |
|---|---|
| "You wake up dehydrated and sodium-depleted" | A full 24-hour fast in healthy men lowered urinary sodium and chloride and raised aldosterone. Varying water intake between 1.0 and 2.5 L a day moved urine concentration but not plasma osmolality, and an extra 716 mL a day for a week left morning urine unchanged. Overnight urine carries about 25 to 50 mmol of sodium as disposal of yesterday's intake, not a deficit. |
| "Fasting makes you lose sodium, so replace it before you eat" | The natriuresis of fasting is cation cover for ketoacid anions: chronic fasting excretes 120 mmol of them a day at a blood level of 5.5 mmol/L, while three weeks at 5 % carbohydrate raised ketones only 0.62 mmol/L. An overnight fast after dinner sits below even that, and the effect self-limits by day four. |
| "The sodium guidelines are wrong; 4 to 6 g a day is optimal" | No cohort found 4 to 6 g optimal (PURE said 3 to 6, INTERSTROKE 2.8 to 3.5). Every J-curve cohort estimated sodium from one spot urine through formulas that correlate with true intake at r = 0.50; in the same people with three to seven 24-hour urines the formula bent a linear mortality relationship into a J and still predicted death with sodium held constant. Measured properly, each extra 1,000 mg carried HR 1.18. |
| "Salt prevents cramps" | Losing 3 to 4.7 % of body mass and 3 to 4 g of sodium did not change the electrically induced cramp threshold; a nerve block abolished the cramp; 210 Ironman triathletes who cramped matched those who did not on every electrolyte; pickle juice relieved cramps in about a minute with plasma unchanged. |
| "Magnesium prevents cramps" | The Cochrane pool for nocturnal cramps is −0.18 cramps a week with a confidence interval spanning zero, intravenous 5 g a day was null, and there is no trial at all in exercise cramps. A sachet's 60 to 120 mg is below every dose that failed. |
| "You sweat out a lot of sodium and magnesium" | Whole-body sweat sodium averages the mid-30s mmol/L; a temperate hour of exercise costs about half a gram of sodium and 1.3 mg of magnesium. A sedentary day loses a few tenths of a gram of sodium through the skin at most, and heat acclimation cuts sweat sodium 40 % within a week. |
| "Sodium supplements prevent hyponatremia in sport" | In 413 Ironman finishers randomised to an extra 3.6 g of sodium, placebo or nothing, serum sodium did not differ. Exercise-associated hyponatremia is caused by drinking more than you sweat over four hours or more; at Boston 2002, weight gain (OR 4.2) and finishing over four hours (OR 7.4) predicted it and fluid type did not. |
| "Electrolyte water hydrates better than water" | Oral rehydration solution left 300 mL less urine from an unneeded litre over four hours; a sports drink equalled water, and a sugar-free electrolyte drink gave a hydration index of 0.99 to 1.01. The effect needs 27 to 52 mmol/L of sodium, has poor within-person reproducibility, and no trial has a health outcome. |
| "Electrolytes hydrate your cells" | Sodium raises extracellular osmolality and pulls water out of cells. The documented effect of a sodium drink is reduced urine output, about 0.31 L held in the extracellular fluid per 43.5 mmol of sodium. |
| "Sodium needs glucose to be absorbed" | In human perfusion the ileum absorbed sodium actively against a 110 mEq/L gradient with no stimulation by glucose. Glucose accelerates uptake in the jejunum, which is why oral rehydration solution works in cholera; a glucose-free salt drink is absorbed lower down and later. |
| "The potassium balances the sodium" | A 200 mg sachet is 5 mmol, 6 % of the WHO target and half a banana. A 1,000/200 mg stick raises a typical day's sodium-to-potassium ratio from 2.85 to 3.33; the salt substitute that cut death 12 % in a randomised trial lowered that ratio. |
| "Water first thing in the morning boosts metabolism and flushes toxins" | The metabolism claim rests on one 14-person study worth about 24 kcal per 500 mL that a better-controlled replication did not reproduce. No study tests a toxin-flushing effect. Extra water dilutes urine. |

**What you actually learn**

- **Sodium balance runs on days to weeks:** total-body sodium swings ±200 to 400 mmol at fixed intake without moving water, a single 24-hour urine classifies a 3 g salt difference only 49 % of the time, and a 43.5 mmol stick sits inside the ±25 mmol day-to-day noise of excretion.
- **The measurement decides the sodium debate:** the Kawasaki formula over-estimated intake by 1,297 mg a day, over-estimating at low and under-estimating at high intake, which is exactly the bias that manufactures a J-curve; with three to seven real collections the relationship is linear.
- **The randomised cost of 1,000 mg a day of sodium is about 1 to 2.5 mmHg systolic** in a normotensive adult, two to three times that with age, hypertension or higher baseline pressure, and the sodium-pressure slope shows no flattening anywhere between 0.4 and 7.6 g a day.
- **Potassium is the Western shortfall:** US adults excrete 2,155 mg a day against a 3,510 mg target, only 0.002 % of a 103,570-person cohort met both WHO targets, and each extra 1,000 mg tracks 18 % fewer cardiovascular events; the pressure effect of supplements belongs to hypertensives at 60 to 120 mmol a day.
- **Cramps are spinal:** the generator sits in the alpha-motor-neuron pool, prior cramps, race pace, muscle damage, sex, age and injury predict them, and the one real electrolyte effect is sodium-containing fluid drunk during hot, dehydrating exercise, which raised the cramp threshold while plain water lowered it.
- **Replacement during exercise has a corner:** duration over 90 minutes, heat or sweat rates at or above 1.5 L/h, whole-body sweat sodium at or above 40 mmol/L, and fluid replacement over 80 %. Outside it, a model found sodium unnecessary in every realistic soccer or marathon scenario, and drinking to thirst matched programmed drinking.
- **The 2 % dehydration rule holds for fixed-workload laboratory tasks**, not for self-paced exercise up to 3 to 4 % in trained people, including when the dehydration is blinded.
- **Magnesium form is "oxide versus anything soluble":** oxide is 4 % absorbed and indistinguishable from placebo; citrate, chloride, lactate and aspartate are not separated by human data; glycinate has one trial in 12 bowel-resection patients; malate's superiority is a rat finding. Dose dominates, from 65 % absorbed at low doses to 11 % at high.
- **Mild dehydration moves mood reliably and cognition weakly:** 1 to 2 % loss worsens mood, headache and perceived effort; measured cognition pools to −0.14 with a confidence interval touching zero at or below 2 %, and a 4 % loss left cognition intact.
- **Coffee counts:** four cups a day left total body water and urine volume unchanged in habitual drinkers; about 300 mg of caffeine adds 109 mL of urine at rest and nothing during exercise.

!!! tip "What this means for you"
    - **Drop the overnight-repletion rationale:** nothing in your morning urine or thirst tells you that you are depleted, and the sodium in the first void is last night's dinner leaving.
    - **If you keep the drink, mix it in at least 500 mL:** a 1,000 mg sodium stick in 500 mL is about 200 mOsm/kg and absorbs fine; in 250 mL it is hypertonic and pulls fluid into the gut until diluted.
    - **Count the sodium:** 1,000 mg adds 28 % to a 9 g salt day and takes a typical intake to twice the 2,300 mg chronic disease risk reduction intake; if your pressure, age or family history argue for care, this is the line to cut first.
    - **Take sodium during the session, not before it:** on long, hot, salty training days replace it as you go; on a rest day the kidney excretes the stick over the day.
    - **Buy potassium as food:** one or two servings of potatoes, beans, greens, dairy or fruit do what the 200 mg in the stick cannot, and a potassium-enriched salt on the table is the move the outcome trials tested.
    - **Keep the magnesium, as a top-up:** 60 to 120 mg of a soluble salt roughly closes the median dietary gap; do not stack it with a 300 to 400 mg capsule without a serum check, and avoid oxide.
    - **Treat added potassium and sodium as drugs if you have kidney disease, take an ACE inhibitor or ARB with spironolactone or eplerenone, or have heart failure:** these groups were excluded from every salt-substitute trial and are where 40 mmol a day caused hyperkalaemia.
    - **Never gain weight during exercise:** exercise-associated hyponatremia comes from drinking beyond sweat loss, and the fatal mistake is treating the confused, vomiting athlete as dehydrated and giving more fluid.
    - **Do not expect cramp protection:** stretch to stop a cramp, condition to prevent one, and if you train hot and long, drink sodium-containing fluid during the session.

## 1. Executive Summary and the Chain of Claims

A fasted morning electrolyte drink is sold on a chain of six claims. First, that you wake up dehydrated and sodium-depleted after an eight-hour fast, because fasting makes the kidney dump sodium and falling insulin stops it being retained. Second, that the sodium guidelines are wrong and 4 to 6 g a day is the real optimum, so a 1,000 mg sachet is corrective rather than excessive. Third, that salt and potassium prevent muscle cramps. Fourth, that you sweat out so much sodium and magnesium that they must be replaced. Fifth, that electrolyte water "hydrates better than water" through "cellular transport technology". Sixth, that the potassium and magnesium in the stick fix a deficiency your blood test cannot see. This synthesis tests each link against 341 PubMed-verified records drawn from seven evidence sweeps: sodium and cardiovascular outcomes, potassium and the sodium-to-potassium ratio, fasting and overnight renal physiology, sweat and exercise-associated hyponatremia, exercise-associated muscle cramps, magnesium and fluid compartments, and formulations and general hydration claims.

The short version has six parts.

**The overnight-deficit premise is false.** A full 24-hour fast in healthy men on a controlled diet lowered urinary sodium and chloride and raised urinary aldosterone [^S113]. The natriuresis of fasting is real, but it is obligatory cation cover for ketoacid anions, with sodium loss tracking anion excretion at r = 0.891 [^S100]; chronic fasting excretes 120 mmol/day of ketoacid anions at a blood concentration of 5.5 mmol/L [^S107], while three weeks at 5 % of energy from carbohydrate raised beta-hydroxybutyrate by 0.62 mmol/L [^S126]. An overnight fast in someone who ate dinner is below even that, and the natriuresis self-limits by day 4 of continued carbohydrate withdrawal [^S106]. Morning urine is concentrated because copeptin peaks at 04:00 to 06:00 [^S125]; varying water intake by 1.5 L/day moved urine but not plasma osmolality [^S120], an extra 716 mL/day for a week left morning urine unchanged [^S130], and first-morning urine does not track plasma osmolality [^S134]. Sodium balance is settled over days to weeks, with total-body sodium swinging ±200 to 400 mmol at fixed intake [^S121] and a single 24-h urine classifying a 3 g salt difference only 49 % of the time [^S122].

**The "guidelines are wrong" claim rests on a measurement artefact.** Every cohort that found a J-shaped sodium-mortality curve estimated sodium from a single spot urine through a formula whose individual-level correlation with true 24-h sodium is 0.50 [^S8] and whose validators call it invalid for individuals [^S18] [^S37]. In 2,974 people with three to seven 24-h urines, the Kawasaki formula over-estimated intake by 1,297 mg/day, over-estimating at low and under-estimating at high intake, so that measured sodium was linear with mortality while the same urines through the formula looked J-shaped [^S21]; all three formulas did it, and they still predicted death with urinary sodium concentration held constant [^S27]. Measured properly in 10,709 adults, each extra 1,000 mg/day of sodium carried HR 1.18 and each 1,000 mg of potassium HR 0.82, with no J [^S40]. No cohort found 4 to 6 g optimal: PURE said 3 to 6 g [^S14], INTERSTROKE 2.8 to 3.5 g [^S34], and Graudal's flat zone was roughly 2.6 to 4.9 g [^S11]. No trial has ever raised sodium against hard outcomes; the one large trial that lowered sodium while raising potassium cut all-cause death 12 % [^S35].

**Cramps are not a salt problem.** Losing 3 % of body mass and 145 mmol of sodium did not change the electrically induced cramp threshold [^S216], nor did 4.7 % and 4 g of sodium [^S224]; a nerve block abolished the cramp [^S219]; 210 Ironman triathletes who cramped did not differ from those who did not in any electrolyte or body-weight measure [^S220]; pickle juice relieved cramps in about a minute with plasma unchanged [^S217]. Magnesium is null for nocturnal cramps in the Cochrane pool (−0.18 cramps/week) and has no trial at all in exercise cramps [^S239]. The one place salt keeps a foothold is sodium-containing fluid drunk during or right after hot, dehydrating exercise, which raised the cramp threshold while plain water lowered it [^S237] [^S244].

**Sweat sodium is proportional to litres, concentration, hours and how much you drink back, and a sedentary day loses almost none.** Whole-body sweat sodium averages the mid-30s mmol/L [^S173]; a temperate hour of exercise costs about 0.5 g of sodium [^S195]; sweat magnesium is 1.27 mg/L, so a hard session loses about 3 mg [^S269]. Balance studies recovering 86 to 92 % of intake in urine bound skin loss at a few tenths of a gram a day [^S139] [^S122]. Sodium supplements did not change serum sodium in 413 randomised Ironman finishers [^S149] and did not improve performance in controlled trials [^S161] [^S168] [^S194]; a model found replacement unnecessary in every realistic soccer or marathon scenario [^S193]. Exercise-associated hyponatremia is caused by drinking more than you sweat over four or more hours [^S147] [^S148].

**"Hydrates better than water" means less diuresis of a litre you did not need.** Oral rehydration solution left 1,038 g of urine over 4 h against 1,337 g for water, while a sports drink equalled water [^S282]; the effect needs 27 to 52 mmol/L of sodium [^S333], a sugar-free electrolyte drink performed like water with within-person reproducibility of 0.11 to 0.31 [^S341], and no trial has a health outcome. Sodium absorption does not need glucose [^S300]; the glucose-sodium cotransport story is why ORS cuts intravenous drips in children with diarrhoea [^S307]. Sodium moves water out of cells, not in [^S265]; 43.5 mmol holds about 0.31 L in the extracellular fluid.

**The potassium and magnesium are small, real top-ups, and potassium is the actual Western deficit.** US adults excrete 3,608 mg of sodium and 2,155 mg of potassium a day [^S20] against a potassium target of 3,510 mg [^S59]; a 200 mg sachet is 6 % of it and half a banana, and a 1,000/200 mg stick raises the day's sodium-to-potassium ratio by about 17 %, the variable three gold-standard analyses find most predictive [^S4] [^S15] [^S40]. Sixty to 120 mg of magnesium roughly matches the median dietary gap [^S262], is at the efficient end of the absorption curve [^S255] and is far below the laxative range [^S290], but it is a fifth to a third of the doses that moved anything in trials.

**The morning sachet.** About 1,000 mg of sodium, 200 mg of potassium and 60 mg of magnesium in 500 mL of water (label values, not PubMed-verifiable). Dissolved in 500 mL it is hypotonic salty water that absorbs fine; it adds 28 % to a 9 g salt day and takes a typical US intake to twice the chronic disease risk reduction intake [^S28]; its randomised blood-pressure cost is about 1 to 2.5 mmHg systolic [^S32] [^S9]; it replaces no overnight loss and prevents no cramp. Its magnesium is a reasonable top-up and its potassium is noise. The money is better spent on potassium-dense food and, on the hot, long, salty training days where sodium matters, on sodium during the session.

## 2. Sodium Intake, Blood Pressure and Cardiovascular Mortality

### 2.1 Where the guidelines come from

The sodium guidelines rest on randomised trials with blood pressure as the endpoint plus the inference that pressure translates into events; the AHA science advisory says so explicitly, and notes that until well-designed general-population cohorts exist it is appropriate to base guidance on the sodium-pressure evidence [^S10]. DASH-Sodium fed 412 adults a control or DASH diet at 150, 100 and 50 mmol/day of sodium for 30 days each: on the control diet the step from 150 to 100 mmol lowered systolic pressure 2.1 mmHg and the step from 100 to 50 a further 4.6 mmHg, so the largest increment came below 100 mmol (2,300 mg); inside the potassium-rich DASH diet the same steps gave 1.3 and 1.7 mmHg [^S2]. Among non-hypertensive participants the sodium effect was 7.0/3.8 mmHg above age 45 and 3.7/1.5 at 45 or younger [^S3], and across baseline systolic strata of <130, 130 to 139, 140 to 149 and ≥150 mmHg the high-to-low sodium change gave −3.20, −8.56, −8.99 and −7.04 mmHg [^S19]. The relevant number for a healthy normotensive adult is the lowest stratum, about 3 mmHg from a large sodium change.

Eighty-five trials spanning 0.4 to 7.6 g/day with 24-h urinary sodium fitted an approximately linear sodium-pressure relationship with no flattening at either end [^S33]; 133 trials in 12,197 participants gave 4.26 mmHg systolic for a 130 mmol reduction, 1.10 mmHg per 50 mmol, and showed trials under 15 days giving 1.05 mmHg per 50 mmol against 2.13 in longer trials [^S32]. The Cochrane review restricted to modest reductions sustained 4 weeks or more gave −4.18/−2.06 mmHg for a 75 mmol reduction, −5.39/−2.82 in hypertensives and −2.42/−1.00 in normotensives, with a small renin and aldosterone rise and no significant change in cholesterol, LDL, HDL or triglycerides [^S9]. The TOHP trials randomised 2,382 overweight adults with high-normal pressure to a sodium target of 80 mmol/day: pressure fell 2.9/1.6 mmHg at 6 months and 1.2/0.7 at 36 months as adherence decayed, yet incident hypertension through 48 months fell by about a fifth [^S1]. In the 2014 NHANES sample with 24-h urines, each 1,000 mg of usual sodium excretion went with +4.58 mmHg systolic and each 1,000 mg of potassium with −3.72, and the top sodium quartile carried hypertension odds of 4.22 [^S22]; that cross-sectional slope is roughly four times the randomised slope because it compares different people. The 2017 ACC/AHA guideline sets an optimal goal below 1,500 mg/day and at least a 1,000 mg/day reduction in most adults [^S25], the 2019 primary-prevention guideline carries sodium reduction as dietary policy [^S26], and the 2019 NASEM report set an adult adequate intake of 1,500 mg and a chronic disease risk reduction intake of 2,300 mg, replacing the old tolerable upper level and meaning "reduce if above", not "harm begins here" [^S28]; the AHRQ evidence review that fed it screened 15,912 citations [^S24].

### 2.2 The randomised hard-outcome evidence

SSaSS is the only large trial here with cardiovascular endpoints: 600 rural Chinese villages, 20,995 participants with prior stroke or aged 60 or more with hypertension, randomised to a 75 % NaCl / 25 % KCl substitute or regular salt for 4.74 years. Stroke ran 29.14 vs 33.65 per 1,000 person-years (RR 0.86), major cardiovascular events 49.09 vs 56.29 (RR 0.87), all-cause death 39.28 vs 44.61 (RR 0.88), and hyperkalaemia-attributed serious adverse events 3.35 vs 3.30 [^S35]. It is a combined intervention: the pooled salt-substitute literature of 21 trials and 31,949 participants gives systolic −4.61 mmHg, total mortality RR 0.89 and a further −1.53 mmHg for each 10 % lower NaCl fraction, consistent with the sodium side carrying part of the effect without isolating it [^S41]. SODIUM-HF is the null counter-example: 806 ambulatory heart-failure patients randomised to under 1,500 mg/day achieved only 1,658 vs 2,073 mg/day and returned HR 0.89 (0.63 to 1.26) for the primary composite with 39 deaths [^S39]; both camps quote it and neither should. Among the 3,126 TOHP participants randomised to sodium reduction, 24-year mortality gave HR 0.85 (0.66 to 1.09) [^S15], the randomised mortality signal in a general-ish population, directionally favourable and not significant.

### 2.3 The J-curve cohorts

PURE estimated 24-h sodium from a single fasting morning urine in 101,945 people across 17 countries and followed them 3.7 years: against 4.00 to 5.99 g/day, intake at or above 7 g carried OR 1.15 and below 3 g OR 1.27, and the stated conclusion was that 3 to 6 g associated with the lowest risk [^S14]. The same cohort's pressure companion found +2.11 mmHg systolic per gram of estimated sodium, steeper above 5 g/day (2.58), in hypertensives (2.49) and with age (2.97 above 55), positive at every level [^S13]. ONTARGET/TRANSCEND, 28,880 patients with established vascular disease or diabetes, gave cardiovascular death HR 1.66 above 8 g and 1.37 below 2 g [^S6], a secondary-prevention population where reverse causation is most plausible. The pooled analysis of four cohorts (133,118 people) found high sodium harmful only in hypertensives (HR 1.23 at or above 7 g) and low sodium associated with risk in both groups (HR 1.34 and 1.26 below 3 g), with nothing between 3 and 7 g and no reason to move from 3.6 to 4.6 g [^S17]. The community-level analysis of 369 communities, an ecological design, found sodium associated with events only where community means exceeded 5 g/day, on a non-significant top-tertile estimate (P = 0.0712), while all major outcomes fell with rising potassium in every country [^S23]. The joint sodium-potassium analysis of 103,570 people found only 0.002 % met both WHO targets, lowest risk at 3 to 5 g of sodium with above-median potassium, and potassium attenuating the sodium risk [^S29]. INTERSTROKE, 9,275 first strokes and 9,726 controls, placed lowest risk at 2.8 to 3.5 g/day with OR 1.81 above 4.26 g, strongest for intracerebral haemorrhage, and potassium linearly protective [^S34]. The one J-curve-adjacent cohort with real 24-h collections, 3,681 Flemish and European participants, found cardiovascular deaths falling across rising sodium tertiles (50, 24, 10 deaths) on a single baseline urine, with total mortality, the composite endpoint and incident hypertension all null [^S7]. Graudal's meta-analysis of 23 cohorts gave usual-versus-low sodium mortality HR 0.91 and high-versus-usual 1.16, with the "usual" band spanning 115 to 215 mmol (about 2.6 to 4.9 g) and the statement that no RCTs in healthy populations were identified [^S11].

### 2.4 The measurement-method controversy

He and colleagues assessed sodium four ways in 2,974 TOHP participants followed 24 years: the mean of three to seven 24-h urines (3,769 mg/day), the same urines through the Kawasaki formula, and single first collections of each. The formula over-estimated by 1,297 mg/day (95 % CI 1,267 to 1,326), over-estimating at low and under-estimating at high intake; measured sodium was linear with mortality, estimated sodium J-shaped, and single collections flattened the relationship [^S21]. The follow-up ran Kawasaki, Tanaka and INTERSALT in the same cohort: all three bent the line into a J or U, and with urinary sodium concentration held constant the formulas still produced values inversely related to mortality, so "the formulas per se seem to be related to mortality independent of sodium" [^S27]. Independent validation in 554 MESA and CARDIA participants found spot-urine bias from −182 to +1,090 mg/day and concluded that "a single spot urine sample is not a valid indicator of individual sodium intake" [^S18]; the INTERSALT formula's own validation in 5,693 people put the individual correlation at 0.50 to 0.51 and recommended casual urine for population estimation only [^S8]; in 323 Tibetan adults the formulas misclassified 48 to 76 % of individuals [^S37]. Dietary self-report has the parallel problem, under-estimating sodium by 0 to 15 % and the sodium-potassium ratio by about 20 % against 24-h urine [^S12]. The AHA audit of 26 cohort studies found systematic error in sodium assessment in 22 and reverse causality in 16, with findings of 13 direct, 8 inverse, 2 J-shaped and 8 null associations whose direction tracked the method [^S10].

Measured properly, the picture changes. Six prospective cohorts with at least two 24-h urines per person, 10,709 generally healthy adults with 571 events over 8.8 years and a median sodium of 3,270 mg/day, gave quartile 4 versus 1 HR 1.60 for sodium, 0.69 for potassium and 1.62 for the ratio, and per 1,000 mg/day HR 1.18 for sodium and 0.82 for potassium, with a dose-response and no J [^S40]. In the TOHP observational arm with 24 years of follow-up the categories gave HRs 0.75, 0.95, 1.00 and 1.07 (p trend 0.30) and 1.12 per 1,000 mg (1.00 to 1.26, P = 0.05), with "no evidence of a J-shaped or nonlinear relationship" and a ratio HR of 1.13 per unit [^S15]; the earlier TOHP analysis found sodium and potassium trends non-significant alone while the sodium-potassium ratio was significant (RR 1.24 per unit) with the best model fit [^S4].

### 2.5 Hormones, lipids and salt sensitivity

The 2020 Cochrane update by Graudal pooled 195 references at a reduction from 203 to 65 mmol/day: systolic −1.14 mmHg in white normotensives and −5.71 in hypertensives, with renin +1.56 ng/mL/h, aldosterone +104 pg/mL, noradrenaline +62 pg/mL, cholesterol +5.19 mg/dL and triglycerides +7.10 mg/dL, and the conclusion that the side-effect signals were more consistent than the pressure effect in normotensives [^S31]. He's review answers that pooling very short, very large reductions is irrelevant to a sustained modest reduction, and restricted to 4 weeks or more finds no lipid change [^S9]; the duration analysis showing short trials deliver under half the pressure effect adjudicates the dispute in He's favour on the pressure side [^S32]. A mechanistic review reads the same hormone axis the other way, linking salt excess to RAAS and sympathetic activation, oxidative stress and insulin resistance [^S5]; whether the renin and aldosterone rise on restriction is benign homeostasis or a durable harm has no hard-outcome answer. Salt sensitivity is pathophysiologically real, associated with organ damage and events, but continuous, categorised by convention (>10 % sensitive, <5 % resistant), measurable only by multi-week dietary protocols with poor compliance, and "not helpful, so far, to the practising physician" [^S16]; the modifiers that can be measured, age, baseline pressure and ethnicity, all steepen the slope [^S32] [^S3] [^S19] [^S31].

### 2.6 What people actually eat

The best US figure uses 24-h urines in a nationally representative sample: 827 NHANES 2014 participants excreted a mean 3,608 mg of sodium (men 4,205, women 3,039) and 2,155 mg of potassium (men 2,399, women 1,922) [^S20]. Subtracting the Kawasaki over-estimate of 1,297 mg from PURE's estimated mean of 4,930 mg gives 3,633 mg, almost exactly the measured US mean. A global audit found 96 national salt-reduction initiatives in 2019, with 17 countries reporting measured falls and none yet meeting the 30 % target [^S36]. A 2022 polemic argues that the J-curve literature serves food-industry interests and should face tighter peer review, from an author who discloses WHO, salt-advocacy and device-company relationships [^S38]; read it as a position paper, since the PURE papers list public funders and the Graudal review carries an explicit no-industry statement. The methodological question is settled by the TOHP experiments, not by anyone's disclosures.

## 3. Potassium and the Sodium-to-Potassium Ratio

### 3.1 The ratio versus absolute sodium

INTERSALT, 10,079 people in 52 centres with one 24-h urine each, found potassium inversely related to pressure within centres and the sodium-to-potassium ratio following "a pattern similar to that of sodium" [^S44]; it does not show the ratio beating sodium. In TOHP the ratio was the one significant predictor of 193 cardiovascular events (RR 1.24 per unit, lowest BIC) while sodium (RR 1.42 per 100 mmol, P = .05) and potassium (0.67 per 50 mmol, P = .12) were not [^S4]. In NHANES III with dietary recall, sodium carried HR 1.20 and potassium 0.80 per 1,000 mg for all-cause mortality, and the top ratio quartile HR 1.46 for all-cause and cardiovascular death and 2.15 for ischaemic heart disease [^S57]. In the pooled 24-h-urine cohorts the ratio (HR 1.62) was no better than sodium alone (1.60) [^S40]. In PREVEND, 5,511 normotensive Dutch adults with four 24-h urines, the lowest potassium tertile carried HR 1.20 for incident hypertension while "no association was found between the sodium to potassium excretion ratio and risk of hypertension" [^S63]. In NHANES 2014 each 0.5-unit rise in the ratio went with +1.72 mmHg systolic [^S22]. Two reviews argue the ratio is the stronger predictor in hypertensives and unclear in normotensives, one authored by consulting-firm employees [^S64], the other promoting casual-urine ratio monitoring with device-company ties [^S73]; NASEM named the ratio a key evidence gap and no trial has targeted it [^S79]; a nutrition review holds that the ratio is more strongly related to risk than either nutrient and that potatoes are the top US potassium source [^S62]. The fair statement is that sodium and potassium both matter in opposite directions and the ratio is a useful single number whose primacy depends on the cohort.

### 3.2 Potassium supplementation and blood pressure

Thirty-three trials gave −3.11/−1.97 mmHg, enhanced by high sodium intake [^S48]. The Cochrane review of six trials of 8 weeks or more in hypertensives found −11.2 mmHg (CI −25.2 to 2.7), not significant and inconclusive [^S52]. The WHO evidence review of 22 trials and 11 cohorts gave −3.49/−1.96 mmHg in hypertensives only, −7.16 systolic at 90 to 120 mmol/day without dose-response, no harm to renal function, lipids or catecholamines, and stroke RR 0.76 in cohorts [^S60]. Fifteen trials in unmedicated people gave −4.7/−3.5 overall and −6.8/−4.6 in hypertensives, with 60 to 100 mmol/day of excretion and a lower ratio needed to explain the effect, from authors at a food company's research centre [^S65]; hypertensive trials of 4 weeks or more gave −4.48/−2.96, larger with baseline potassium under 90 mmol/day, sodium at or above 4 g/day and no drugs [^S72], and 23 placebo-controlled trials gave −4.25/−2.53 [^S75]. The dose-response across 32 trials is U-shaped: the pressure fall weakens above a 30 mmol/day active-control difference and pressure rises above about 80 mmol/day in drug-treated hypertensives, with limited trials at high intake [^S30]. In people who are not hypertensive, 36 untreated prehypertensives on a controlled low-sodium, low-potassium diet saw 24-h pressure fall 3.9/1.6 mmHg from 3 g/day of extra potassium while 3 g/day of extra sodium raised it 7.5/2.7 [^S66]; 42 mild hypertensives saw no office-pressure difference between KCl and KHCO3 at 122 to 125 mmol/day, though both improved flow-mediated dilatation, pulse-wave velocity and left-ventricular mass and only the bicarbonate lowered urinary calcium [^S54]; and 35 healthy normotensive adults given 0 to 60 mEq extra as potato or gluconate over nine 5-day periods showed no pressure change at any dose [^S70]. The DASH diet, which roughly doubles potassium alongside magnesium, calcium and fibre at constant sodium, lowered pressure 5.5/3.0 overall and 11.4/5.5 in hypertensives [^S47]. A 200 to 500 mg sachet is 5 to 13 mmol; no trial has tested such a dose, and the dose-response says the first 30 mmol carry most of the benefit.

### 3.3 Guidelines, intake and the 99 mg question

The WHO 2012 guideline, whose evidence base is the Aburto review, conditionally recommends at least 90 mmol (3,510 mg) a day [^S59]. The 2019 NASEM report revised the adequate intake down from 4,700 mg to 3,400 mg for men and 2,600 mg for women, set no upper level and no chronic disease intake for potassium, and noted that blood electrolyte concentrations are usually not influenced by intake because kidneys and hormones regulate them [^S28]. Against the old intake, under 2 % of US adults qualified while 90.7 % exceeded 2,300 mg of sodium [^S58]; against the 2019 intakes, 36.0 % of men and 38.1 % of women aged 50 and over met them and fewer than a third had a mass ratio under 1.0 [^S83]. PREVEND's median excretion of 70 mmol/day converts to about 91 mmol of intake at 77 % recovery [^S63]; PURE's estimated mean was 2.12 g/day across 18 countries [^S14]. Extracellular potassium is 2 % of the body's total, and the physiology was tuned to a high-potassium, low-sodium diet [^S74].

Potato potassium and potassium gluconate were equally bioavailable (>94 %), with higher urinary recovery from potato [^S70]; the differences lie in the anion. Bicarbonate and citrate salts lowered urinary calcium, net acid excretion and a bone-resorption marker with no effect on formation markers or bone density, and did more than chloride [^S67], but two years of potassium citrate in 276 postmenopausal women changed neither turnover nor density [^S53]. Potassium citrate at 30 to 60 mEq/day cut stone recurrence from 1.2 to 0.1 per patient-year in hypocitraturic formers [^S45] and pools to RR 0.26 with adverse-event dropouts RR 4.45 [^S68]; this is a prescription treatment that chloride does not share. The US rule that keeps over-the-counter potassium under 100 mg per solid dose followed small-bowel ulceration [^S42] and perforation from slow-release KCl [^S43], with stenoses and perforations in French pharmacovigilance [^S46], a dosage-form hazard of concentrated tablets against mucosa that says nothing about dissolved potassium in water. Forty mmol of potassium citrate or chloride taken fasted raised plasma potassium similarly over 4 hours, with citrate driving more red-cell uptake and a higher transtubular gradient, and lisinopril blunting the citrate effect [^S84].

### 3.4 Stroke, mortality and the hard-outcome trials

Across 247,510 participants and 7,066 strokes, 1.64 g/day more potassium tracked RR 0.79 [^S55]; across 268,276 participants RR 0.89 per 1,000 mg, with no protection against haemorrhagic stroke [^S56]; across 16 cohorts the spline nadir sat at 90 mmol/day (RR 0.78), which is why the WHO number is 3.5 g [^S71]. SSaSS, above, is the flagship [^S35]. DECIDE-Salt randomised 48 elderly-care facilities: the substitute gave −7.1/−1.9 mmHg, cardiovascular events HR 0.60 and more biochemical hyperkalaemia without clinical harm, while restricting the salt supply did nothing [^S85]; Taiwanese veterans' homes gave cardiovascular mortality HR 0.59 [^S51]. The Cochrane pool of 26 trials and 34,961 adults gives −4.76/−2.43 mmHg, serum potassium +0.12 mmol/L and hyperkalaemia +4 per 100,000, with every trial excluding people in whom extra potassium is potentially harmful [^S80]; 23 trials in 32,073 patients moved urine sodium −22.96 mmol and potassium +14.41 mmol for mortality HR 0.88 [^S82]. The translation is that about half a gram less sodium and half a gram more potassium a day moved pressure 4 to 5 mmHg and outcomes measurably in largely hypertensive populations.

### 3.5 Hyperkalaemia and the potassium switch

In 1,217,986 people the potassium-mortality curve is U-shaped with its nadir at 4.0 to 4.5 mmol/L (HR 1.22 at 5.5, 1.49 at 3.0) and the hyperkalaemic tail tied to low eGFR and albuminuria [^S76]. In 191 CKD G3b to 4 patients, 40 mmol/day of KCl for two weeks raised plasma potassium 0.4 mmol/L and made 11 % hyperkalaemic with no change in urinary sodium or pressure [^S81]; in healthy people the same acute load produced 13 mmol of extra natriuresis at 2 hours that was absent in CKD [^S86]. The standard review of RAAS-inhibitor hyperkalaemia has no abstract [^S50]; the population consequence appeared after RALES, when spironolactone prescribing in elderly ACE-inhibitor users rose from 34 to 149 per 1,000 and hyperkalaemia admissions from 2.4 to 11.0 per 1,000 [^S49]. Nephrology consensus has shifted from potassium restriction toward plant-rich patterns while acknowledging the paucity of data [^S77]. Mechanistically, an oral potassium load dephosphorylates the thiazide-sensitive cotransporter NCC within 15 minutes and produces natriuresis within 30, independently of aldosterone [^S61]; potassium deficiency activates NCC through membrane voltage, intracellular chloride and WNK kinases, retaining sodium at the cost of pressure [^S69]; NCC therefore acts as a potassium sensor, and potassium-induced natriuresis explains part of a high-potassium diet's pressure effect [^S78]. A sachet's 5 to 13 mmol of potassium arrives with 10 to 45 mmol of sodium from the same stick; whether any net natriuresis follows has not been tested, and the extra sodium is excreted in a healthy kidney regardless.

## 4. Fasting, Insulin, Aldosterone and Overnight Fluid Balance

### 4.1 The natriuresis of fasting: what was measured

The marketing chain says sleep is an eight-hour fast, fasting makes the kidney dump sodium because insulin falls, and the deficit must be replaced before eating. The physiology behind links two and three was mapped in metabolic wards between 1959 and 1985, mostly in obese patients on therapeutic fasts, and most of it carries no abstract in PubMed. The founding observation of increased salt excretion during fasting [^S87], carbohydrate's inhibition of that excretion [^S88], the claim that carbohydrate deficiency reproduces fasting weight loss, electrolyte excretion and fatigue [^S89], the extension to lean subjects [^S90], carbohydrate-versus-protein effects on sodium excretion [^S91], the constant-intake fasting and refeeding series [^S93], tubular sites of reabsorption [^S92], the therapeutic-fasting excretion record [^S94], the time-course paper on the pattern of sodium excretion in starvation [^S96], the aldosterone-blockade test [^S97], the renin-aldosterone dissociation and mineralocorticoid refractoriness during starvation [^S98], the canonical renin, aldosterone and glucagon account [^S101] and the insulin-glucagon review in obese subjects [^S105] all exist and are verified, and none can supply a mmol/day figure from its indexed record. The glucose-induced alkalosis of refeeding [^S95] and the paradoxical aciduria after alkali in prolonged-fasted patients [^S102] are in the same position.

The one classic with numbers is the mechanistic keystone. In nine obese women fasted with sodium intake maintained, 3-hour increments in urinary sodium, ammonium and potassium tracked increments in organic-acid anions plus phosphate at r = 0.891, with the regression y = 0.73x + 19; ammonium lagged at first, "making sodium coverage necessary", then replaced sodium as the major urinary cation; sodium loss far exceeded chloride loss; and glucose refeeding promptly cut sodium excretion in step with the anions [^S100]. The anion load that makes this obligatory was quantified in chronically fasted subjects: 120 ± 7 mmol/day of ketoacid anion excretion at a blood concentration of 5.5 ± 0.2 mmol/L, falling to 9 mmol/day when an acid load raised fractional reabsorption from 77 to 97 % [^S107]. The segment and time course come from four days of carbohydrate withdrawal: proximal reabsorption fell from 70 to 60 mL/min, accounting for the natriuresis and 1.8 kg of weight loss, distal reclamation rose to compensate, and sodium excretion was back to control by day 4 [^S106].

### 4.2 The critical negative result

Fifteen healthy men ate a controlled diet for seven days and fasted on the eighth, with urine collected in seven fractions. During the fast, urinary potassium, aldosterone, 17-hydroxycorticosteroids and adrenaline rose significantly, while sodium, chloride, creatinine, urea, uric acid, 17-ketosteroids, noradrenaline and dopamine fell significantly, with circadian rhythms preserved [^S113]. A full 24-hour fast in healthy normal-weight men reduced sodium excretion and raised aldosterone. If that is the 24-hour result, a 12- to 16-hour overnight fast has no plausible route to a sodium deficit: the sodium in the morning void is yesterday's dinner leaving, not reserves draining.

The far end of the dose-response confirms the sign. Eight days water-only in twelve men produced weight loss, ketogenesis, hyperuricaemia, lower glucose and hyponatraemia "accompanied by Na+, K+ and protein sparing" with reduced volumes of acidic, concentrated urine [^S132]; a 40-day fast presented with hyponatraemia and then hypokalaemia, hypophosphataemia and hypomagnesaemia on refeeding [^S118]; the 44-day Blaine fast documented deficiency of thiamine, riboflavin and vitamin K, not sodium, with mineral losses from 1.2 % of body manganese to 40.5 % of zinc and no sodium figure [^S114]. A 1,610-subject fasting-clinic cohort showed ketonuria in over 95 % from day 4, modulated but not suppressed by 72 to 236 kcal/day of juice, soup and honey [^S136], with a registered protocol for its successor study [^S133]; three authors are clinic employees and the soup is itself a sodium source.

### 4.3 Insulin's antinatriuresis

Insulin infused to 98 to 193 µU/mL in six water-loaded men cut urinary sodium from 401 to 213 µEq/min with GFR, renal plasma flow and aldosterone unchanged and free-water clearance rising, placing the effect in the distal diluting segment [^S99]. Dog micropuncture showed euglycaemic hyperinsulinaemia inhibiting proximal sodium, fluid, calcium and phosphate reabsorption by 8 to 14 % while fractional excretion fell, so the distal effect wins [^S103]; the scope's picture of insulin as a proximal sodium-retaining hormone has the sign backwards. Removing basal insulin and glucagon with somatostatin for five hours raised serum potassium 0.5 to 0.6 mEq/L extrarenally, left serum sodium unchanged, and produced a biphasic urinary sodium response, falling 20 to 60 % in the first two hours before rising 50 to 80 % above basal at three to four, with the mechanism "to be established" [^S104]. Physiological and supraphysiological clamps in 20 normal subjects cut fractional sodium excretion 34 % then 50 %, with distal reabsorption rising and proximal falling, and neither insulin sensitivity nor salt sensitivity correlated with the overall change [^S111]; in salt-sensitive and salt-resistant hypertensives the physiological clamp cut it 38 and 36 % with proximal reabsorption unaffected [^S112]. A 10-fold insulin rise cut excretion from 0.315 to 0.207 mmol/min with no pressure change, and a low-dose ANF infusion that merely doubled plasma ANF abolished the effect [^S109]; the renal haemodynamic and sympathetic study of insulin in normals has no abstract [^S110]. At the transporter level, insulin phosphorylates NCC at threonine 58 through PI3K-mTORC2-AKT1 and WNK3 [^S119] and acutely in distal cells and mouse kidney [^S117]; four weeks of insulin in rats raised mean arterial pressure from 98 to 107 mmHg and increased the natriuretic response to ENaC and NCC blockers through apical trafficking [^S115]; insulin and IGF-1 activate Kir4.1/5.1 channels that set collecting-duct basolateral voltage [^S124]; the whole is reviewed in the context of insulin resistance and hypertension [^S116]. Every human number comes from insulin raised 10-fold or to postprandial peaks for 90 to 120 minutes; no study measured renal sodium handling across the overnight fall from postprandial to basal insulin, the one pharmacological removal of basal insulin gave a messy biphasic result, and a physiological natriuretic peptide rise cancels the effect.

### 4.4 Circadian rhythm and the timescale of balance

In 50 normotensive, borderline and mildly hypertensive men, sodium and catecholamine excretion was high by day and low at night with comparable 24-hour totals [^S108]; in 21 young men, daytime bright light phase-advanced the rhythm of urine volume and sodium, chloride and uric acid excretion without changing the daily total [^S131]. The night is the trough, and the rhythm's phase is shiftable while its total is not. At fixed salt intake of 12, 9 and 6 g/day for months in a spaceflight simulation, daily sodium excretion showed aldosterone-dependent weekly rhythms and total-body sodium changed by ±200 to 400 mmol on roughly monthly periods with no change in body weight or extracellular water [^S121]; over 105 and 205 days urinary recovery was 92 % of intake, yet a single 24-h urine classified a 3 g salt difference correctly only 49 % of the time, three collections 75 % and seven 92 %, against a ±25 mmol prediction interval [^S122]; with potassium held at 4 g/day, all three electrolytes showed a 7-day infradian rhythm with aldosterone as the common regulator [^S123]. There is no nightly ledger to balance; the body stores and releases sodium on a scale of five to ten sachets without moving water.

### 4.5 "You wake up dehydrated"

Copeptin sampled every 30 minutes for 24 hours in 19 volunteers peaked at 04:00 to 06:00 (5.9 ± 1 pmol/L) and troughed at 17:00 to 19:00 (2.3 ± 0.2), a rhythm "of minor extent" seen only in people with higher baseline levels [^S125]. That nocturnal vasopressin rise concentrates urine, which is its job. A five-day inpatient crossover between 1.0 and 2.5 L/day moved urine osmolality, specific gravity, colour and volume "but not plasma nor saliva osmolality", with afternoon urine the most representative sample of the day [^S120]. Twenty volunteers who raised intake by 716 mL/day for a week shifted 24-h urine osmolality −15 % and creatinine −20 % while morning urine and body fluid volumes were unchanged; morning urine "seemed to mirror long-term corrections" [^S130]. In 67 active adults, first-morning urine osmolality correlated with every variable except the previous five days' plasma osmolality and needed a cut-point above 710 mOsm/kg to flag a 24-h value above 500 [^S134]. Morning thirst in 24 young adults predicted neither afternoon body-mass loss, plasma osmolality nor water intake [^S135]. Creating a real deficit took three days at 1 L/day, and correcting it took about 1,500 mL of extra water (total 2,990 to 3,515 mL/day) [^S129]. Six hours of sleep versus eight associated with higher urine specific gravity and 1.4- to 1.6-fold odds of inadequate hydration in cross-sectional NHANES and Chinese data, with self-reported sleep, single spot urines and an ambiguous causal arrow [^S127]. A 14-day doubly-labelled-water study of 21 young adults put total water intake at 3,023 mL/day and loss at 1,931 mL/day, 81 % urinary, with respiratory loss coupled to energy expenditure; its intake and loss figures do not balance and its insensible losses were estimated from equations, so no overnight figure is taken from it [^S138]. No PubMed-indexed study measuring total overnight water loss in sleeping healthy adults could be found; the 0.3 to 0.5 L figure in circulation is a convention.

### 4.6 Ketogenic diets and "keto flu"

A 2025 systematic search found the keto-induction literature "highly heterogenous", its mechanisms "proposed based on general insights into physiology, but few have been empirically tested", and "a physiological rationale for supplementation with electrolytes and ketone esters, but a lack of clinical studies documenting their effect" [^S137]. The most-cited keto-flu paper is a content analysis of 448 posts from 300 forum users describing 54 symptoms peaking in week one and 18 proposed remedies that "reflected assumptions" [^S128]. The one randomised dose-response, 77 adults at 5, 15 or 25 % carbohydrate for three weeks, raised beta-hydroxybutyrate by 0.62, 0.41 and 0.27 mmol/L and found symptom change not different between groups (P = 0.264), with only halitosis and muscle weakness differing and mood improving overall [^S126]. The predicted gradient did not appear, and the highest-ketosis arm sat roughly an order of magnitude below the state in which sodium cover is obligatory. Searching for time-restricted-eating or intermittent-fasting trials with electrolyte endpoints in healthy adults returned none.

## 5. Sweat Losses, Sweat Testing and Exercise-Associated Hyponatremia

### 5.1 Sweat rate and sweat sodium

In 506 athletes measured with forearm patches, sweat sodium averaged 43.6 ± 18.2 mmol/L (12.6 to 104.8), predicted whole-body 35.9 ± 10.4 (18.2 to 70.8), and sweat rate 1.21 ± 0.68 L/h (0.26 to 5.73) [^S173]. The expansion to 1,303 athletes gave whole-body sweat rates of 1.51 L/h in American football, 1.28 in endurance sport, 0.95 in basketball, 0.94 in soccer and 0.83 in baseball, with sodium losses of 55.9, 51.7, 34.5, 34.6 and 27.2 mmol/h [^S185]. Ninety minutes of cycling at 45 versus 65 % VO2max in 30 °C lost 0.86 versus 1.27 L at 32.6 versus 52.7 mmol/L, a total of 659 versus 1,565 mg of sodium, both volume and concentration rising with intensity [^S184]. The most everyday dataset, 285 Chinese adults doing one hour of brisk walking, running or cycling in temperate conditions, sweated 0.71 ± 0.28 L/h (running 0.92) at an estimated 34.1 ± 10.3 mmol/L for a 0.78 % mass loss [^S195], about 0.55 g of sodium. Resting pilocarpine tests in 696 professional team-sport athletes gave regional values of 43 to 54 mmol/L by sport, from a company that sells the test [^S178]. Healthy salty sweaters have lower luminal CFTR in biopsied sweat ducts, as do cystic-fibrosis patients, without carrying disease mutations; sweat sodium is partly a fixed ductal trait [^S157]. In eight trained men over two 5-hour rides, whole-body sweat sodium varied within a trial by 6 to 7 mmol/L and between trials by 5.6, and a 40-minute sample predicted the session mean with R² 0.87 [^S196].

### 5.2 Measuring it

Five-site regional patches overestimated whole-body sodium (59 vs 41 mEq/L), with thigh the best single site [^S155]; across nine regions, patches estimated whole-body concentration acceptably (r² 0.55 to 0.77) but predicted sweating rate with r² of only 0.44 to 0.69, which "does not meet the accuracy or reliability required to inform fluid intake recommendations" [^S179]; cross-validation in 49 new participants cut corrected bias to 0 to 5 mmol/L with limits of agreement of ±12 to 17 mmol/L [^S190]. The methodological review puts body-mass change as the sweat-rate standard and lists collection system, timing, skin cleaning, storage, analysis, intensity, environment, acclimation, body size, equipment, sex, age, diet and hydration as sources of variation [^S175]. The resting pilocarpine test over-estimated exercise sweat sodium at low intensity, matched it at moderate and under-estimated it at high (44.5, 54.9, 61.3 mmol/L at 0.62, 1.26, 1.92 L/h), with a visit-to-visit CV of 5.5 %, in a company-authored comparison [^S197]. No peer-reviewed validation of the consumer colorimetric patch was retrievable. Of 344 endurance athletes, 82 % believed they would benefit from sweat testing, 74 % that sodium prevents hyponatremia and 75 % that it prevents cramps, informed mostly by social supports, self-experimentation and media [^S187]. A modelling study across sweat sodium of 20 to 80 mmol/L, rates of 0.5 to 2.5 L/h and 10 to 90 % fluid replacement found sodium replacement unnecessary in all realistic soccer and elite-marathon scenarios, needed in a 160-km ultramarathon only at ≥40 mmol/L with over 80 % replacement, and above half of losses only at ≥1.5 L/h, ≥40 mmol/L and ≥90 % replacement; athletes outside that corner "can choose sodium intake according to taste" [^S193].

### 5.3 What moves sweat sodium

Ten days of exercise-heat acclimation in eight men lowered the sweat-sodium intercept by 15 mmol/L at any sweat rate [^S152]; in four volunteers the downward shift was significant by day 3 and linear over seven days [^S180]; in eight participants under controlled hyperthermia, sweat sodium and chloride fell to about 60 % of day-1 values by day 10 with conservation from day 3 and potassium unchanged [^S192]. Three days of low versus high sodium diet moved estimated whole-body sweat sodium by 10 to 12 % in each direction, tracking aldosterone, with no effect on heart rate or perceived exertion [^S188]. The comprehensive sweat-gland review states that glands do not concentrate sweat or conserve water through vasopressin, that sweat is a minor route for toxicants, and that "sweat-induced deficiencies appear to be of minimal risk for trace minerals and vitamins", with only high-chloride sweaters at risk during prolonged heavy sweating [^S183].

### 5.4 The position stands and the thirst debate

The 2007 ACSM stand set the goal of preventing dehydration beyond 2 % of body weight and recommended customised replacement programmes based on weighing, with electrolyte-carbohydrate drinks useful "under certain circumstances" [^S151]. The 2017 NATA statement names both hypohydration and excessive intake as risks and asks for "sufficient but not excessive" practice [^S177]. The IOC consensus on supplements names caffeine, creatine, buffers and nitrate as evidence-backed and is not a fluid guideline [^S182]. The planned-drinking position, published with a sports-drink honorarium, recommends planning for events over 90 minutes, heat, high sweat rates and competitive stakes, allows thirst for under 60 to 90 minutes in cooler conditions, and says never to gain weight [^S181]. A meta-analysis of eight crossover estimates found ad libitum drinking (505 mL/h, −2.1 %) beat programmed drinking (1,073 mL/h, −1.0 %) by 0.98 %, "likely trivial" [^S186]. Twenty-five soldiers marching 40 km at 27 °C drinking to thirst lost 4.0 % of mass, saw serum sodium fall from 141 to 136 mmol/L and vasopressin rise from 2 to 11 pg/mL, and concentrated their urine above 1,000 mOsm/kg [^S189]; 181 Ironman finishers lost 4.9 % of body weight with plasma volume and serum sodium maintained, so weight is not an absolute surrogate of fluid balance in ultra-endurance [^S153].

### 5.5 The 2 % threshold and its challengers

The defence argues that volume loss impairs endurance from 2 % of body mass, that strength and cognition have no threshold, and that the distinction between hypo-osmotic (intracellular) and iso-osmotic (extracellular) water loss is the essential point of assessment [^S165]. Five cycling time-trial studies found 2.2 % dehydration changed performance by +0.06 % (P = 0.94) and drinking to thirst beat drinking below thirst by 5.2 % [^S158]; 15 articles and 28 estimates found dehydration neutral in time trials (+0.09 %) and harmful only in fixed-intensity protocols (−1.91 %), so the rule "does not apply to out-of-doors exercise conditions" [^S162]. Blinding settled the rest: cyclists reinfused to 0, −2 or −3 % with wind at 33 °C showed no time-trial difference, only a 0.3 °C higher rectal temperature at −3 % [^S172], and separating 3 % dehydration from thirst in a hot 20-km time trial gave 203 versus 207 W [^S167]. The rule holds for fixed-workload laboratory and occupational tasks and does not hold for self-paced exercise up to 3 to 4 % in trained people.

### 5.6 Exercise-associated hyponatremia

At the 2002 Boston Marathon 13 % of 488 finishers had sodium at or below 135 mmol/L and 0.6 % at or below 120; weight gain (OR 4.2) and finishing over four hours (OR 7.4) predicted it, and fluid composition, female sex and NSAID use did not [^S147]. Across 2,135 weighed performances 89 % finished euhydrated or lighter, 11 % gained weight and 30 % of those were hyponatremic; post-race sodium was a linear negative function of weight change; prevention is not drinking to excess, "known since 1985" [^S148]. At the New Zealand Ironman 18 % were hyponatremic, 31 % of them sought care, and 73 % of severe cases had maintained or gained weight [^S141]; hospitalised cases had lost less weight in the race, excreted a median 1,346 mL of excess fluid overnight, carried no larger sodium deficit than controls, and had lower post-race vasopressin [^S143]. London 2003 gave 12.5 % asymptomatic hyponatremia in cool weather with four of eleven losing weight, pointing to inappropriate antidiuretic hormone [^S159]; Zurich 2006 gave 3 % under a five-hour limit [^S154]. Western States 2009 gave 30 %, half with 3 to 6 % mass loss [^S160]; five years of 161-km races gave 15.1 % (4.6 to 51 % by year), rising with heat, with 35.6 % of cases dehydrated and weight loss over 3 % not hurting performance [^S163]. Vasopressin correlated with IL-6 mid-race and seven of ten hyponatremic runners took NSAIDs [^S166]; the mechanism is non-osmotic vasopressin release overriding osmotic suppression [^S156], with overdrinking and non-osmotic vasopressin the most common factors and volume and sodium depletion in a subset [^S176]. A US Army model predicts overdrinking as the primary cause, salty sweaters finishing dehydrated and hyponatremic, and electrolyte drinks delaying the fall [^S150]. Seven marathon runners with sodium 121 ± 3 mmol/L developed cerebral and pulmonary oedema; hypertonic saline cured six and one undiagnosed runner died [^S142]; the first Army trainee death from water intoxication followed misreading his symptoms as dehydration [^S140]; 17 Army hospitalisations at sodium 122 ± 5 mmol/L, nine of whom drank 2 quarts or more an hour, revised the Army's fluid policy [^S144]. The Wilderness Medical Society guideline defines EAH as sodium below 135 mmol/L during or up to 24 h after prolonged activity and warns that mismanagement as dehydration worsens mortality [^S191]; the third international consensus, dual-published, carries no indexed abstract [^S169] [^S170].

### 5.7 Does sodium supplementation prevent EAH or improve performance?

In 413 Cape Town Ironman finishers randomised to sodium tablets, placebo or nothing, the sodium group took an extra 3.6 ± 2.0 g over about 12 hours and showed no difference in serum sodium, weight change, finishing time, temperature or pressure; the adequate intake "seems sufficient" without supplementation [^S149]. About 700 mg/h did not change serum sodium in weight-losing Ironman athletes (1.52 vs 0.04 mmol/L, P = 0.08) and nobody finished hyponatremic [^S145]. In 13 women forced to drink 1 L/h for four hours, 680 mg/L of sodium halved the fall in plasma sodium (2.5 vs 6.2 mmol/L) and cut mild hyponatremia from 92 to 46 %, with no performance effect; the trial shows sodium slows the dilution you create by overdrinking, not that it is needed when drinking to thirst [^S146]. Twenty 161-km finishers eating 2,541 to 38,338 mg of sodium showed intake unrelated to hyponatremia, cramping or nausea, and more salt went with more retained fluid [^S171]; at Western States 2011, drinking plan and sodium beliefs did not change EAH risk, though believers used more supplements [^S164]. Replacing 100 % of individually measured sweat sodium over five hours at 30 °C changed neither fluid intake nor balance, raised plasma sodium +4 versus +1 mmol/L, had no effect on heart rate, temperature or strain, and in the placebo arm "a large sodium deficit incurred during exercise leads to substantial renal sodium conservation postexercise" (urinary sodium 21 vs 66 mmol/24 h) [^S194]. Performance trials: 113 mmol of sodium before and during a real half-Ironman gave a lower race time (P = 0.04) in 26 triathletes [^S174]; 700 mg/h over a 72-km time trial at 14 °C gave 171 versus 172 minutes [^S161]; 1,800 mg over two hours changed neither sweat rate, cardiovascular drift nor time to exhaustion [^S168]. A 164 mmol/L pre-exercise sodium load of about 757 mL raised plasma volume 4.5 %, lowered core temperature and extended time to exhaustion in 32 °C from 75 to 96 minutes in eight trained men [^S314], the strongest evidence that a concentrated sodium drink does something measurable, and it is pre-exercise plasma-volume expansion before running in heat.

### 5.8 What a sedentary person loses

No study directly measures 24-h sweat sodium in sedentary adults. Twenty-eight free-living adults over four 7-day balance periods ate 3.4 g of sodium a day, absorbed 98 % and excreted 86 % in urine, leaving an apparent +0.47 g/day "balance" that cannot be real accumulation and is the sum of skin loss and error [^S139]; ten men sealed in simulation habitats for 105 and 205 days recovered 92 % of intake in urine [^S122]. Non-renal, non-faecal loss in ordinary life is therefore bounded at roughly 0.2 to 0.4 g/day, and with sweat sodium falling at low flow rates the resting skin figure is plausibly 0.1 to 0.3 g/day, an inference to be labelled as such. Eight hours in bed lose on the order of 1 to 4 mmol through the skin plus whatever the kidney, under nocturnal vasopressin and aldosterone, chooses to excrete.

## 6. Exercise-Associated Muscle Cramps

### 6.1 The two theories

The salt-loss account began with industrial heat cramps; the one 1930s primary PubMed indexes, from the Harvard Fatigue Laboratory, has no abstract and no retrievable numbers [^S198]. Its modern statement cites a triad of salt loss, fluid loss and fatigue, historical saline treatment, salty-sweating field studies and clinical experience [^S209], and argues that tennis heat cramps are sodium-deficit cramps avoidable with salt and fluid [^S203], resting on a single 17-year-old player with a projected sweat sodium loss of 89.8 mmol/h at 2.5 L/h whose cramps resolved after more dietary salt [^S200]. The alternative, stated in 1997, found that recorded EMG during cramps and animal data on fatigued muscle supported "sustained abnormal spinal reflex activity which appears to be secondary to muscle fatigue", with increased spindle and decreased Golgi tendon organ afferent drive and two-joint muscles held shortened [^S201]. By 2009 the accounting was that the salt and water hypotheses rested on anecdote, case series totalling 18 cases and one n = 10 case-control, while four prospective cohorts did not support them [^S214]. In 13 subjects, electrical stimulation of the Achilles tendon reflexly inhibited an induced gastrocnemius cramp by the same pathway seen in voluntary EMG, and tendon inhibition was weaker in those who cramped [^S210]; under a peripheral nerve block the cramp threshold rose from 13 to 18 Hz, cramp duration fell from 55.6 to 2.6 s and peak EMG from 47.5 to 13.9 µV, placing the generator at spinal level [^S219]. The 2016 review calls EAMC multifactorial, an imbalance between spindle excitation and tendon-organ inhibition from overload and fatigue "rather than dehydration or electrolyte deficits" [^S230]; the 2022 NATA evidence review, co-authored by Schwellnus, keeps gentle static stretching as treatment and individualised prevention over "drink more fluids" [^S245]; Maughan and Shirreffs conclude that two cramp types are likely, one tied to salt and water disturbance in large industrial and field settings and one to fatigue and spinal reflexes [^S238].

### 6.2 Crampers versus non-crampers

In 82 marathon runners, 18 % cramped at about 35 km and serum sodium, potassium and plasma volume did not differ from non-crampers [^S199]. In 72 runners of a 56-km race, body weight, plasma and blood volume did not differ, post-race serum sodium was statistically lower in crampers (139.8 vs 142.3 mmol/L) and magnesium higher, both within normal limits [^S204]; in 20 Ironman athletes sodium was 140 versus 143 mmol/L with EMG higher in the cramping muscle [^S208]. In 210 Ironman triathletes there were no pre- or post-race electrolyte or body-weight differences and only faster race time and prior cramping predicted it [^S220]; in 49 runners of a 56-km race, a cramp history (100 vs 48 %), a faster first 28 km and a trend to higher pre-race creatine kinase did [^S221]; in 433 triathletes a cramp history went with taller, heavier builds, faster-than-predicted racing, family history and tendon injury [^S222]. In 280 finishers of a 161-km race, 14.3 % cramped, prior history and creatine kinase differed, and weight change, sodium supplements, sodium intake rate and post-race serum sodium did not [^S229]; in 20 finishers at up to 39 °C with full dietary records, sodium intake of 2,541 to 38,338 mg was unrelated to cramping, hyponatremia or nausea [^S171]. Yet 59 % of 1,152 ultrarunners believe sodium supplements prevent cramping [^S240]. In 76,654 Two Oceans entrants, men, age over 40, chronic disease and recent injury predicted a cramp history [^S247]; in 10,973 Comrades entrants lifetime prevalence was 14.4 %, men PR 1.94 and recent musculoskeletal injury PR 2.38 [^S249].

### 6.3 The salty-sweater argument and the laboratory models

Five cramp-prone footballers sweated 54.6 versus 25.3 mmol/L against matched teammates, projecting 5.1 versus 2.2 g of sodium per practice, in a sports-drink-institute study [^S207]; in 14 players blood sodium tended to fall in the cramp-prone (137.8 to 135.7 mmol/L) with three of six below 135, and they drank more plain water, with no cramps observed [^S211]. Across 350 collegiate athletes in 11 sports, sweat rate and sweat electrolyte concentration did not differ by cramp history except in American football [^S241], and in 30 runners with diet, hydration and heat controlled, nothing differed [^S246]. The electrically induced cramp threshold separates people with a cramp history (14.9 Hz) from those without (25.5 Hz), with family history in 89 versus 27 % [^S212]. Cycling one-legged at 41 °C to 3 % mass loss raised plasma osmolality from 282.5 to 295.1 and cost 144.9 mmol of sodium, 11.2 of potassium and 3.3 of magnesium, and the un-fatigued leg's threshold was unchanged (23.7 vs 21.3 Hz, P = 0.12) [^S216]; 3.8 hours at 39 °C to 4.7 % loss and 4.0 g of sodium left threshold, intensity and EMG unchanged [^S224]. Dehydration without fatigue does not make a muscle easier to cramp.

### 6.4 Pickle juice, TRP agonists and the rehydration-solution trials

One mL/kg of pickle juice after an induced cramp in hypohydrated men shortened it by 49.1 s (84.6 vs 133.7 s) with plasma unchanged at five minutes, an effect the authors attribute to an oropharyngeal reflex inhibiting alpha-motor neurons [^S217]; the same volume of pickle juice, carbohydrate-electrolyte drink or water changed no plasma variable over 60 minutes [^S213], and about 1.5 g of sodium as pickle juice or mustard after a 96.8 mmol sweat loss changed nothing in plasma within an hour [^S225]. A TRPV1/TRPA1 agonist drink in 39 participants raised the contraction duration and force needed to produce a cramp (13.8 vs 9.9 kg) and cut cramp EMG area, in a trial whose senior author serves on the maker's scientific advisory board [^S232]; an independent double-blind trial in 22 men found a slightly larger threshold rise at 15 minutes (3.1 vs 2.0 Hz) and nothing at 4 to 24 hours [^S231], and an editorial called it a tale of multiple complexities [^S234]. The best evidence on the salt side is the Lau and Nosaka series: after downhill running at 35 to 36 °C to 2 % mass loss, the threshold did not change with the run itself, then fell 4.3 to 5.1 Hz after water and rose 3.7 to 5.4 Hz after an oral rehydration solution [^S237]; drunk during the run to match mass loss, water lowered the threshold 3.8 to 4.5 Hz and the solution raised it 6.5 to 13.6 Hz, with serum sodium falling under 2 % with water only [^S244]. A sodium-fortified carbohydrate-electrolyte drink at sweat-matched rate doubled time to cramp (36.8 vs 14.6 min) in 13 men with a cramp history, but 69 % cramped anyway [^S206]. These show a genuine electrolyte effect on a neuromuscular surrogate when fluid loss is replaced in real time during hot exercise; none tests spontaneous cramps, a pre-exercise drink or a rested person.

### 6.5 Nocturnal cramps: magnesium, quinine, vitamin K2, stretching

The 2020 Cochrane review of 11 trials and 735 participants gives, for idiopathic cramps versus placebo, a percentage change of −9.59 % (CI −23.14 to 3.97), cramps per week −0.18 (−0.84 to 0.49), responders RR 1.04, minor adverse events RR 1.51 with diarrhoea in 11 to 37 %, and "no RCTs evaluating magnesium for exercise-associated muscle cramps" [^S239]. Magnesium oxide versus placebo in 94 adults cut cramps by 3.41 and 3.03 a week respectively, a difference of 0.38 (P = .67) that "is probably a placebo effect that may explain the wide use of magnesium" [^S233]; 5 g of intravenous magnesium sulfate daily for five days gave −2.4 versus −1.7 cramps per week (p = .51) [^S218]; 900 mg of citrate twice daily gave 11.8 versus 11.1 cramps a month [^S202]; a simulation meta-analysis of seven trials found no effect outside pregnancy [^S226]; one multicentre trial of a proprietary 226 mg magnesium oxide monohydrate found a 0.8-episode advantage over 60 days [^S242]. Quinine at about 300 mg/day cut cramp number 28 %, intensity 10 % and cramp days 20 % with rare serious toxicity restricting its use [^S227]; the AAN rates it likely effective and to be avoided routinely [^S215]; it is the useful contrast, a membrane-excitability drug that works where salt and magnesium do not. A menaquinone-7 trial in 199 older adults reported 0.96 versus 3.63 cramps a week [^S250] and carries a 2025 notice of retraction and replacement [^S251], so its figures are not to be relied on. Quinine prescriptions and searches for leg cramps roughly double in midsummer in a pattern the authors frame as unexplained [^S228]. Nightly calf-plus-hamstring stretching cut cramps by 1.2 a night in 80 adults [^S223]; calf stretching alone in 191 quinine users did nothing [^S205]; the Cochrane pool rates the evidence low to very low certainty [^S243]; three minutes of static or PNF stretching raised range of motion but not the cramp threshold [^S236], and pre-event stretching does not predict who cramps [^S248]. Six weeks of cramp-inducing electrical stimulation cut spontaneous cramps 78 % in the stimulated calf and 63 % in the other, raising the threshold from 15.5 to 21.7 Hz in an unblinded pilot [^S235], the one prevention intervention that moves the spinal marker and the cramp count together, and a conditioning intervention, which is what the fatigue model predicts.

## 7. Magnesium, Calcium, Chloride, Bicarbonate and Fluid Compartments

### 7.1 Dose against requirement and intake

Commercial sodium-forward sticks carry 50 to 120 mg of elemental magnesium (label values), 14 to 39 % of the 310 to 420 mg RDA and well under the US supplemental upper level of 350 mg or the EFSA level of 250 mg (reference values, not PubMed records). In NHANES 1999 to 2000, median dietary magnesium was 326 mg/day in Caucasian men, 237 in African American men and 297 in Mexican American men, and 237, 177 and 221 in the corresponding women, falling with age [^S262]. Forty-eight percent of the US population ate less than the requirement in 2005 to 2006 and the dietary calcium-to-magnesium ratio had risen above 3.0 [^S275]; about half consume less than the estimated average requirement, and the 0.75 to 0.95 mmol/L serum interval derives from the 1974 NHANES I distribution rather than from outcomes [^S280]. The gap between median intake and the RDA is about 80 to 100 mg, which is, roughly, a sachet. Serum magnesium does not reflect the more than 99 % of body magnesium that is intracellular, a point made in a review whose author also wrote a book defending salt [^S286], and the status assays are unvalidated, a point made by a chelate supplier's employees who put "deficiency" at 45 % on an intake basis [^S288]. Hypomagnesemia below 0.76 mmol/L was present in 14.5 % of about 16,000 unselected Germans, more in older women [^S261]; the prevalence is a function of the cut-off.

### 7.2 Forms and bioavailability

Magnesium oxide was 43 % soluble at peak acid and citrate 55 % in plain water, and after a 25 mmol load the urinary increment was 0.22 versus 0.006 mg per mg creatinine [^S254]. Sixty days of 300 mg/day as citrate, chelate or oxide in 46 adults put the organic forms ahead on 24-h urinary magnesium, citrate highest on serum and saliva, and oxide "no differences compared to placebo" [^S263]; after five days of saturation, a single 400 mg dose of citrate raised 24-h urinary and plasma magnesium and oxide did not [^S293]. Four commercial preparations at about 255 mg/day gave oxide a fractional absorption of 4 % while chloride, lactate and aspartate were equal and higher, so "inorganic magnesium salts, depending on the preparation, may have bioavailability equivalent to organic magnesium salts" [^S260]. The human base for glycinate is one stable-isotope crossover in 12 ileal-resection patients where diglycinate and oxide absorbed 23.5 and 22.8 % overall and the chelate did better only in the four worst absorbers [^S257]. The ranking behind "malate is best absorbed" is a rat study of five salts that puts oxide and citrate last [^S292]. Absorption runs through a paracellular passive route and a transcellular TRPM6/7 route, dose is the major controller, multiple small doses absorb more than one large one, salt type is "less relevant than is often thought", and a replete person excretes what is absorbed [^S284]; across 0 to 80 mEq added to a meal, fractional absorption fell from 65 to 11 %, almond magnesium matched acetate and enteric-coated chloride was poor [^S255]; at usual intakes magnesium is absorbed mostly by paracellular diffusion and solvent drag in the distal gut, and its interactions with calcium and phosphate "cannot be readily explained" [^S256]. A 60 mg dose of a soluble salt taken fasted in water sits at the efficient end of the curve, around 50 to 65 % absorbed; the same dose as oxide would barely register.

### 7.3 Exercise, sweat and the laxative threshold

The USDA review holds that strenuous exercise redistributes magnesium and "apparently increases urinary and sweat losses that may increase magnesium requirements by 10-20%", that intakes under 260 mg (men) or 220 mg (women) may produce deficiency in athletes, and that supplementation of magnesium-adequate active people "has not been shown to enhance physical performance" [^S266]; the human exercise literature mostly measured pressure, heart rate and VO2max rather than performance [^S285]. Twenty-five volleyball players on 350 mg/day for four weeks lowered lactate and raised jump height up to 3 cm while plasma magnesium fell [^S277]; 22 college students on 350 mg/day for ten days reported soreness one to two points lower and perceived recovery higher after eccentric bench press, with repetitions "approaching significance" [^S298]; 139 elderly women on 300 mg/day of oxide for 12 weeks, unblinded, improved physical performance scores most where intake was below the RDA [^S278]. Clean-site sweat in seven heat-acclimated subjects over seven hours carried 1,265 ng/mL of magnesium (1.27 mg/L) against 863 µg/mL of sodium and 222 of potassium, stable over time and unaffected by sweat rate [^S269]; a hard 2.5 L session loses about 3 mg, under 1 % of the RDA. The reported 43 % fall in sweat magnesium with acclimation [^S271] disappeared when the skin was cleaned first, with skin residue inflating early values [^S276], and the sweat-physiology review places trace-mineral loss at minimal risk [^S183]. The laxative dose is 1.5 g/day of magnesium oxide (about 900 mg elemental), which improved chronic constipation in 70.6 versus 25.0 % [^S290], eight to fifteen sachets; the Cochrane cramp trials at 300 to 400 mg/day recorded gastrointestinal events in 11 to 37 % [^S239]. Calcium at 1,000 mg/day did not change magnesium absorption (50 vs 55 %) or balance in the one controlled human study, funded by the calcium product's maker [^S258]; the competition marketed in either direction is undetectable at drink doses.

### 7.4 Chloride, citrate, bicarbonate and acid-base

Chloride is the principal extracellular anion and a major acid-base determinant in the strong-ion approach, with hyperchloraemic acidosis a problem of intravenous litres [^S272]; in 77 healthy adults dietary chloride independently predicted lower plasma bicarbonate, with NaCl carrying 50 to 100 % of the acidosis-producing effect of the diet's net acid load [^S268]. The ergogenic buffer literature is a different dose universe: 0.3 g/kg of sodium bicarbonate (22.5 g, 268 mmol of sodium, 6.2 g) gave +1.7 % in one-minute sprints while sodium citrate at 0.5 g/kg gave 0.0 % and ammonium chloride −1.6 % [^S274]; across 189 articles buffers raised blood bicarbonate 5.2 mmol/L for an effect size of 0.17, bicarbonate ahead of citrate [^S299]; the position stand puts 0.3 g/kg optimal 60 to 180 minutes before 30-second to 12-minute efforts with bloating, nausea and vomiting as the cost and part of the effect placebo [^S296]; 0.1, 0.2 and 0.3 g/kg raised bicarbonate 2 to 5, 5 to 8 and 6 to 12 mmol/L with peaks at 30 to 180 minutes [^S281]; capsules keep over 80 % of people above +5 mmol/L from 75 to 240 minutes [^S294]; sodium citrate at 500 to 900 mg/kg gave equal alkalosis peaking at 180 to 212 minutes with dose-dependent gut symptoms [^S283]. A sachet with 1 g of sodium as citrate carries about 43 mmol of alkali-equivalent, a sixth of the minimum ergogenic load, into an extracellular bicarbonate pool of about 360 mmol that the kidney re-sets within hours; as chloride it carries 43.5 mmol into a daily dietary chloride intake of 150 to 250 mmol. Neither anion does anything measurable at drink dose.

### 7.5 Fluid compartments

The compartment model and the Edelman relation descend from a 1959 review [^S252]; plasma sodium is set by total exchangeable sodium plus potassium over total body water, with osmotically inactive sodium and potassium modulating it [^S265]. Adding sodium raises extracellular osmolality and moves water out of cells until osmolality equalises; sodium does not hydrate cells. Hypo-osmotic sweat loss leaves plasma hypertonic and dehydrates cells, iso-osmotic loss drains the extracellular space, endurance impairment runs through volume from about 2 %, and cognitive effects are "small and related primarily to distraction or discomfort" [^S165]. The interstitium is a dynamic interface integrating exchangeable sodium stores and transcapillary dynamics rather than a static reservoir [^S273], and total-body sodium moves ±200 to 400 mmol without water [^S121]. Total body water is measured by oxygen-18 and deuterium dilution, the deuterium space 3 % larger from non-aqueous exchange [^S253]; bioimpedance estimates fat-free mass and water only in normal fluid states, and estimating changes in extracellular versus intracellular water "requires further research" [^S264]; no concentration measure, plasma osmolality included, has been shown to track body-water gain and loss in daily life, and a single sample cannot represent fluctuating compartments [^S267]. The hydration effect that is documented is extracellular retention: an oral rehydration solution left 1,038 g of urine against 1,337 g for water over four hours while a sports drink equalled water [^S282], and after 2 % exercise dehydration a 61 mmol/L drink at 100 % replacement produced 260 mL of urine against 493 mL at 23 mmol/L, since "unless the sodium content of the beverage is sufficiently high this will merely result in an increased urinary output" [^S259]. A 1,000/200/60 mg stick in 500 mL computes to about 190 to 200 mOsm/kg, hypotonic; its 43.5 mmol of sodium holds about 0.31 L in the extracellular fluid for a few hours; its potassium is 0.15 % of body potassium and its magnesium 0.25 % of body magnesium. "Cellular hydration" is not a property of this drink.

### 7.6 Who actually has low magnesium

In the Rotterdam Study of 9,818 people, proton-pump-inhibitor users had serum magnesium 0.022 mEq/L lower and hypomagnesemia OR 2.00 (7.22 with a loop diuretic), only after prolonged use, with 36 cases at or below 0.72 mmol/L [^S279]; thiazides carried OR 2.7 to 3.1 while loop diuretics were associated with higher serum magnesium and no excess risk [^S287]. Sixteen observational studies put hypomagnesemia at 19.4 % of PPI users against 13.5 % of non-users, adjusted OR 1.71 with a dose-response [^S291], while a parallel meta-analysis of 15 studies found RR 1.44 with I² 85 % and the association "unclear" in subgroups [^S289]. Chronic alcohol-use disorder markedly lowers total, ionised and muscle magnesium with blunted renal conservation [^S297]. Type 2 diabetes runs 13.5 to 47.7 % in selected clinic populations [^S270] and 9.6 % below 0.70 mmol/L in 929 unselected Dutch primary-care patients, with metformin, HbA1c and BMI associated [^S295]. A healthy adult on none of these has a low prior for hypomagnesemia; one on a daily PPI plus a thiazide does, and the trial-tested response is 300 to 400 mg/day with a serum check, not a sachet.

## 8. Formulations, Osmolarity, Rehydration Solutions and General Hydration

### 8.1 What oral rehydration solution is for

SGLT1 on the brush border couples sodium, glucose and water in the upper small intestine, sodium leaving by the basolateral pump [^S305]. In human perfusion the ileum absorbed sodium against a 110 mEq/L gradient, unaffected by water flow and unstimulated by glucose, galactose or bicarbonate, while jejunal sodium chloride moved by bulk flow along osmotic gradients that glucose generates [^S300]; the molecular identities are electroneutral NHE3 along the small intestine and proximal colon, nutrient-coupled SGLT1 and amino-acid transporters in the jejunum, ENaC in the distal colon and the paracellular route [^S327]. Glucose accelerates sodium and water uptake in the jejunum; the ileum absorbs sodium without it; a glucose-free salt drink is absorbed lower down and later. Solutions with two transportable substrates absorbed more water at a given osmolality, with solute flux (r = 0.69) explaining absorption better than osmolality (0.32) [^S301]; 6 % carbohydrate drinks at 197, 295 and 414 mOsm/kg emptied and absorbed no differently from each other during exercise (633 to 802 mL per 50 cm per hour) [^S303]; carbohydrate content governed gastric emptying more than osmolality, dilute drinks emptying in 14 to 17 minutes and concentrated ones in 64 to 130 [^S302]; volume and energy density dominate, osmolality, temperature and pH are minor, and hypertonic drinks cause transient net secretion into the lumen [^S304]. Reduced-osmolarity ORS in children with diarrhoea cut unscheduled intravenous rehydration (OR 0.61, then 0.59 in the Cochrane update), stool output and vomiting with no extra hyponatraemia [^S306] [^S307]; in cholera it raised biochemical hyponatraemia (RR 1.67) while rice-based ORS shortened diarrhoea by 17 hours [^S311]. The industry's own review states that the optimal beverage depends on whether the loss is sweat, urine, respiration or diarrhoea [^S323]. No trial in this corpus involves a euvolemic adult who slept eight hours.

### 8.2 The Beverage Hydration Index

Seventy-two euhydrated, fasted men drank 1 L of water and three of twelve other drinks over 30 minutes: four-hour urine was 1,337 ± 330 g after water, 1,038 after ORS, 1,052 after full-fat and 1,049 after skimmed milk, with BHI values at two hours of 1.54, 1.50 and 1.58, while cola, diet cola, tea, coffee, lager, orange juice, sparkling water and a sports drink were not different from water; the ORS standard deviation (0.74) was half its mean [^S282]. Follow-up cohorts found retention rising only at 27 and 52 mmol/L of sodium or 10 and 20 % sucrose, with caffeine 0 to 400 mg/L irrelevant and no vasopressin or aldosterone change [^S333]. In 24 adults, repeat-water trials six months apart gave an intraclass correlation of 0.11 at two hours and 0.31 at four, and a sugar-free caffeinated electrolyte drink gave a BHI of 0.99 to 1.01 at two to four hours, equal to water, in a study whose senior author reports funding from beverage companies including the drink's category [^S341]. A commercial electrolyte drink sipped over four hours cut six-hour urine by 32 % against water sipped over four hours, in a design the authors acknowledge confounds ingestion rate with composition and with maker funding [^S339]; a 40 mmol/L sodium and glycerol solution retained 112 ± 23 g more over eight hours, about 11 % of the litre, from sports-drink-institute employees [^S340]. Across the five trials the effect is slower diuresis of an unneeded bolus, scaling with sodium from about 27 mmol/L, absent for a sugar-free electrolyte drink, measured with poor within-person reproducibility, never with a health outcome, and four times out of five with industry money.

### 8.3 Sports drinks, osmolality and sodium pre-loads

Measured osmolalities were 218 mOsm/kg for a hypotonic drink, 281 for PowerAde and 327 for Gatorade, with deuterium appearing fastest after the hypotonic drink and performance differences inconclusive [^S318]. The 2022 meta-analysis of 28 studies defines hypertonic above 300, isotonic 275 to 300 and hypotonic below 275 mOsm/kg, and found plasma volume during exercise fell 6.3 % with hypotonic, 8.7 % with isotonic, 7.4 % with hypertonic drinks and 7.5 % with water, so "hypotonic carbohydrate-electrolyte drinks ingested continuously during exercise provide the greatest benefit to hydration", from an author previously funded by hypotonic-drink makers [^S337]. Gatorade is hypertonic under that convention, and "isotonic matches your body" is marketing. After exercise, plain water produces prompt diuresis and sodium, carbohydrate, protein and food aid retention [^S328]. A 164 mmol/L sodium pre-load raised plasma volume 4.5 % and extended time to exhaustion in heat from 75 to 96 minutes in eight trained men [^S314]. A 1,000 mg sodium stick in 500 mL is 87 mmol/L, above the 61 mmol/L "high" drink of the Shirreffs study and the 75 mmol/L of ORS, below Sims' 164; in 250 mL it is about 400 mOsm/kg and hypertonic.

### 8.4 Glucose-free powders, osmotic diarrhoea and sweeteners

Sodium is absorbed without glucose [^S300] [^S327]; the sports-nutrition claim that it cannot be is false in a healthy gut, and the clinically true version is that in secretory diarrhoea the glucose-coupled route is what still works. Gastrointestinal tolerance of a concentrated glucose-free salt drink on an empty stomach at rest is untested; gut comfort was highest with the hypotonic drink in the one exercise comparison [^S318]. Magnesium's osmotic effect follows its unabsorbed fraction, 35 % at the lowest dose and 89 % at the highest [^S255], with the therapeutic laxative dose at about 900 mg/day [^S290]. For the sugar alcohols in some powders, the non-effective doses for diarrhoea were erythritol 0.46 to 0.68 g/kg, xylitol 0.37 to 0.42 and lactitol 0.25 to 0.34 [^S313]; 50 g of xylitol caused nausea, bloating, colic and watery stools, 35 g loosened stools, and 20 to 35 g of erythritol produced no symptoms [^S315], an order of magnitude above sweetener doses.

### 8.5 How much water, and what extra water buys

No scientific study supports eight 8-ounce glasses a day; intake surveys and the precision of osmoregulation argue that healthy sedentary adults in temperate climates need less, caffeinated drinks count, and more is advisable in heat, hard work and some diseases [^S308]. Seventy-one adults divided into low (≤1.2 L/day) and high (2 to 4 L/day) drinkers produced 1.0 versus 2.4 L of urine at 767 versus 371 mOsm/kg with similar plasma osmolality and higher cortisol, creatinine and vasopressin in the low drinkers [^S321]. A review proposes vasopressin as an individual biomarker and reports that a mild neuroendocrine defence of body water begins below 1.8 L/day of total intake, from authors with bottled-water consultancies [^S329]; a narrative review by Danone researchers proposes 2.5 to 3.5 L/day so as to excrete 2 to 3 L of dilute urine, framed by its own title as a hypothesis [^S335]. Four cups of coffee a day left total body water (51.5 vs 51.4 kg), urine volume (2,409 vs 2,428 mL) and every hydration marker unchanged in 50 habitual drinkers [^S324]; acute 250 to 300 mg of caffeine is diuretic only in the caffeine-naive and tolerance develops [^S310]; across 16 studies about 300 mg added 109 ± 195 mL of urine (16 %) at rest and nothing during exercise [^S326]. The hard outcomes are two: increased intake defined as 2 L of urine a day cut stone recurrence RR 0.45 in one secondary-prevention trial of 220 people, low certainty, with no primary-prevention trial [^S334], and 1.5 L of extra water a day cut cystitis episodes from 3.2 to 1.7 and antibiotic courses from 3.6 to 1.9 in 140 low-drinking women, open-label and with company authors [^S331]. Coaching 631 stage-3 CKD patients to drink 0.6 L/day more left the eGFR decline unchanged (difference −0.3, CI −1.8 to 1.2) with copeptin and creatinine clearance moving as hypothesised and the authors noting possible underpowering [^S330]. In 15,752 ARIC participants, serum sodium above 142 mmol/L at midlife carried chronic disease HR 1.39 and above 144 premature mortality HR 1.21, using serum sodium as a proxy for hydration habits in an observational design [^S338]. Pre-meal water gave about 2 kg of extra weight loss in 48 adults aged 55 to 75 over 12 weeks, the acute satiety effect fading by the end [^S316]; 500 mL of water raised metabolic rate about 100 kJ in 14 adults, 40 % of it from warming the water [^S309], and a controlled crossover found no thermogenesis after distilled water or saline, only after sucrose [^S312]. No study tests a toxin-flushing effect; extra water dilutes urine. The water-intoxication review of 590 adults found a median intake of 8 L/day, median sodium 118 mmol/L, 31 % with no underlying condition and 13 % dead, requiring intake far above any recommendation [^S336].

### 8.6 Mild dehydration, mood and cognition

Twenty-six men dehydrated 1.59 % by exercise with or without a diuretic made more vigilance errors and slower working-memory responses with more fatigue and tension [^S317]; 25 women at 1.36 % reported worse mood, concentration and headache while "most aspects of cognitive performance were not affected" [^S319]; 24 hours of fluid deprivation in 20 women cut alertness and raised sleepiness and confusion with plasma osmolality unchanged [^S322]; restricting high drinkers to 1 L hurt mood and raising low drinkers to 2.5 L cut fatigue and confusion in an unblinded three-day crossover [^S325], these four with bottled-water funding. Across 33 studies, 280 effect sizes and 413 subjects, the pooled effect was −0.21, attention −0.52, reaction time −0.10, above 2 % −0.28 and at or below 2 % −0.14 with a confidence interval reaching 0.00 [^S332]; a 4.1 % loss across four ambient temperatures left cognition and balance unchanged while mood disturbance rose [^S320]. The consistent finding is that 1 to 2 % dehydration worsens mood, thirst and perceived effort and unreliably affects measured cognition, in trials that induce it with exercise, heat or a loop diuretic; an overnight fast in a temperate bedroom does not reach these levels.

## 10. The Morning Sachet, Worked Through

Take the typical label of 1,000 mg sodium, 200 mg potassium and 60 mg magnesium in 500 mL (manufacturer values, not PubMed-verifiable). Conversions: 1 g sodium = 43.5 mmol = 2.54 g NaCl; 1 g potassium = 25.6 mmol; 1 mmol magnesium = 24.3 mg.

**Sodium.** The stick is 43.5 mmol. Against the measured US mean of 3,608 mg/day [^S20] it gives 4,608 mg, 2.0 times the NASEM chronic disease risk reduction intake of 2,300 mg [^S28], 2.3 times the WHO maximum of 2,000 mg (fact sheet), 3.1 times the ACC/AHA optimum of 1,500 mg [^S25], and 1.46 times the midpoint of the INTERSTROKE lowest-risk band of 2.8 to 3.5 g [^S34]; it sits at the 54th percentile of PURE's own 3 to 6 g band [^S14], which is the honest version of the marketing claim and depends on that band being real rather than a formula artefact [^S21]. Against an overnight urinary output of roughly 25 to 50 mmol at a 9 g salt intake, it is about 140 % of the night's output, and that output was disposal of intake, not a deficit [^S113]. It is less than two of the ±25 mmol noise units of excretion at fixed intake [^S122] and 11 to 22 % of the spontaneous ±200 to 400 mmol total-body swings [^S121]. Its expected blood-pressure cost in a normotensive adult is 1 to 2.5 mmHg systolic from randomised slopes [^S32] [^S9], more with age, baseline pressure or hypertension [^S3] [^S19]; its cohort association is HR 1.18 per 1,000 mg [^S40], a between-person figure not to be multiplied into personal risk.

**The ratio.** US means of 157 mmol of sodium and 55 mmol of potassium give a molar ratio of 2.85; adding 43.5 and 5.1 mmol makes it 3.33, about 17 % higher, on the variable the gold-standard analyses find most predictive [^S4] [^S15] [^S40]. The salt substitute that cut mortality is about 2.25:1 sodium to potassium by mass; the stick is 5:1 [^S35].

**Potassium.** Two hundred mg is 5.1 mmol, 5.7 % of the WHO 3,510 mg [^S59], 7.7 % of the female adequate intake [^S28], 48 % of a medium banana and 22 % of a medium potato (USDA values); the pressure-lowering doses were 60 to 120 mmol [^S60] [^S65], the acute natriuresis dose 40 mmol [^S86].

**Magnesium.** Sixty mg is 2.5 mmol, 14 to 19 % of the RDA and 24 % of the EFSA supplemental upper level; at this dose fractional absorption is at the efficient end of the curve [^S255], so 30 to 40 mg reach the blood and are excreted if not needed [^S284]; it is a fifth to a sixth of the 300 to 400 mg used in every positive trial [^S263] [^S298] and about 7 % of the laxative dose [^S290].

**Osmolality.** In 500 mL about 200 mOsm/kg, hypotonic to plasma; in 250 mL about 400, hypertonic, drawing fluid into the gut until diluted [^S304]; in 1 L about 100. The sodium concentration in 500 mL, 87 mmol/L, is in the range of sweat itself and above the 61 mmol/L drink that halved post-exercise urine [^S259].

**What it buys.** On a rest day, about 0.3 L of water held in the extracellular fluid for a few hours longer than plain water would be held [^S282], a magnesium top-up, and sodium the kidney excretes over the day [^S194]. On a hot, long training day it replaces about 30 % of a two-hour hot session's sodium before the session begins, when the evidence that sodium helps is for fluid drunk during it [^S244] [^S314]. At about $1.50 a stick against under $0.30 for a quarter-teaspoon of salt and a banana (retail, confirm), one a day is about $550 a year.

## 11. Funding and the Shape of the Evidence Base

Three funding streams shape this literature. The normative sweat data, the Beverage Hydration Index programme and the planned-drinking position come largely from the Gatorade Sports Science Institute and PepsiCo or from the European Hydration Institute's circle [^S173] [^S185] [^S184] [^S181] [^S340] [^S282]; the hydration-biomarker and mild-dehydration literature is heavily Danone-funded [^S120] [^S129] [^S321] [^S335] [^S317] [^S319] [^S322] [^S325] [^S330] [^S331]; the sweat-testing comparisons are company-authored [^S178] [^S197]. Several of these findings cut against their funders' interests: the Danone papers show plasma osmolality unresponsive to intake and three days of restriction needed to create a correctable deficit; the sweat-gland review from a sports-drink institute dismisses trace-mineral sweat loss [^S183]; the industry's own review says ORS is for diarrhoea [^S323]. On the sodium side, the J-curve cohorts list public funders [^S14] [^S17] [^S23] while a polemic attributes them to industry [^S38]; the Cochrane sodium review carries a no-industry statement [^S31] and its critics disclose salt-advocacy roles [^S32]; potassium meta-analyses come from a food company's research centre [^S65], consulting-firm employees [^S64], device-company affiliates [^S73] and WHO staff [^S60]. The cramp literature's salty-sweater arm is GSSI [^S207] [^S211] [^S209] and its TRP-agonist trial has a maker's advisory-board author [^S232], while the null and spinal-mechanism work is academic [^S220] [^S219] [^S216]. The magnesium status reviews come from a chelate supplier [^S288] and a salt-book author [^S286]. The method does not resolve on disclosures; it resolves on the repeated 24-h-urine experiments, the blinded dehydration trials and the controlled fasting day, none of which has an industry sponsor.

## 12. Method and Limits of This Review

Seven schema-free evidence sweeps, each ending in an identifiers table, were parsed programmatically and every PMID resolved against the NCBI esummary record for author, year, publication type and retraction status: 341 unique candidates, 339 verified as studies, 2 comment-type records (an editorial and a pharmacovigilance letter) kept only as records, 0 author or year mismatches after folding diacritics and group-author names, 0 missing and 0 retracted; one cited trial carries a notice of retraction and replacement and is cited only with that flag [^S250] [^S251]. The bibliography was generated from the verified records and the report and findings were authored with PMID tokens converted against it, so no citation points at an unverified source. Limits: the classic fasting series has no indexed abstracts and is cited for existence only; no study measures total overnight water or sodium loss in sleeping adults, so the overnight figures are bounded inferences; manufacturer label values, WHO and EFSA documents, the US potassium labelling rule and food-composition and retail-price figures are not PubMed records and are marked as such; the review covers healthy adults and names patients only where their data bear on the risk of the practice; and the Beverage Hydration Index, sweat-patch and cramp-threshold literatures are surrogate-endpoint literatures that no trial has carried to a health outcome.

## 13. Evidence by Population

**Healthy, normotensive, sedentary or recreationally active adults.** No overnight deficit [^S113]; sodium excreted over the day [^S194]; pressure cost 1 to 2.5 mmHg per 1,000 mg [^S32]; cramps not prevented [^S220] [^S239]; magnesium a plausible top-up [^S262]; potassium best from food [^S70]. **Hypertensive, older and non-white adults.** The sodium-pressure slope is two to three times steeper [^S3] [^S19] [^S31] [^S32], the potassium response is where the trials show it [^S60] [^S72], and the salt-substitute outcome trials were run in this group [^S35] [^S85]. **Endurance and heat athletes.** Sodium during exercise matters at ≥1.5 L/h, ≥40 mmol/L, over 90 minutes and over 80 % fluid replacement [^S193] [^S181]; EAH prevention is not gaining weight [^S148]; heat acclimation cuts sweat sodium 40 % in a week [^S192]; a sodium pre-load expands plasma volume in heat [^S314]. **Kidney disease and RAAS-blocker users.** Potassium-induced natriuresis is absent and hyperkalaemia risk real at 40 mmol/day [^S86] [^S81] [^S76] [^S49]; every salt-substitute trial excluded them [^S80]. **Heart failure.** Sodium restriction evidence is underpowered and the group is not the healthy case [^S39]. **PPI, thiazide, heavy alcohol and type 2 diabetes.** The groups in whom clinically low magnesium concentrates and in whom 300 to 400 mg/day with a serum check is the tested response [^S279] [^S287] [^S297] [^S295]. **Fasting beyond a day and ketogenic induction.** Sodium loss is plausible and unmeasured, electrolyte replacement untested, and the clinical emergencies are refeeding potassium, phosphate and magnesium [^S137] [^S118] [^S132].

## 14. Electrolytes and the Other Dossiers

The magnesium dossier Health/Research/magnesium/index established that almost every magnesium benefit is deficiency correction and that the dietary gap is real; this project adds the electrolyte-drink specifics: the 50 to 120 mg dose is a top-up at the efficient end of absorption [^S255], oxide is the form to avoid [^S260], sweat is not a drain [^S269], and the cramp claim is null [^S239]. The creatine dossier Health/Research/creatine/index records creatine's water retention as intracellular; this project's sodium arithmetic shows the opposite direction for sodium [^S265]. The nutrition guide's sodium-to-potassium item and its "never work dehydrated" item Health/Protocols/nutrition are checked here: the ratio is well supported and the lever is potassium-dense food and less salt [^S35] [^S82], while the 1 to 2 % dehydration cognition estimate pools to a confidence interval touching zero [^S332]. The exercise guide's "hydrate 0.5 to 1 L per session" Health/Protocols/exercise matches the measured 0.71 L/h sweat rate of everyday exercise [^S195]. The VL9 morning-water routine morning-water-green-tea-and-detox-claims is measured against the thermogenesis and toxin claims [^S309] [^S312]. ## 15. Open Questions

## The full findings — all 45

Every conclusion this review reached, grouped by how strongly the evidence backs it. Each finding is distilled from the fully-cited evidence above.

??? note "Tier 1 — Settled (High Confidence) (16 findings)"

    **1. An overnight fast does not create a sodium deficit: a full 24-hour fast in healthy men lowered urinary sodium and chloride and raised urinary aldosterone.**

    Fifteen men on a controlled diet fasted for one day; sodium and chloride excretion fell significantly while aldosterone excretion rose, with circadian rhythms preserved. The "natriuresis of fasting" is a multi-day phenomenon of total fasting, and even under continued carbohydrate withdrawal sodium excretion was back to control by day 4 as the distal nephron compensated for the proximal defect. The classic fasting series that the marketing cites carries no abstracts in PubMed, so no mmol/day figure for days 1 to 7 can be sourced from it.

    **2. Fasting natriuresis is cation cover for ketoacid anions, and the anion load after one night is roughly two orders of magnitude below the state in which it is obligatory.**

    Sodium, ammonium and potassium increments tracked organic-acid-anion increments with r = 0.891 (y = 0.73x + 19); ammonium replaced sodium as ammoniagenesis ramped up and glucose refeeding promptly cut sodium excretion. Chronic fasting excretes 120 ± 7 mmol/day of ketoacid anions at blood ketoacid anions of 5.5 mmol/L, while three weeks at 5 % of energy from carbohydrate raised beta-hydroxybutyrate by only 0.62 ± 0.49 mmol/L. No anion load, no obligatory sodium loss.

    **3. "You wake up dehydrated" fails its direct tests: concentrated morning urine is the mechanism working, and neither morning urine nor morning thirst tracks plasma osmolality.**

    Varying intake between 1.0 and 2.5 L/day in an inpatient crossover moved urine concentration but not plasma or saliva osmolality; adding 716 mL/day for a week changed 24-h urine osmolality by −15 % and left the morning urine unchanged. First-morning urine did not correlate with the prior five days' plasma osmolality and needed a cut-point of >710 mOsm/kg to flag anything; morning thirst predicted neither plasma osmolality nor body-mass loss. Copeptin, the vasopressin surrogate, peaks at 04:00 to 06:00 (5.9 vs 2.3 pmol/L in the late afternoon) and is the reason the first void is concentrated. Creating a real deficit took three days at 1 L/day and about 1.5 L extra to correct.

    **4. Sodium balance is settled over days to weeks, not before breakfast; a 43.5 mmol sachet sits inside the body's own day-to-day noise.**

    At fixed salt intake for months, total-body sodium swung ±200 to 400 mmol on roughly monthly rhythms with no change in body weight or extracellular water; urinary recovery was 92 % of intake yet a single 24-h urine classified a 3 g salt difference only 49 % of the time, with a ±25 mmol prediction interval; one aldosterone-governed 7-day rhythm ran sodium, potassium and chloride together. Excretion is highest by day and lowest at night, and the rhythm's phase, not its total, shifts with daytime light.

    **5. Sweat sodium in ordinary exercise is modest: whole-body means sit in the mid-30s mmol/L, and a temperate one-hour session loses about half a gram.**

    Forearm sweat sodium averaged 43.6 ± 18.2 mmol/L (12.6 to 104.8) and predicted whole-body 35.9 ± 10.4 (18.2 to 70.8) in 506 athletes, with sweat rates of 1.21 ± 0.68 L/h; 285 adults doing 60 minutes of brisk walking, running or cycling in temperate conditions sweated 0.71 ± 0.28 L/h at 34.1 mmol/L, a 0.78 % mass loss. Ninety minutes at 45 vs 65 % VO2max in 30 °C cost 659 vs 1,565 mg of sodium; by sport, losses ran 27 to 56 mmol/h with American football and endurance highest.

    **6. A sedentary adult loses almost no sodium through the skin, and the kidney covers what little leaves overnight.**

    Free-living balance studies recovered 86 % of a 3.4 g/day sodium intake in urine with 98 % absorption and 92 % of intake over 105 to 205 days indoors, bounding all non-renal loss at roughly 0.2 to 0.4 g/day; the resting skin figure is plausibly 0.1 to 0.3 g/day and is an inference, not a measurement. After a 5-hour sweat deficit, urinary sodium fell to 21 mmol/24 h as the kidney conserved. No retrieved study shows an overnight sodium deficit in healthy adults.

    **7. Dehydration and salt loss alone do not make a muscle easier to cramp; the cramp generator is spinal.**

    Losing 3 % of body mass and 144.9 mmol of sodium left the electrically induced cramp threshold unchanged (23.7 vs 21.3 Hz, P = 0.12), as did 4.7 % and 4.0 g of sodium. A peripheral nerve block cut cramp duration from 55.6 to 2.6 s and raised the threshold from 13 to 18 Hz; tendon-afferent stimulation reflexly inhibits the cramp and is weaker in people who cramp. The model's authors frame it as a spindle versus Golgi tendon organ imbalance of fatigue, and the 2022 evidence review calls EAMC multifactorial with stretching as the treatment.

    **8. Athletes who cramp do not differ from those who do not in hydration or electrolytes; prior cramps, race pace, muscle damage, sex, age and injury predict it.**

    In 210 Ironman triathletes there were no pre- or post-race electrolyte or body-weight differences; faster race time and a cramp history were the only predictors, as in 82 marathon runners and 280 ultramarathon finishers where weight change, sodium supplements and serum sodium were identical. Two cohorts found post-race serum sodium 2.5 to 3 mmol/L lower in crampers, within the normal range and with no hydration difference; pacing and pre-race creatine kinase, family history and tendon injury, and in 76,654 and 10,973 runners male sex, age over 40, chronic disease and recent injury were the risk factors. The salt theory's case base is 18 cases plus one n = 10 case-control.

    **9. Pickle juice relieves a cramp in about a minute with no change in plasma electrolytes, which is evidence that the cramp does not need its salt back.**

    One mL/kg of pickle juice shortened an induced cramp by 49.1 s (84.6 vs 133.7 s) while plasma was unchanged at 5 minutes; neither pickle juice nor a carbohydrate-electrolyte drink changed plasma sodium, magnesium, calcium or osmolality over 60 minutes, and a bolus of about 1.5 g of sodium after a 96.8 mmol sweat loss moved nothing in plasma within an hour.

    **10. Magnesium does not prevent the nocturnal cramps a sedentary adult actually gets, and no trial has tested it for exercise cramps.**

    The Cochrane pool for idiopathic cramps gives −0.18 cramps/week (95 % CI −0.84 to 0.49) with 11 to 37 % gastrointestinal adverse events and "no RCTs evaluating magnesium for exercise-associated muscle cramps"; magnesium oxide versus placebo differed by 0.38 cramps/week (P =.67), a placebo effect the authors say explains its popularity; 5 g of intravenous magnesium sulfate daily for five days was null, as was 900 mg of citrate twice daily and the simulation meta-analysis outside pregnancy. A sachet's 60 to 120 mg is below every dose that failed.

    **11. Spot-urine sodium formulas are invalid for individuals, and their bias manufactures the J-curve.**

    The INTERSALT formula correlates with true 24-h sodium at r = 0.50 to 0.51 in individuals and is recommended for populations only; spot-urine bias ran to +1,090 mg/day and "a single spot urine sample is not a valid indicator of individual sodium intake"; individual misclassification was 48 to 76 %. In the same 2,974 people with three to seven 24-h urines, the Kawasaki formula over-estimated intake by 1,297 mg/day, over-estimating at low and under-estimating at high intake; measured sodium was linear with mortality and the estimated value J-shaped, all three formulas did it, and they still predicted death with urinary sodium concentration held constant. Dietary self-report under-estimates sodium by 0 to 15 % and the sodium-potassium ratio by about 20 %.

    **12. Measured with repeated 24-hour urines, the sodium-risk gradient is positive and roughly linear, and the sodium-blood-pressure slope has no low-end flattening.**

    Six cohorts with at least two 24-h urines per person (10,709 adults, median 3,270 mg/day) gave HR 1.18 (1.08 to 1.29) per extra 1,000 mg/day of sodium and 0.82 (0.72 to 0.94) per 1,000 mg of potassium, with no J-curve; TOHP at 24 years gave HR 1.12 per 1,000 mg (1.00 to 1.26) and "no evidence of a J-shaped relationship". Across 85 trials from 0.4 to 7.6 g/day the sodium-blood-pressure relationship was approximately linear with no flattening at either end, and 133 trials gave 1.10 mmHg systolic per 50 mmol with trials under 15 days showing less than half the effect of longer ones.

    **13. Sodium is absorbed without glucose; the glucose-sodium cotransport story belongs to diarrhoeal disease.**

    In human perfusion the ileum absorbed sodium actively against a 110 mEq/L gradient with no stimulation by glucose, while jejunal sodium chloride moved mostly by solvent drag that glucose accelerates; the molecular pathways are NHE3, nutrient-coupled SGLT1, ENaC and the paracellular route, and SGLT1 couples sodium, glucose and water in the upper gut. A glucose-free salt drink is absorbed, somewhat lower down and somewhat later.

    **14. "Hydrates better than water" means less diuresis of a litre you did not need; it requires 27 to 52 mmol/L of sodium, and a sugar-free electrolyte drink performed like water.**

    In 72 euhydrated men a 1 L bolus of oral rehydration solution left 1,038 g of urine over 4 h against 1,337 g for water (Beverage Hydration Index 1.54 ± 0.74), while a sports drink, cola, tea, coffee, lager and juice were no different from water; retention rose only at 27 to 52 mmol/L sodium or 10 to 20 % sucrose, with caffeine 0 to 400 mg/L irrelevant. A sugar-free caffeinated electrolyte drink gave a BHI of 0.99 to 1.01 at 2 to 4 h and the within-person reproducibility of the measurement was poor (ICC 0.11 to 0.31); a 40 mmol/L glycerol-electrolyte drink retained 112 g more over 8 h, and a metered commercial drink cut 6-h urine by 32 % in a design that confounded rate with composition. None has a health outcome, and four of the five are industry-funded.

    **15. Sodium pulls water out of cells, not into them; the only measurable hydration effect of a sodium drink is extracellular retention.**

    Plasma sodium is set by exchangeable sodium plus potassium over total body water, so adding sodium raises extracellular osmolality and moves water out of cells; hypo-osmotic sweat loss dehydrates cells while iso-osmotic loss drains the extracellular space. Holding 43.5 mmol of sodium isotonically needs about 0.31 L kept in the extracellular fluid, which is the whole ORS effect; 5.1 mmol of potassium is 0.15 % of body potassium and 2.5 mmol of magnesium 0.25 % of body magnesium. The interstitium is a dynamic interface that integrates exchangeable sodium stores with transcapillary dynamics rather than a static reservoir. Consumer bioimpedance cannot resolve a change in intracellular water of this size, no single concentration measure tracks daily body-water change, and deuterium or oxygen-18 dilution is the reference method.

    **16. Potassium, not sodium, is the Western shortfall, and higher potassium tracks lower risk in every cohort regardless of how urine was collected.**

    US 24-h urine means were 3,608 mg of sodium and 2,155 mg of potassium; fewer than 2 % met the old 4,700 mg potassium intake and 90.7 % exceeded 2,300 mg of sodium, and 36 to 38 % of older adults met the 2019 adequate intakes of 3,400 and 2,600 mg; the WHO target is 90 mmol (3,510 mg), conditional, and only 0.002 % of 103,570 people met both WHO targets at once. Each 1.64 g/day more potassium tracked stroke RR 0.79, each 1,000 mg RR 0.89, with the nadir at 90 mmol/day; extracellular potassium is 2 % of the body's total and the physiology is tuned to a high-potassium, low-sodium diet.


??? note "Tier 2 — Well Supported (Moderate Confidence) (17 findings)"

    **17. Heat acclimation cuts sweat sodium by about 40 % within 7 to 10 days, diet shifts it only ±10 %, and salty sweat is partly a fixed trait.**

    Ten days of controlled-hyperthermia acclimation lowered sweat sodium and chloride to about 60 % of day-1 values, with conservation from day 3; acclimation lowered the sodium-versus-sweat-rate intercept by 15 mmol/L, the shift appearing after two days of heat. Three days of low versus high sodium diet moved sweat sodium only 10 to 12 %, tracking aldosterone; healthy salty sweaters have lower ductal CFTR expression; whole-body sweat sodium is reproducible within 6 to 7 mmol/L and a 40-minute sample predicts a session.

    **18. Sodium supplements do not prevent exercise-associated hyponatremia; sodium in a drink only slows the dilution caused by overdrinking.**

    In 413 Ironman finishers randomised to an extra 3.6 ± 2.0 g of sodium, placebo or nothing, serum sodium, weight change, finishing time and temperature did not differ; about 700 mg/h did not change serum sodium in weight-losing triathletes; 161-km finishers eating 2.5 to 38 g of sodium showed no relation to hyponatremia, cramping or nausea, and a drinking plan or sodium beliefs did not alter EAH risk. Replacing 100 % of measured sweat sodium over 5 hours at 30 °C raised plasma sodium +4 vs +1 mmol/L with no thermoregulatory benefit. Under forced drinking of 1 L/h for 4 hours, 680 mg/L of sodium halved the fall in plasma sodium (2.5 vs 6.2 mmol/L) without helping performance, the scenario a US Army model also predicts.

    **19. Exercise-associated hyponatremia is a disease of drinking more than you sweat over four hours or more, and its lethal error is treating it as dehydration.**

    At the 2002 Boston Marathon 13 % of 488 finishers had sodium ≤135 mmol/L and 0.6 % ≤120; weight gain (OR 4.2) and finishing over 4 hours (OR 7.4) predicted it, and fluid type did not. Across 2,135 weighed performances, 11 % gained weight and 30 % of those were hyponatremic; 18 % of New Zealand Ironman finishers were hyponatremic and 73 % of severe cases had maintained or gained weight, hospitalised cases had excreted 1,346 mL of excess fluid with no extra sodium deficit, and cool marathons gave 3 to 12.5 %. Non-osmotic vasopressin release sustains the retention; a dehydrated hyponatremic subset exists in hot 161-km races (15.1 % EAH, 35.6 % of cases dehydrated). Deaths followed cerebral and pulmonary oedema treated with more fluid; the consensus definition is <135 mmol/L during or up to 24 h after prolonged activity, and water intoxication outside sport needs a median 8 L/day.

    **20. Sodium does not improve endurance performance in controlled trials; one real-race trial was positive and a concentrated pre-load expands plasma volume.**

    Seven hundred mg/h over a 72-km time trial at 14 °C gave 171 vs 172 minutes; 1,800 mg over 2 hours changed neither sweat rate, cardiovascular drift nor time to exhaustion; full personalised replacement over 5 hours changed no physiological strain measure. A parallel-group half-Ironman trial of 113 mmol of sodium in 26 triathletes gave a lower race time (P = 0.04) with higher serum sodium, and a 164 mmol/L pre-exercise load of about 757 mL raised plasma volume 4.5 % and extended running time in 32 °C from 75 to 96 minutes.

    **21. Electrolyte replacement during exercise is indicated only when duration, heat, salty sweat and high fluid replacement converge; otherwise thirst suffices and the 2 % rule does not hold for self-paced exercise.**

    A model across sweat rates, sodium concentrations and replacement fractions found sodium replacement unnecessary in all realistic soccer and elite-marathon scenarios and needed only at ≥1.5 L/h, ≥40 mmol/L and ≥80 to 90 % fluid replacement; even the planned-drinking position carves out exercise under 60 to 90 minutes for thirst and says never to gain weight. Ad libitum drinking (505 mL/h, −2.1 %) beat programmed drinking (1,073 mL/h, −1.0 %) by a trivial 0.98 %; 2.2 % dehydration changed time-trial performance by +0.06 % and ≥2 % impaired only fixed-intensity protocols; blinded −2 to −3 % trials were null, against a review defending the 2 % threshold for endurance via volume loss. Soldiers finishing a 40-km march 4 % lighter kept plasma sodium at 136 mmol/L with urine over 1,000 mOsm/kg; Ironman finishers lost 4.9 % of body weight with plasma volume and sodium maintained. The 2007 ACSM stand is the source of the 2 % rule and customised replacement, the 2017 NATA statement names overdrinking as an equal risk, and the IOC supplements consensus does not list sodium among evidence-backed supplements.

    **22. Sodium-containing fluid taken during or right after hot, dehydrating exercise raises the cramp threshold while plain water lowers it; salty-sweater cramping is confined to American football in heat.**

    After downhill running in 35 to 36 °C to 2 % mass loss, water lowered the cramp threshold by 4.3 to 5.1 Hz and an oral rehydration solution raised it by 3.7 to 5.4 Hz; drunk during the run, ORS raised it 6.5 to 13.6 Hz. A sodium-fortified drink doubled time to cramp (36.8 vs 14.6 min) but 69 % still cramped. Cramp-prone footballers sweated 54.6 vs 25.3 mmol/L in a GSSI study of ten and their blood sodium tended to fall, but across 350 athletes in 11 sports sweat differed by cramp history only in American football and not at all when diet, hydration and heat were controlled; two cramp types are likely. The salt side's reviews rest on a single tennis case report and a 1933 industrial study with no retrievable numbers.

    **23. Potassium supplementation lowers blood pressure by 3 to 5 mmHg in hypertensive people, not measurably in normotensive people, and a sachet dose has never been tested.**

    Pooled trials give −3.49/−1.96 mmHg in hypertensives only, with −7.16 at 90 to 120 mmol/day and no dose-response, −3.11/−1.97 enhanced by high sodium, −4.7 (−6.8 in hypertensives) needing 60 to 100 mmol/day, −4.48/−2.96 larger with low baseline potassium and no drugs and −4.25/−2.53; the Cochrane review found no significant effect (−11.2, CI −25.2 to 2.7). Five days of up to 60 mEq extra as potato or gluconate did nothing to blood pressure in 35 normotensive adults; 3 g/day in untreated prehypertensives gave −3.9/−1.6 while 3 g/day of sodium gave +7.5/+2.7; the dose-response is U-shaped with benefit flattening above a 30 mmol/day difference and pressure rising above about 80 mmol/day in drug-treated hypertensives. A 200 to 500 mg sachet is 5 to 13 mmol.

    **24. Salt substitutes that lower sodium and raise potassium together cut stroke, cardiovascular events and death in high-risk, high-salt populations.**

    In 600 Chinese villages (20,995 people, 73 % prior stroke, 88 % hypertensive), a 75/25 NaCl/KCl substitute gave stroke RR 0.86, major cardiovascular events 0.87 and all-cause death 0.88 with no excess hyperkalaemia events; the Cochrane pool gives −4.76/−2.43 mmHg and serum potassium +0.12 mmol/L with every trial excluding people at hyperkalaemia risk; 23 trials moved urine sodium −23 mmol and potassium +14 mmol for mortality HR 0.88; each 10 % lower NaCl fraction gave a further −1.53 mmHg; care-home residents saw −7.1 mmHg and events HR 0.60 and Taiwanese veterans cardiovascular mortality HR 0.59. The intervention lowered the sodium-potassium ratio; a 5:1 sachet raises it.

    **25. The sodium-to-potassium ratio predicts cardiovascular risk at least as well as sodium alone in the gold-standard cohorts, and a 1,000/200 mg sachet raises a typical day's ratio by about 17 %.**

    In TOHP the ratio was the only significant predictor (RR 1.24 per unit, lowest BIC); in NHANES III the top ratio quartile carried all-cause HR 1.46 and ischaemic heart disease 2.15; in the pooled 24-h-urine cohorts the ratio (HR 1.62) equalled sodium (1.60); in PREVEND it was not associated with incident hypertension while low potassium was; INTERSALT found it behaved like sodium. Reviews disagree on the ratio's primacy and NASEM named it an evidence gap with no trial targeting it. On US means (157 mmol sodium, 55 mmol potassium) the molar ratio is 2.85; a 1,000/200 mg stick makes it 3.33.

    **26. The randomised blood-pressure cost of a sustained extra 1,000 mg/day of sodium in a normotensive adult is about 1 to 2.5 mmHg systolic, larger with age, baseline pressure and hypertension.**

    Randomised slopes give 1.10 mmHg per 50 mmol overall and 2.13 in trials of 15 days or more and 5.8 mmHg per 100 mmol in trials of 4 weeks or more; the cross-sectional NHANES slope of 4.58 mmHg per 1,000 mg compares different people and overstates it, and PURE's own slope was positive at every intake (2.11 mmHg/g). In DASH-Sodium the largest step came below 100 mmol/day (4.6 mmHg on the control diet) and the effect shrank inside the potassium-rich DASH diet; age roughly doubles it (7.0 vs 3.7 mmHg) and baseline pressure triples it (−3.2 below 130 mmHg vs −9.0 at 140 to 149). TOHP's sodium arm lowered pressure 2.9/1.6 mmHg at 6 months and incident hypertension by about 20 %; the DASH diet itself gave −5.5/−3.0 overall and −11.4/−5.5 in hypertensives at constant sodium.

    **27. Magnesium form matters as "oxide versus anything soluble"; dose dominates absorption, and malate's superiority is a rat finding.**

    Magnesium oxide was 4 % absorbed while chloride, lactate and aspartate were equal and higher, so inorganic salts can match organic ones; citrate beat oxide on urinary magnesium in three human studies, with oxide "no different from placebo" at 300 mg/day. Glycinate's human base is one crossover in 12 ileal-resection patients, equal to oxide on average; the ranking that favours malate is a rat study that puts citrate last. Fractional absorption falls from 65 % to 11 % as the added dose rises from 0 to 972 mg, the salt type is "less relevant than is often thought" and split doses absorb better, and magnesium is absorbed mainly paracellularly in the distal gut.

    **28. Sweat is not a magnesium drain, and the magnesium-exercise trial base is thin and conditional on status.**

    Clean-site sweat magnesium is 1.27 mg/L against 863 mg/L of sodium, stable over 7 hours; a hard 2.5 L session loses about 3 mg, under 1 % of the RDA. The reported 43 % fall in sweat magnesium with acclimation was a skin-contamination artefact that disappeared when the skin was washed; sweat is a minor excretory route and trace-mineral loss is of minimal risk. Strenuous training may raise the requirement 10 to 20 %, with no performance gain in magnesium-adequate people; positive trials are n = 22 to 25 at 350 mg/day with perceptual endpoints, an unblinded trial in elderly women that worked best below the RDA, and reviews with surrogate endpoints.

    **29. The 50 to 120 mg of magnesium in a sachet roughly matches the median dietary shortfall; a normal serum value proves little, and so does the absence of a validated alternative.**

    Median US intakes were 326 mg/day in Caucasian men and 237 mg/day in Caucasian women; about 48 to 50 % of Americans eat less than the estimated average requirement. The 0.75 to 0.95 mmol/L serum interval is a 1974 population distribution, not an outcome-anchored cut-off; more than 99 % of body magnesium is intracellular and the status assays are unvalidated, a point made by a chelate supplier's employees. Prevalence of hypomagnesemia runs 14.5 % at <0.76 mmol/L in 16,000 Germans and under 1 % at ≤0.72 mmol/L in Rotterdam.

    **30. Clinically low magnesium concentrates in identifiable groups: long-term PPI users, thiazide users, heavy drinkers and people with type 2 diabetes; loop diuretics do not lower it in the general population.**

    PPI use carried hypomagnesemia OR 2.00 (7.22 with a loop diuretic) after prolonged use; thiazides OR 2.7 to 3.1 while loop diuretics were associated with higher serum magnesium; two meta-analyses of the PPI literature give OR 1.71 and an "unclear" RR 1.44 with I² 85 %; chronic alcohol-use disorder markedly lowers total, ionised and muscle magnesium; type 2 diabetes runs 13.5 to 47.7 % in selected clinics and 9.6 % in unselected primary care.

    **31. Hyperkalaemia from added potassium belongs to kidney disease and RAAS-blocking drugs; healthy kidneys excrete a sachet within hours.**

    Across 1.2 million people the potassium-mortality curve is U-shaped with its nadir at 4.0 to 4.5 mmol/L and the hyperkalaemic risk tied to low eGFR and albuminuria. In CKD G3b to 4, 40 mmol/day of KCl raised plasma potassium 0.4 mmol/L and 11 % became hyperkalaemic with no natriuresis; the acute potassium-induced natriuresis seen in healthy people (+13 mmol of sodium at 2 h) was absent in CKD. After RALES, hyperkalaemia admissions in elderly ACE-inhibitor users rose from 2.4 to 11.0 per 1,000; nephrology guidance has shifted from potassium restriction toward plant-rich patterns while admitting the data gap.

    **32. Mild dehydration of 1 to 2 % reliably worsens mood, thirst and perceived effort; its effect on measured cognition is small and pools to a confidence interval touching zero.**

    A 1.59 % mass loss raised vigilance errors and working-memory latency with more fatigue and tension; 1.36 % in women worsened mood, concentration and headache while "most aspects of cognitive performance were not affected"; 24 h of fluid deprivation cut alertness with plasma osmolality unchanged, and moving intake between 1 and 2.5 L/day moved mood in both directions. Pooled across 33 studies the effect is −0.21 overall, −0.28 above 2 % and −0.14 (CI to 0.00) at ≤2 %; a 4.1 % loss left cognition and balance unchanged, and the USARIEM review calls the cognitive effect small and mostly distraction.

    **33. There is no basis for eight glasses a day, coffee counts as fluid, and extra water has hard-outcome support in two settings only: recurrent kidney stones and recurrent cystitis.**

    No study supports 8 × 8 and caffeinated drinks count; plasma osmolality was identical in habitual low (1.0 L urine) and high (2.4 L urine) drinkers while vasopressin ran higher in the low drinkers. Four cups of coffee a day left total body water (51.5 vs 51.4 kg) and urine volume unchanged; about 300 mg of caffeine adds 109 ± 195 mL of urine at rest and nothing during exercise, with tolerance in habitual users. Increased intake cut stone recurrence RR 0.45 in one trial (low certainty) and cystitis from 3.2 to 1.7 episodes a year in low-drinking women; it did not slow CKD progression in 631 patients (eGFR difference −0.3, CI −1.8 to 1.2). Serum sodium above 142 mmol/L tracked chronic disease (HR 1.39) as a proxy for hydration habits in an observational cohort; the "2.5 to 3.5 L a day" position is a hypothesis paper from a bottled-water company's researchers, and six hours of sleep associates with less dilute urine in cross-sectional data with an ambiguous causal arrow. Water turnover in young adults is 81 % urinary, with respiratory loss tied to energy expenditure.

    **34. The sodium J-curve is real in spot-urine cohorts and absent in measured ones; no cohort found 4 to 6 g/day optimal, and no trial has raised sodium against hard outcomes.**

    PURE gave OR 1.27 below 3 g/day and 1.15 at or above 7 g/day against 4 to 5.99 g/day, concluding 3 to 6 g; the pooled four-cohort analysis found high sodium harmful only in hypertensives and low sodium associated with risk in both groups; the community-level analysis found harm only above 5 g/day on a non-significant top-tertile estimate (P = 0.0712), and the joint analysis put lowest risk at 3 to 5 g with above-median potassium. ONTARGET/TRANSCEND, a secondary-prevention cohort, gave cardiovascular death HR 1.66 above 8 g and 1.37 below 2 g; INTERSTROKE placed lowest stroke risk at 2.8 to 3.5 g/day; Graudal's U had a flat zone of roughly 2.6 to 4.9 g/day and states no RCTs in healthy populations exist; the one 24-h-urine cohort with an inverse mortality signal rested on 10 deaths in its high tertile with a null composite. The AHA audit found systematic sodium error in 22 of 26 studies and reverse causation in 16, with direction tracking method; the dispute has a polemic, an evidence review commissioned for the DRI committee, the guidelines that set 1,500 mg optimal with a 2,300 mg ceiling, and a global audit in which no country has yet met the 30 % reduction target.

    **35. "Sodium restriction raises stress hormones and cholesterol" is a real but small, duration-dependent finding; salt sensitivity has no clinical test; and the heart-failure null is underpowered.**

    Pooling 195 references at 203 to 65 mmol/day gave renin +1.56 ng/mL/h, aldosterone +104 pg/mL, cholesterol +5.19 mg/dL and triglycerides +7.10 mg/dL alongside −1.14 mmHg in normotensives and −5.71 in hypertensives; restricted to modest reductions sustained 4 weeks or more, the lipid change vanished and the hormone rise was small, and short trials understate the pressure benefit by more than half. The mechanistic counter-reading links salt excess itself to RAAS and sympathetic activation and insulin resistance. Salt sensitivity is pathophysiologically real, continuous, forced into categories by convention and "of no practical value" to the practising physician. SODIUM-HF achieved only about 415 mg/day of separation in 806 heart-failure patients and returned HR 0.89 (0.63 to 1.26).

    **36. Insulin's sodium-retaining action is distal, measured at clamp doses and cancelled by a physiological rise in natriuretic peptide; the overnight insulin fall has never been shown to cause sodium loss.**

    Insulin at 98 to 193 µU/mL cut urinary sodium from 401 to 213 µEq/min with unchanged GFR and aldosterone, localised to the distal diluting segment; euglycaemic hyperinsulinaemia inhibited proximal reabsorption 8 to 14 % while net excretion fell; removing basal insulin with somatostatin produced a biphasic, unexplained sodium response. Physiological and supraphysiological clamps cut fractional sodium excretion 34 to 50 % with proximal reabsorption falling and distal rising; a 10-fold insulin rise cut excretion 34 % and a doubling of ANF abolished it. The molecular nodes are NCC phosphorylation via PI3K-mTORC2-AKT1 and WNK3, apical ENaC and NCC trafficking with a chronic pressure rise in rats, and Kir4.1/5.1, reviewed in; the renal haemodynamic study has no abstract.

    **37. The "keto flu needs electrolytes" story is a mechanism without a trial, and the electrolyte problems of real fasting are hyponatraemia with sodium sparing plus potassium, phosphate and magnesium falls on refeeding.**

    A 2025 systematic review states a physiological rationale for electrolytes "but a lack of clinical studies documenting their effect"; the most-cited keto-flu paper is a content analysis of 448 forum posts whose remedies "reflected assumptions"; across 5, 15 and 25 % carbohydrate for 3 weeks, symptoms did not differ between groups (P = 0.264) despite a clear ketone gradient. Eight days water-only produced hyponatraemia with renal sodium, potassium and protein sparing; a 40-day fast presented with hyponatraemia and then hypokalaemia, hypophosphataemia and hypomagnesaemia on refeeding; a 44-day fast depleted thiamine, riboflavin and vitamin K rather than sodium. The large fasting-clinic cohort (>95 % ketonuria from day 4) supplies 75 to 250 kcal/day including soup, a sodium source, and three authors are clinic employees. The early refeeding alkalosis work carries no abstracts.

    **38. Of the non-electrolyte cramp interventions, quinine works but is restricted, TRP-agonist drinks have one conflicted positive and one independent near-null, stretching is mixed, and the 2024 vitamin K2 trial was retracted and replaced.**

    Quinine cut cramp number 28 % and intensity 10 % but toxicity restricts it and the AAN rates it effective yet to be avoided routinely. A TRPV1/TRPA1 drink raised the force needed to cramp (13.8 vs 9.9 kg) in a trial whose senior author sits on the maker's advisory board; the independent RCT found a 1 Hz threshold difference at 15 minutes and nothing at 4 to 24 h, with an editorial calling it a tale of complexities. Nightly calf-plus-hamstring stretching gave −1.2 cramps/night, calf-only stretching was null, the Cochrane pool is low certainty, acute stretching does not raise the threshold and pre-event stretching does not predict who cramps; six weeks of cramp-inducing electrical stimulation cut spontaneous cramps 63 to 78 %. The threshold-frequency marker separates crampers (14.9 Hz) from non-crampers (25.5 Hz); cramps double in midsummer for unexplained reasons; one proprietary 226 mg magnesium oxide trial was positive by 0.8 episodes; the menaquinone-7 trial reporting 0.96 vs 3.63 cramps/week carries a notice of retraction and replacement. Ultrarunners nonetheless believe sodium prevents cramps (59 %).

    **39. Potassium's bone and kidney-stone benefits belong to alkaline salts at prescription doses in specific patients, not to the chloride in electrolyte mixes.**

    Bicarbonate and citrate salts lower urinary calcium, acid excretion and a resorption marker with no effect on bone density, and chloride does less; two years of potassium citrate in 276 postmenopausal women changed neither turnover nor bone density. Potassium citrate at 30 to 60 mEq/day cut stones from 1.2 to 0.1 per patient-year in hypocitraturic formers, pooling to RR 0.26 with adverse-event dropouts RR 4.45; food potassium arrives with organic anions and potatoes are the top US source. KCl and KHCO3 improved vascular measures equally over 12 weeks without changing office pressure, and only the bicarbonate lowered urinary calcium; 40 mmol of either salt taken fasted raised plasma potassium similarly, with citrate driving more cell uptake. The potassium-switch mechanism is NCC dephosphorylation within 15 minutes of a load, NCC activation by low potassium that retains sodium at the cost of pressure, and NCC as the potassium sensor.


??? note "Tier 4 — Claims That Fail the Evidence (3 findings)"

    **40. "Cellular transport technology" and "medical-grade hydration" describe why ORS works in cholera, not a benefit at breakfast; isotonic sports drinks are not matched to the body, and Gatorade measures hypertonic.**

    Reduced-osmolarity ORS cut unscheduled intravenous rehydration in children with diarrhoea (OR 0.59 to 0.61) and raised biochemical hyponatraemia in cholera (RR 1.67); the optimal beverage depends on the source of the loss. Multiple transportable carbohydrates raise water absorption by solute flux rather than osmolality; 6 % carbohydrate drinks at 197 to 414 mOsm/kg were absorbed no differently from water during exercise; energy density, not osmolality, governs gastric emptying. Measured osmolalities were Gatorade 327 (hypertonic), PowerAde 281 and a hypotonic drink 218 mOsm/kg; under the convention of isotonic 275 to 300, hypotonic drinks preserved plasma volume best (−6.3 % vs −8.7 % isotonic, −7.5 % water). After exercise, plain water prompts diuresis and sodium at 61 vs 23 mmol/L halves urine output.

    **41. "Water first thing in the morning boosts metabolism and flushes toxins" rests on one unreplicated 14-person study; the kidney dilutes urine and nothing else.**

    Five hundred mL of water raised metabolic rate by about 100 kJ (24 kcal), 40 % of it from warming the water, in 14 adults; a better-controlled crossover found no thermogenesis after distilled water or saline, only after sucrose. No retrieved study tests a toxin-flushing effect. Pre-meal water gave about 2 kg extra weight loss in 48 older adults over 12 weeks, with the acute satiety effect fading by week 12.

    **42. "Citrate buffers lactic acid" and "we leave calcium out to protect magnesium" are dose errors; the 99 mg potassium cap is a tablet-form rule, not a dose hazard.**

    Ergogenic sodium bicarbonate is 0.3 g/kg (22.5 g, 268 mmol of sodium, 6.2 g) taken 60 to 180 minutes before 30-second to 12-minute efforts, giving +1.7 % in sprints with sodium citrate at 0.0 %; blood bicarbonate rises 5 to 12 mmol/L and peaks 75 to 240 minutes after capsules; citrate needs 500 mg/kg and causes dose-dependent gastrointestinal symptoms. A sachet with 1 g of sodium as citrate carries about 43 mmol of alkali, a sixth of the minimum ergogenic load; chloride is the principal extracellular anion and dietary NaCl nudges plasma bicarbonate down chronically, neither measurable at sachet dose. One thousand mg/day of calcium did not change magnesium absorption (50 vs 55 %) in the one controlled balance study. The US rule limiting over-the-counter potassium to under 100 mg per solid dose followed small-bowel ulceration and perforation from solid KCl tablets, with stenoses in French pharmacovigilance; dissolved potassium in water is a banana's worth.


??? note "Practical — what to actually do (3 findings)"

    **43. The arithmetic of a 1,000 mg sodium morning sachet: it adds 28 % to a 9 g salt day, takes a typical US intake to 4,608 mg (twice the 2,300 mg chronic disease risk reduction intake), and lands inside PURE's disputed reference band.**

    On the measured US mean of 3,608 mg the sachet gives 4,608 mg/day, 2.0 times the CDRR, 2.3 times the WHO <2,000 mg maximum (fact sheet, not PubMed) and 3.1 times the AHA optimum; it is 43.5 mmol, equal to 2.54 g of table salt, about 140 % of an overnight urinary output of roughly 31 mmol at a 9 g salt intake, and less than two of the ±25 mmol noise units of excretion at fixed intake. The salt substitute that cut mortality sits near 2.25:1 sodium to potassium by mass; a 1,000/200 mg stick is 5:1. Label values are the manufacturer's and not PubMed-verifiable.

    **44. If the drink is kept, mix the stick in at least 500 mL, keep total supplemental magnesium under the 250 to 350 mg upper levels, and treat added potassium as a drug if kidneys or RAAS-blocking drugs are involved.**

    A 1,000/200/60 mg stick in 500 mL computes to about 200 mOsm/kg and is hypotonic; in 250 mL it is about 400 mOsm/kg, and hypertonic drinks cause transient net secretion into the gut until diluted. Laxative magnesium is about 900 mg/day as oxide and the supplement-trial GI event rate at 300 to 400 mg is 11 to 37 %; erythritol causes no symptoms at 20 to 35 g and xylitol loosens stools at 35 to 50 g, an order of magnitude above sweetener doses. People with eGFR under about 45, on an ACE inhibitor or ARB plus a mineralocorticoid antagonist, or with heart failure or diabetic kidney disease were excluded from every salt-substitute trial and are the group in whom 40 mmol/day caused hyperkalaemia. Hypertensive, older and non-white adults carry the steepest sodium-pressure slopes.

    **45. For the sodium-potassium ratio the levers are less sodium and one to two servings of potassium-dense food; for sweat sodium, a test is worth doing only in the long-hot-salty corner, and a patch cannot measure sweat rate.**

    Potato potassium is as bioavailable as a gluconate supplement (>94 % absorbed), and the trial-tested salt substitute moved sodium down 0.5 g and potassium up 0.5 g a day; a 200 mg sachet is 48 % of a banana. Sweat testing is worth doing only with ≥1.5 L/h, ≥40 mmol/L and ≥80 % replacement; raw patches overestimate whole-body sodium (59 vs 41 mEq/L), corrected values carry ±12 to 17 mmol/L limits of agreement, regional patches cannot estimate sweat rate and body-mass change is the standard, and the resting pilocarpine test over-, matches and under-estimates exercise sweat at low, moderate and high intensity in a company-authored comparison. Of 344 endurance athletes 74 to 82 % believed sodium prevents EAH and cramps and that sweat testing helps. At about $1.50 a stick against under $0.30 for salt plus a banana (retail, confirm), one stick a day is about $550 a year.
