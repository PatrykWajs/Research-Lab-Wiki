# Magnesium: What It Actually Does vs What People Think

> An essential mineral with a genuinely real dietary gap — where almost every benefit turns out to be deficiency-correction, the strongest evidence is for a use nobody buys a supplement for, and the popular claims mostly don't survive.

*Independent research, 14 August 2026. 151 cited sources (147 PubMed-verified — every PMID title-matched against PubMed). Not medical advice.*

## Start here

!!! quote "Bottom line"
    **Magnesium is an essential mineral with a genuinely real dietary gap — but almost every benefit is deficiency-correction, not "boosting." Its strongest evidence is intravenous hospital medicine (the opposite of what supplements are sold for), and the popular claims you actually buy it for — cramps, the "sleep form," testosterone — are weak or refuted. Status predicts health; a pill mostly helps if you're low.**

**Myths vs. reality**

| "Magnesium stops muscle cramps" | Refuted for adult leg cramps (Cochrane + independent trials null); the one positive trial was manufacturer-funded |

| "Rub on magnesium oil / soak in Epsom salts" | The one skin-cream trial missed significance; the Epsom claim was never peer-reviewed |
| "Take it for your heart" | Its strongest evidence is IV magnesium in the hospital (eclampsia, torsades); oral heart benefit is small and deficiency-gated |
| "Magnesium oxide is useless" | Poorly absorbed (~4%), but a genuine, guideline-recommended constipation laxative |

**What you actually learn**

- **The best magnesium evidence is intravenous, in a hospital** — halving eclampsia seizures, protecting preterm babies' brains, stopping a lethal heart rhythm — not a daily capsule.
- **The dietary gap is real** (unlike vitamin D's cutoff artifact): ~half of adults under-eat magnesium, and blood tests genuinely miss tissue depletion.
- **Almost every oral benefit is deficiency-correction** — blood pressure, blood sugar and mortality signals all concentrate in the deficient or diseased, and "more is better" is false (risk is U-shaped).
- **The consumer claims mostly fail:** cramps refuted, "glycinate for sleep" unproven, L-threonate is rodent + industry data, testosterone and transdermal are myths.
- **One under-sold real use:** magnesium oxide is a legitimate constipation laxative.

!!! tip "What this means for you"
    - **Eat magnesium before you supplement it** — leafy greens, pumpkin seeds, almonds, legumes, whole grains and dark chocolate close most of the gap.
    - **Supplement only for a real reason:** a plausible intake gap, or a depleting condition (type 2 diabetes, heavy alcohol, long-term acid-blockers or diuretics, heavy sweating).
    - **If you do:** a modest dose (~200 mg), citrate or glycinate, with food, split if it loosens your stool — don't pay for exotic forms.
    - **Don't take it for** cramps, testosterone, "detox," or because a particular form is marketed as a sleep drug.
    - **Check with a clinician first** if you have kidney disease or take regular medication.

## What this review covered

Magnesium is one of the most claim-laden supplements on the shelf: "everyone's deficient," "glycinate is the sleep form," "it stops cramps," "it boosts testosterone," "spray it on your skin," "soak in an Epsom bath." Two structured evidence sweeps (a broad core pass and a deepen pass for the newest 2024–26 trials and industry-independent replications) tested each claim against the peer-reviewed literature — meta-analyses, randomized trials, Mendelian-randomization studies and large cohorts. Every one of the 147 PubMed citations was title-matched against PubMed before use. The result is a genuinely different shape from the [vitamin D](vitamin-d.md) story, and it's worth being precise about *how* it differs.

## Tier 1 — The strongest evidence

**Magnesium is genuinely essential, and blood tests hide most of it.** It's a cofactor for over 600 enzymes, and ATP only works as Mg-ATP[^S1]. About 99% of your magnesium is *inside* cells and bone; only ~1% is in the blood, where it's tightly defended — so a "normal" serum magnesium can't rule out tissue depletion[^S2]. That measurement gap is why intake and status keep looking more important than supplement trials do.

**The best magnesium evidence is intravenous, in a hospital — not a pill.** IV magnesium sulfate more than halves eclampsia seizures in pregnancy (the 10,141-woman MAGPIE trial, RR 0.42)[^S3] and beats the standard anti-seizure drugs for recurrence[^S4]; it's first-line for torsades de pointes, a dangerous heart rhythm, even when blood magnesium is normal[^S5]; and it's a proven add-on in severe asthma attacks[^S6]. The single highest-certainty magnesium indication anywhere is **antenatal magnesium to protect preterm babies' brains** — the updated Cochrane review grades it HIGH: cerebral palsy down 29%, severe brain hemorrhage down 24%[^S7]. None of this is a daily capsule. The irony is worth saying plainly: magnesium's best evidence is for uses almost nobody buys a supplement for.

**The dietary gap is real — which is the key difference from vitamin D.** About 68% of US adults consume less than the magnesium reference intake[^S8][^S9]. Unlike vitamin D's "everyone is deficient" scare (which was mostly a moved goalpost), this is a genuine dietary shortfall.

**But "everyone is clinically deficient" still overstates it.** Actual measured low blood magnesium is only ~2–15% of the general population[^S10] — higher in type 2 diabetes (~32%)[^S11], intensive care, and people on certain drugs. The scary figure conflates *"not eating the recommended amount"* with *"clinically deficient."*

**Leg cramps — the flagship reason people take it — is refuted.** The Cochrane review (11 trials) found magnesium "unlikely to provide clinically meaningful" cramp relief[^S12], an independent trial in a major journal was null[^S13], and so was an independent 2026 trial (compression stockings worked; magnesium didn't)[^S14]. The one clearly positive cramp trial was funded by the product's manufacturer[^S15]. It survives only for *pregnancy* cramps.

**Safety is a kidney story, not a poison story.** The famous 350 mg/day ceiling is the *diarrhea* threshold for supplements, not a toxic dose, and food magnesium has no limit — healthy kidneys just excrete the excess[^S16]. Genuinely dangerous magnesium build-up happens almost only with impaired kidneys plus magnesium-containing laxatives or antacids[^S17][^S18].

**One oral use marketing under-sells: constipation.** Magnesium oxide (~1.5 g/day) roughly tripled the response rate over placebo and matched a stimulant laxative in double-blind trials[^S19][^S20], and it's now conditionally recommended in the 2023 gastroenterology guideline[^S21]. This is a plumbing (osmotic) effect in the gut that works whether or not you're deficient.

**Forms matter less than folklore says.** Magnesium oxide is poorly absorbed (~4%)[^S22] — inefficient, but not "useless," since it's ~60% elemental magnesium. Citrate and chelated forms absorb better than oxide[^S23], yet the differences between forms shrink at steady state when you take them with food[^S24] — and "premium" liposomal magnesium showed *no* absorption advantage even in its own maker's trial[^S25].

## Tier 2 — Real but bounded (all deficiency-gated)

**Blood pressure: a small drop, and only where there's a deficit.** Supplements lower blood pressure ~2 mmHg on average[^S26], and the 2025 meta-analysis pinned it down: up to −7.7 mmHg in medicated hypertensives and −6 in the magnesium-deficient, but **essentially nothing in normotensive, replete adults**, and bigger at ≥400 mg/day[^S27].

**Type 2 diabetes: the food, not the pill.** Higher *dietary* magnesium robustly predicts lower diabetes risk (−14% per 100 mg/day)[^S28], but *supplements* barely move glucose (fasting glucose −0.58 mmol/L, HbA1c ~−0.16%)[^S29], and the largest 2026 insulin-resistance meta-analysis was actually null, with the effect depending on your baseline[^S30].

**The cardiovascular story is genuinely contested.** Mendelian randomization once supported a causal effect on ischemic stroke[^S31], but composite cardiovascular disease is null in dose-response meta-analysis[^S32], and — the tell — *dietary* magnesium tracks lower mortality while *supplemental* magnesium shows nothing[^S33], the classic signature of a nutrient that marks a healthier overall diet. In 1.28 million diabetic veterans, benefit was **deficiency-gated and risk was U-shaped** (both low and high were harmful; replacement helped only the low)[^S34]. Meanwhile a magnesium-*depletion* score predicts ~2.5–2.9× mortality across many cohorts[^S35] — a powerful marker that, as with omega-3, doesn't prove pills fix the outcome.

**Migraine and depression: modest, and bounded.** Oral magnesium has guideline-level (but dated) support for migraine prevention[^S36][^S37], though the definitive Cochrane review isn't finished[^S38]; depression shows a fragile pooled signal in *diagnosed* patients only, built on small trials — the most-cited of which was unblinded[^S39][^S40].

**Depletion is real — and one drug reverses it.** Long-term acid-blocking PPIs are linked to low magnesium[^S41], as are diuretics, metformin and heavy drinking. Usefully, the newer SGLT2-inhibitor diabetes drugs do the opposite — they *raise* magnesium[^S42].

## Tier 3 — Weak, unproven, or over-sold

**Sleep — a small, deficiency-gated signal, and no "special form."** The only meta-analysis found magnesium shortens *time to fall asleep* by ~17 minutes in older insomniacs but does **not** increase total sleep time, at low certainty[^S43][^S44]. And the popular belief that **magnesium glycinate is *the* sleep form has zero head-to-head evidence** — the single clean bisglycinate trial was barely significant (p=0.049, tiny effect), and its responders were the people with low dietary magnesium[^S45]. The "glycinate for sleep" reputation borrows glycine's own separate story.

**L-threonate for the brain — rodent data and manufacturer-funded trials.** The brain-magnesium boost behind L-threonate is shown only in rats[^S46]; every positive human cognition or sleep trial is small and run or funded by the compound's commercial owner[^S47][^S48][^S49], and no independent human trial exists.

**The rest of the Tier 3 list.** Anxiety benefit appears only in already-anxious groups, at poor quality[^S50]. Higher magnesium tracks slightly better bone density but **not** fewer fractures[^S51]. No performance boost in athletes who aren't deficient[^S52]. PMS mood relief is unproven[^S53], and Mendelian randomization is null for ADHD[^S54].

## Tier 4 — Myths & marketing

**"Boosts testosterone."** The whole idea rests on one small 2011 trial with no real placebo, where testosterone rose even in the exercise-only group[^S55]; the proposed hormone mechanism was shown only in a test tube[^S56]. No regulator recognizes a testosterone claim.

**ZMA.** The best-designed trial found no effect on testosterone, body composition or strength[^S57], replicated by independent 2024 crossovers[^S58]. The only positive study is non-indexed and co-authored by the ZMA patent-holder.

**Transdermal "magnesium oil" and Epsom-salt "detox."** The one human skin-cream trial missed significance on its main result[^S59], and a review judged skin absorption unsupported[^S60]; the Epsom-bath claim was never peer-reviewed. Oral is the evidenced route.

**"Your food has no magnesium anymore."** The most-cited "declining nutrients" study never actually measured magnesium[^S61], and the best critical review blames apparent declines on changed lab methods and crop breeding, not depleted soil[^S62].

**And the causal caveat that ties it together:** the impressive observational links keep dissolving under Mendelian randomization — later, larger analyses find no causal magnesium effect on diabetes, heart disease, heart failure or atrial fibrillation[^S63], against one earlier positive study[^S64]. Magnesium status is a great *marker* of health; supplementing it is mostly *correcting a deficiency.*

## Who actually benefits

- **You probably don't need a pill if** you eat leafy greens, nuts, seeds, legumes and whole grains — that closes most of the gap (magnesium is the atom at the center of chlorophyll).
- **Topping up is genuinely justified if** you have a plausible intake gap, or a depleting condition: type 2 diabetes, heavy alcohol use, long-term PPI or diuretic use, or heavy sweating.
- **If you supplement:** a modest dose (~200 mg elemental), citrate or glycinate, with food, split if it loosens your stool. Don't pay up for exotic forms.
- **Skip it for:** cramps, testosterone, "detox," and the belief that a particular form is a sleep drug.

## The bottom line

Magnesium is a real mineral with a real dietary gap and a real place — just not the one the supplement aisle sells. Its best evidence is intravenous hospital medicine; its oral benefits are modest and concentrated in people who are actually low; and its headline consumer claims (cramps, the "sleep form," testosterone, transdermal) are weak or refuted. **Eat the greens and nuts. If you have a plausible gap, a cheap citrate or glycinate with food is a sensible, low-risk top-up — for the gap, not the marketing.**

*Not medical advice. If you have kidney disease or take a PPI, diuretic or other regular medication, check with a clinician before supplementing.*

## The full findings — all 35

Every conclusion this review reached, grouped by how strongly the evidence backs it. Each finding is distilled from the fully-cited evidence above.

??? note "Tier 1 — Settled: strongest, cleanest evidence (9 findings)"

    **1. Magnesium is genuinely essential, and serum testing hides most of it.**

    Magnesium is a cofactor for over 600 enzymes and ATP is biologically active only as Mg-ATP. About 99% of body magnesium is intracellular (bone ~60%, soft tissue ~39%) and only ~1% is extracellular, with serum holding ~0.3% of the total — the anatomical reason a "normal" serum magnesium can't rule out tissue depletion.

    **2. The single strongest evidence in the whole field is IV magnesium as emergency medicine, not the supplement.**

    Intravenous magnesium sulfate more than halves eclampsia seizures (MAGPIE, N=10,141, RR 0.42), beats diazepam and phenytoin for recurrent eclamptic convulsions, is first-line for torsades de pointes even when serum magnesium is normal, and is a proven add-on in acute *severe* asthma. This is pharmacologic IV dosing under monitoring — the opposite end of the spectrum from a daily oral pill.

    **3. Antenatal magnesium for fetal neuroprotection is the highest-certainty magnesium indication on record.**

    The updated 2024 Cochrane review found antenatal magnesium sulfate cuts cerebral palsy (RR 0.71, HIGH grade) and severe intraventricular hemorrhage (RR 0.76) in babies at risk of preterm birth, reconfirmed by an independent 2025 meta-analysis.

    **4. Leg cramps — the flagship consumer claim — is refuted.**

    The Cochrane review (11 RCTs, N=735) found oral magnesium "unlikely to provide clinically meaningful" cramp prophylaxis, an independent JAMA Internal Medicine RCT was null, and so was an independent 2026 three-arm RCT (compression stockings worked; magnesium did not). The one clearly positive nocturnal-cramp trial was funded by the product's manufacturer. The effect survives only for *pregnancy* cramps, and even there trials split.

    **5. The dietary shortfall is real — which makes magnesium different from vitamin D.**

    Roughly half of Western adults consume less than the magnesium reference intake (in NHANES, 68% of US adults below the RDA, 19% below half). Unlike the vitamin-D "everyone is deficient" panic — which was mostly a cutoff artifact — the magnesium intake gap is a genuine dietary shortfall.

    **6. But "everyone is clinically deficient" still overstates it.**

    Measured hypomagnesemia (serum <0.75 mmol/L) is only ~2–15% of the general population — higher in type 2 diabetes (~32%), ICU, and PPI/diuretic users. The scary "everyone" figure conflates dietary-intake inadequacy with clinical deficiency, and no alternative status marker (RBC, ionized, the loading test) has a validated standardized cutoff.

    **7. Safety is a kidney story: the 350 mg ceiling is the diarrhea threshold, not a poison dose.**

    The 350 mg/day upper limit applies only to *supplemental* elemental magnesium and is set by the osmotic-diarrhea threshold, not systemic toxicity; food magnesium has no upper limit because healthy kidneys excrete the excess. Diarrhea is the dose-limiter. Dangerous hypermagnesemia is essentially a renal-impairment event — reduced kidney function plus magnesium-containing laxatives or antacids.

    **8. Magnesium oxide is a genuine, guideline-recommended laxative — a real oral use marketing under-sells.**

    Two double-blind RCTs found magnesium oxide (~1.5 g/day) roughly tripled the constipation-response rate over placebo and matched the stimulant laxative senna, and the 2023 AGA–ACG guideline conditionally recommends it for chronic idiopathic constipation. This is a lumen-level osmotic effect that works regardless of magnesium status — not deficiency correction.

    **9. Oxide is inefficiently absorbed but not "useless," and exotic forms matter less than folklore says.**

    Magnesium oxide's fractional absorption is only ~4%, far below soluble salts, but at ~60% elemental magnesium by weight it still delivers a usable amount. Citrate, chloride and chelates are better absorbed than oxide, yet a systematic review shows inter-form differences shrink at steady state when taken with food. "Premium" delivery forms don't reliably beat cheap salts — liposomal magnesium showed no absorption advantage even in its own maker's trial.


??? note "Tier 2 — Real but modest, and deficiency-gated (10 findings)"

    **10. Blood pressure: a small drop, cleanly concentrated in deficiency and hypertension.**

    Magnesium supplements lower blood pressure only modestly (~2 mmHg average), and the 2025 Hypertension meta-analysis (38 RCTs) localized the effect sharply: SBP up to −7.7 mmHg in medicated hypertensives and −6 in the hypomagnesemic, but ~null in normotensive replete adults, and larger at ≥400 mg/day. (One 2024 meta found some effect in normotensives too, so this isn't unanimous.)

    **11. Higher dietary magnesium tracks lower type 2 diabetes — but supplements barely move glucose.**

    Dietary magnesium intake is consistently and dose-dependently associated with lower type 2 diabetes risk (RR 0.86 per 100 mg/day). Supplements produce small glycemic gains in diabetics (fasting glucose −0.58 mmol/L, HbA1c ~−0.16%), and the largest 2026 insulin-resistance meta-analysis was actually null, with the effect gated by baseline status.

    **12. The causal cardiovascular story is genuinely contested.**

    Mendelian randomization once supported a causal protective effect on ischemic (especially cardioembolic) stroke and coronary disease, but every later, larger MR analysis found no causal link to type 2 diabetes, coronary disease, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, or cardiac structure, leaving only a weak metabolic-syndrome signal. Composite CVD is null in dose-response meta-analysis.

    **13. Dietary magnesium lowers mortality; supplemental magnesium doesn't — the healthy-diet-marker tell.**

    In a 19-cohort dose-response meta-analysis (1.17M people), *dietary* magnesium tracked lower all-cause (0.87) and cancer (0.80) mortality, but *supplemental* and *total* magnesium showed no mortality benefit — the classic signature of a nutrient that partly marks a healthier overall diet rather than causing the benefit itself.

    **14. Benefit is deficiency-correction, not dose-response — and "more is better" is false.**

    In 1.28M diabetic US Veterans, magnesium–cardiovascular risk was U-shaped (both low AND high serum magnesium were harmful), and propensity-matched oral magnesium replacement cut cardiovascular events only in the hypomagnesemic (HR 0.89), not the normomagnesemic (HR 1.07).

    **15. Magnesium depletion status strongly predicts mortality — the biomarker-vs-supplement paradox.**

    A higher Magnesium Depletion Score (a kidney-reabsorption index, not a serum level) predicts roughly 2.5–2.9× all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, reproducibly across 10+ cohorts of older adults, cancer survivors, fatty-liver, diabetes and stroke. As with omega-3, *status* is a powerful predictor — which does not prove that *supplements* fix the outcome.

    **16. Migraine prophylaxis: guideline-level but modest, and the definitive review isn't in yet.**

    Oral magnesium (~600 mg/day, which exceeds the supplemental upper limit and causes diarrhea in some) has guideline-level support for migraine prevention (an American Academy of Neurology Level-B rating, since formally retired) and a 2025 dose-response signal. IV magnesium does not reliably abort an acute attack, and the definitive Cochrane review is still only a registered protocol.

    **17. Depression: a fragile signal in the already-diagnosed only.**

    Magnesium's antidepressant evidence is a statistically significant but fragile pooled effect in *diagnosed depressed* adults, built on few small, heterogeneous trials — the most-cited of which (Tarleton) was unblinded with no placebo. Independent meta-analyses give at most a tiny effect (SMD 0.16), and it is null for perinatal depression.

    **18. Real depletion drivers: PPIs, diuretics, metformin, alcohol.**

    Long-term proton-pump-inhibitor use is associated with hypomagnesemia (pooled OR ~1.43) via impaired intestinal uptake, strongest with concurrent diuretics, and PPI-induced hypomagnesemia still causes seizures and neurological syndromes. Loop/thiazide diuretics waste magnesium, metformin is an under-recognized driver, and alcohol depletion concentrates in drinkers with liver fibrosis or kidney impairment.

    **19. SGLT2 inhibitors *raise* magnesium — a genuinely new, direction-reversing drug fact.**

    Unlike PPIs, SGLT2-inhibitor diabetes drugs increase serum magnesium by ~0.07 mmol/L as a class effect, can correct hypomagnesemia even in non-diabetics and transplant patients, and have been used to treat refractory hypomagnesemia.


??? note "Tier 3 — Weak or unproven: marketing outruns the data (8 findings)"

    **20. Sleep: a small, low-certainty signal for falling asleep faster in older insomniacs — and no more.**

    The only meta-analysis (3 RCTs, N=151 older adults) found magnesium cut sleep-onset latency ~17 minutes but did NOT change total sleep time, at low-to-very-low certainty. The foundational trial used poorly-absorbed oxide in 43 elderly insomniacs and showed no change in objective sleep time.

    **21. "Magnesium glycinate is special for sleep" has zero head-to-head evidence.**

    The single clean bisglycinate sleep RCT (N=155) was only marginally significant (ISI p=0.049, effect size d≈0.2, subjective-only), and its responders were the low-dietary-magnesium subgroup. No trial shows glycinate beats any other form for sleep — the "glycinate" reputation borrows glycine's own separate sleep story.

    **22. L-threonate cognition and sleep: rodent brain data, and every human trial is manufacturer-linked.**

    The brain-magnesium elevation behind L-threonate is shown only in rats — no human study has measured raised brain or CSF magnesium. Every positive human trial (MMFS-01 cognition, Magtein cognition, the threonate sleep RCT) is small and run or funded by the compound's commercial owner; there is still no independent human RCT, and threonate delivers little elemental magnesium per capsule.

    **23. Anxiety, cortisol and stress: suggestive only in vulnerable groups, unproven in healthy people.**

    Magnesium's anxiety benefit appears only in already-anxiety-vulnerable samples and the evidence quality is poor; no well-powered RCT shows it blunts the cortisol/stress response in replete, healthy adults, and the most-cited stress trial was industry-funded with no placebo.

    **24. Bone: higher intake tracks modestly higher density but not fewer fractures.**

    Higher magnesium intake is associated with slightly higher bone mineral density in observational cohorts (~3% in the WHI top-intake group) but NOT with lower fracture risk in that cohort or in meta-analysis; one cohort dissents, no RCT proves benefit, and Mendelian randomization even suggested lower lumbar bone density.

    **25. No ergogenic effect in already-replete athletes.**

    Any performance signal is confined to magnesium-deficient, older or untrained people; there is no good evidence of a supra-physiologic strength or endurance boost in replete athletes.

    **26. PMS mood is unproven; the menstrual signal is for period pain, not premenstrual mood.**

    Serum magnesium is not reliably lower in PMS, and a 2025 systematic review found insufficient evidence that magnesium relieves psychological PMS symptoms (the supporting evidence is one small 1991 trial). The better signal is for dysmenorrhea (period-cramp) pain.

    **27. ADHD: unproven, and Mendelian randomization is null.**

    Magnesium's ADHD evidence is low-quality and usually confounded by co-administered vitamin B6, and a 2025 Mendelian-randomization study found no causal magnesium effect on ADHD (it flagged a novel, watch-list magnesium–autism signal instead).


??? note "Tier 4 — Myths and refuted claims (4 findings)"

    **28. "Magnesium boosts testosterone" rests on a single flawed trial.**

    The entire claim comes from one small 2011 trial with no true placebo arm and unreported baseline magnesium status, in which testosterone rose even in the exercise-only group — so exercise, not magnesium, plausibly drove it. The proposed SHBG-binding mechanism was shown only in a test tube, the human link is cross-sectional, and no regulator recognizes a testosterone claim. Only deficiency-correction is plausible.

    **29. ZMA is a clean null, replicated three times.**

    The best-designed ZMA (zinc-magnesium aspartate) trial found no effect on testosterone, body composition or strength, and two independent 2024 crossovers found no sleep or performance benefit either. The only positive ZMA study is non-indexed and co-authored by the ZMA patent-holder.

    **30. Transdermal "magnesium oil" and Epsom-salt "detox" are unproven.**

    The only human transdermal-cream pilot missed significance on its primary serum endpoint (p=0.29), reaching it only in a subgroup, and a topical-magnesium review judged skin absorption unsupported. The flagship Epsom-salt absorption claim was never peer-reviewed or indexed, and "detox" has no defined mechanism or endpoint. Oral remains the evidenced route.

    **31. "Your food has no magnesium anymore" (soil depletion) is mostly a mis-citation.**

    The most-cited "declining nutrients" study never actually measured magnesium, and the most rigorous review attributes apparent historical mineral declines mainly to changed analytical methods and high-yield-crop "dilution," not depleted soil. Some datasets show declines, but the confident claim outruns the data.


??? note "Practical — what to actually do (4 findings)"

    **32. Food first — and it's easy to see where magnesium lives.**

    Magnesium is the central atom of chlorophyll, so leafy greens are rich, along with pumpkin seeds, almonds, legumes, whole grains and dark chocolate; refining strips ~80% of a grain's magnesium. A whole-food diet closes most of the intake gap.

    **33. If you supplement: citrate or glycinate, split-dosed, with food — don't chase exotic forms.**

    Aim for a modest elemental dose (~200 mg), take it with food and split it to blunt the laxative effect; citrate and bisglycinate are sensible, well-absorbed, well-tolerated picks, and form differences shrink at steady state. Oxide is fine only for cheap bulk dosing or when you actually want the laxative effect.

    **34. The real reason to top up is a plausible gap or a depleting condition — not the marketing claims.**

    Benefits cluster in people who are magnesium-insufficient or already symptomatic. A genuine intake gap (few greens/nuts/legumes), heavy sweating, or a depleting drug/condition (type 2 diabetes, alcohol, long-term PPI or diuretic use) is the evidence-based reason to supplement — not sleep, testosterone, or cramp marketing.

    **35. Stack cautions: spacing, kidneys, and drug interactions.**

    Space magnesium several hours from tetracycline/fluoroquinolone antibiotics and bisphosphonates (it chelates them); avoid magnesium-containing laxatives/antacids in kidney impairment (hypermagnesemia risk); expect more depletion on a PPI or diuretic — and note an SGLT2-inhibitor diabetes drug raises magnesium instead.
