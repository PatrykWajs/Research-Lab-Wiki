# Extra Virgin Olive Oil & Oleocanthal: What It Actually Does vs What People Think

> A good default fat with a real but small phenolic bonus. A tablespoon holds 0.3 to 3.4 % of an ibuprofen tablet's worth of oleocanthal, and the parent molecule has never been measured in human blood. The heart-outcome trials tested a whole Mediterranean diet, not the oil; against refined olive oil, EVOO lowers oxidised LDL and takes about 3 mmHg off systolic pressure; inflammation, pain and brain claims have no human support at food doses.

*Literature reviewed to 25 October 2026. 304 sources, every identifier resolved against PubMed.*

**What you actually learn**

- **The pharmacokinetics decide the ibuprofen story:** hydroxytyrosol and tyrosol are absorbed but circulate about 98 % conjugated, free hydroxytyrosol peaks near 0.03 µM after 25 mL, and the cell studies used 10 to 100 µM of a parent compound that has not been found in human plasma.
- **The one reproducible acute effect is on platelets:** 40 mL of oleocanthal-rich oil dampened ex vivo platelet aggregation for a few hours in trials of 9 and 10 people, and the authors of the first trial concluded it did not run through COX.
- **The phenolic-specific effects are small and real:** against refined olive oil, oxidised LDL falls (SMD −0.25 to −0.68), systolic pressure falls about 3 mmHg, HDL rises 1 to 2 mg/dL and LDL falls 4 to 5 mg/dL only at very high phenolic doses.
- **The hard outcomes belong to the diet:** PREDIMED and CORDIOPREV tested whole Mediterranean patterns built on EVOO; CORDIOPREV gave 9.6 fewer events per 1,000 person-years in coronary men against a real low-fat diet, with olive-oil-foundation co-funding.
- **Cohorts show a curve that flattens early:** olive oil intake tracks 15 to 20 % lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, with most of it reached by about 18 to 22 g/day; in the US cohorts the advantage is over butter, margarine and mayonnaise, not over other vegetable oils.
- **The label is a moving target:** phenolic content runs from near zero in refined oil to several thousand mg/kg in rare Greek oils, premium oils average 343 to 421 mg/kg, and storage, light, oxygen and heat all erode it.
- **Brain claims are mouse claims:** at least three laboratories report less amyloid and tau in transgenic mice, but the human trials are small pilots and the largest trial that supplied daily EVOO was null.
- **Safety at food doses is good:** no documented allergy to the oil itself, no warfarin interaction study, and contaminants concentrated in pomace oil; retail grade fraud mainly costs the phenolic dose.

!!! tip "What this means for you"
    - **Use EVOO as your default added fat:** the lipid and cohort benefits are largest when it replaces butter, margarine, mayonnaise or other saturated and processed fats, not when it is added on top.
    - **Take the phenolic dose raw:** a hot sauté removes about three-quarters of the phenolics, so dressings, finishing and an uncooked spoon keep the most; cooking with it is still safe.
    - **Buy for freshness and a stated method:** choose the latest harvest date, a phenolic value that names its method and test date, and tin, bag-in-box or other opaque packaging; finish an opened bottle within weeks.
    - **Raise the dose with a better oil, not a bigger pour:** a tablespoon of a typical good EVOO carries about 3 to 7 mg of phenolics and 120 kcal; a higher-phenolic oil gives more phenolics for the same calories.
    - **Do not expect a painkiller, an anti-inflammatory or a brain treatment:** no human trial at a tablespoon dose supports those uses, and pure oleocanthal supplements have no human safety or efficacy data.
    - **There is no evidence for a fasted shot:** no trial compared an empty-stomach dose with oil taken with food, and a fasted bolus contracts the gallbladder; if you have gallstones, take it with a meal.
    - **Mention a switch to very high-oleocanthal oil if you take antiplatelet drugs or anticoagulants:** the platelet effect is measurable at 40 mL, and no study has tested it on top of those drugs.

## 1. Executive Summary and the Chain of Claims

Extra virgin olive oil (EVOO) is sold on a chain of four claims. First, that it contains oleocanthal, a phenolic dialdehyde that "works like ibuprofen". Second, that its phenolics are approved by the EU as protective. Third, that PREDIMED proved it prevents heart attacks and strokes. Fourth, that a daily spoonful of a "high-phenolic" Greek oil is a medicine for the arteries and the brain. This synthesis tests each link against seven evidence sweeps (phenolic chemistry and the EU claim; oleocanthal pharmacology; cardiovascular hard outcomes; endothelial function and blood pressure; lipids, LDL oxidation, glycemia and inflammatory markers; neuroprotection and cognition; sourcing, cooking, storage and safety). 306 candidate PubMed records were resolved against the NCBI esummary database; 304 are cited here, and the two retracted PREDIMED papers are named only as retraction facts.

The short version has five parts.

**The chemistry is real and the dose is small.** The authorised EU claim is a biomarker claim, "olive oil polyphenols contribute to the protection of blood lipids from oxidative stress", at 5 mg of hydroxytyrosol and derivatives per 20 g of oil, which converts to 250 mg/kg [^S36] [^S45]. One 13.5 g tablespoon reaches that 5 mg only if the oil holds about 370 mg/kg when you eat it. The same 30 oils sent to five accredited laboratories "qualified" 1 to 21 times out of 30 depending on the protocol and correction factor [^S37], so a label number without its method is hard to read. Typical premium oils average 343 to 421 mg/kg by LC-MS/MS [^S51], Greek qNMR samples 483 mg/kg [^S45], refined oil essentially zero [^S42], and phenolics fall by roughly 42 to 46 % in a year at room temperature [^S44] [^S45].

**"Natural ibuprofen" is a mechanism label, not a dose.** Oleocanthal inhibits both cyclooxygenase isoforms in a cell-free assay and stings the throat through the same TRPA1 channel as ibuprofen [^S54] [^S57]. A tablespoon of a high-phenolic oil carries about 3.4 to 6.8 mg of it, 0.3 to 3.4 % of a 200 to 400 mg ibuprofen tablet by mass. Parent oleocanthal has never been measured in human plasma, and in animals it is not detected because its aldehydes react with glycine and proteins [^S81] [^S50] [^S46]. The most reproducible human effect is an acute dampening of ex vivo platelet aggregation after 40 mL of an oleocanthal-rich oil, in trials of 9 and 10 people [^S70] [^S87]. The most recent EVOO-only meta-analysis found no effect on CRP, IL-6 or TNF-α [^S89], and no randomized trial of EVOO on pain exists.

**The hard-outcome evidence is for a dietary package, not for the oil.** PREDIMED's 2013 report was retracted for randomisation failures and republished in 2018 with almost unchanged estimates (HR 0.69 for the EVOO arm, 0.72 for nuts) [^S116]. Its benefit was carried mostly by stroke (24 down to 14 per 1,000 over 4.8 years), while cardiovascular death and total mortality were not clearly reduced [^S123]. CORDIOPREV found 28.1 vs 37.7 events per 1,000 person-years against a real low-fat diet in coronary patients, in men only, with olive-oil-sector co-funding [^S128]. Cohorts consistently associate olive oil with 15 to 20 % lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, with most of the association reached by about 18 to 22 g/day [^S135] [^S131]; in the US cohorts the advantage is over butter, margarine and mayonnaise, not over other vegetable oils [^S125] [^S130].

**The phenolic-specific human effects are small and real.** Holding the fat constant, high-phenolic oil lowers oxidised LDL most consistently (SMD −0.25 to −0.68) [^S166] [^S176] [^S178], lowers systolic pressure by about 3 mmHg against refined olive oil [^S178], raises HDL cholesterol by 1 to 2 mg/dL, and lowers LDL cholesterol by about 4 to 5 mg/dL only at high phenolic doses [^S227]. HDL efflux improved in one EUROLIVE sub-study and did not replicate in OLIVAUS [^S205] [^S226].

**Brain claims rest on mice.** The amyloid-clearance mechanism (P-glycoprotein and LRP1 up-regulation at the blood-brain barrier) comes from one commercially linked laboratory network using injected pure oleocanthal [^S238] [^S242]. The MIND trial supplied about two tablespoons of EVOO a day inside a whole-diet change and found no cognitive or MRI benefit at three years [^S259]. The small MCI trials are pilots, one with refined oil improving the dementia rating at least as much as EVOO [^S257].

For a daily tablespoon user, the evidence supports EVOO as the main added fat, used raw for the phenolic dose and replacing butter, margarine or mayonnaise; it does not support a tablespoon "shot" as an analgesic, anti-inflammatory, amyloid-clearing or stand-alone cardioprotective intervention.

### 1.1 How the evidence is layered

Olive-oil claims routinely jump between five layers of evidence, and most disagreements dissolve once the layer is named.

1. **Test tube and cell culture.** Purified oleocanthal, oleacein or hydroxytyrosol at 1 to 100 µM, often in amine-containing media where the dialdehydes are unstable [^S90]. This layer establishes what the molecules can do.
2. **Animals.** Injected or gavaged pure compounds at 5 to 20 mg/kg, or EVOO mixed into chow [^S242] [^S263]. This layer establishes what happens when the compound reaches the target, often by bypassing the gut.
3. **Human pharmacokinetics.** What reaches plasma after real oil: conjugated hydroxytyrosol and tyrosol at low micromolar levels, free hydroxytyrosol in the tens of nanomolar, and no measurable parent oleocanthal [^S9] [^S30] [^S81]. This layer decides whether layers 1 and 2 are relevant to a person.
4. **Human biomarker trials.** Weeks-long crossovers comparing oils that differ only in phenolics, or olive oil against other fats, on oxidised LDL, lipids, blood pressure and FMD [^S13] [^S178]. This layer gives the real, small effects.
5. **Human hard outcomes.** Diet-pattern trials (PREDIMED, CORDIOPREV, MIND) and cohorts [^S116] [^S128] [^S259] [^S130]. This layer gives the large relative effects, attributable to the whole pattern or to what olive oil replaces.

The marketing claim that "oleocanthal is natural ibuprofen" lives in layer 1. The claim that "olive oil prevents heart attacks" lives in layer 5. The phenolic-specific evidence that links them is layer 4, and it is the smallest in effect size.

## 2. Phenolic Chemistry, the EU Health Claim, Measurement and Bioavailability

### 2.1 What is in the bottle

EVOO is about three-quarters oleic acid, with a minor phenolic fraction dominated by secoiridoids. Two families matter. The hydroxytyrosol family comes from oleuropein: oleacein (the dialdehydic form), the oleuropein aglycone isomers and free hydroxytyrosol. The tyrosol family comes from ligstroside: oleocanthal (the dialdehyde), the ligstroside aglycone isomers and free tyrosol. Lignans (pinoresinol, 1-acetoxypinoresinol) and flavones (luteolin, apigenin) sit alongside. Oleocanthal is the compound that produces the peppery catch at the back of the throat: the fraction containing deacetoxy-ligstroside aglycone "produced a strong burning pungent sensation at the back of the throat", while the oleuropein analogue gave only a slight sensation on the tongue [^S53].

### 2.2 The EU claim: wording, threshold and basis

Commission Regulation (EU) No 432/2012 authorises one function claim for olive-oil phenolics: "Olive oil polyphenols contribute to the protection of blood lipids from oxidative stress." The condition of use is at least 5 mg of hydroxytyrosol and its derivatives (e.g. oleuropein complex and tyrosol) per 20 g of olive oil, with consumer information that the effect is obtained with 20 g/day. The analytical literature quotes the text and notes that "no method was specifically suggested or adopted in the regulation" [^S36]; 5 mg per 20 g is universally converted to 250 mg/kg [^S45], and the claim sentence appears verbatim in storage-kinetics work [^S27]. Three features matter. It is a biomarker claim, not a heart-disease, inflammation or pain claim. The threshold is a minimum per 20 g, so "≥250 mg/kg" is the accurate concentration and ">250" a slight misstatement. And the compound family is defined loosely: tyrosol is not a chemical derivative of hydroxytyrosol, which in practice brings the ligstroside family, oleocanthal included, under the claim, and analysts have asked for the terminology to change [^S25]. The olive-oil sector limited use of the claim "to avoid legal implications" because no official method exists [^S36].

The claim followed a 2011 EFSA opinion. The opinion itself could not be retrieved in this run, so its evidence list is not verified here. PubMed confirms that EFSA approved an LDL-oxidation claim [^S19] and holds the human dose-response data on that endpoint:

- **EUROLIVE** [^S13]. 200 healthy men in six centres, 25 mL/day of three oils differing only in phenolic content (2.7, 164 and 366 mg/kg), three-week crossover periods. HDL cholesterol rose linearly (+0.025, +0.032 and +0.045 mmol/L), triglycerides fell about 0.05 mmol/L with every oil, and oxidised LDL changed by +1.21, −1.48 and −3.21 U/L; only the high-phenolic oil's interval excluded zero.
- Sub-studies of the same oils: apolipoprotein B-100 fell 5.94 %, total LDL particles 11.9 % and small LDL 15.3 % after the high-phenolic oil against rises after the low one [^S26]; systemic LDL oxidation and atherogenic gene expression in blood mononuclear cells fell [^S19]; autoantibodies against oxidised LDL rose dose-dependently [^S17].
- The SOLOS pilot (30 volunteers, 0 to 150 mg/kg oils) found falling oxidised LDL and rising ex vivo LDL resistance with phenolic content [^S11]; a four-day trial on a very-low-antioxidant diet found dose-dependent falls in oxidised LDL, 8-oxo-dG and urinary malondialdehyde [^S12]; a 40 mL postprandial load showed dose-dependent phenol absorption and less fat-load oxidative stress with higher-phenolic oils [^S14]; and phenol-enriched oils improved NMR lipoprotein ratios in hypercholesterolaemic adults [^S29].

The evidence also contains well-controlled nulls. A Wageningen crossover in 46 adults at 69 g/day with an 18 mg/day phenol contrast found no change in LDL or HDL oxidation resistance, with "all 95% confidence intervals enclosed zero" [^S2]. A placebo-controlled postprandial study found the same LDL lag-time rise after placebo as after phenols, a meal or time effect [^S3]. A Cretan smoker crossover at 70 g/day with an 18.6 mg/day contrast, sponsored by Unilever Research, found no difference in fasting oxidation markers [^S6]. The positive work comes largely from one research network, and the review proposing health-claim use is co-authored by the EUROLIVE investigators [^S22]. That is a fact about the evidence base, not evidence of error.

### 2.3 The measurement problem

Labs report the "same" number four non-interchangeable ways: Folin-Ciocalteu colorimetry (non-selective reducing capacity), the IOC HPLC biophenol method (peaks expressed as tyrosol), acid hydrolysis followed by HPLC (released hydroxytyrosol plus tyrosol), and direct quantification of intact secoiridoids by LC-MS/MS or quantitative NMR.

- **Inter-laboratory test** [^S37]. 30 authentic oils, five liquid-chromatography protocols and one ¹H-NMR protocol in five expert laboratories. With correction factors, 16 of 30 oils reached 5 mg/20 g; without them, 1 of 30; another laboratory's hydrolysis method qualified 21 of 30; the accredited qNMR laboratory read on average 1.77 mg/20 g higher. Mean pairwise biases ran from −2.07 to +1.93 mg/20 g, about 40 % of the threshold.
- The correction factors themselves (2.2 for hydroxytyrosol, 2.5 for tyrosol) convert released alcohols to complex-form mass [^S36]; hydrolysis protocols differ by at least 50 %, and 5 mg/20 g was met "only when suitable correction factors were introduced" [^S25].
- Summed individual LC-MS values ran 1.9 to 3.0 times the global methods across 50 oils [^S35]; Folin and HPLC totals correlate (R = 0.95) without agreeing in absolute mg/kg [^S23]; by Folin 59 % of oils fell below 250 mg/kg against 26 % by LC-MS/MS [^S51]; methanol extraction creates secoiridoid acetal artefacts [^S32]; a validated hydrolysis method found Italian EVOOs below the threshold [^S34]; and a 2 M HCl hydrolysis method reaches a limit of quantification of 0.8 mg/kg [^S21].
- Quantitative NMR measures oleocanthal and oleacein directly. Across 175 Greek and Californian monovarietal oils each ranged from non-detectable to 355 mg/kg and their sum from 0 to 501 mg/kg, with earlier harvest giving more [^S20]; across 340 oils of 23 varieties the four main secoiridoids summed to as much as 1,534 mg/kg, and some aglycone isomers reported by chromatography were shown to form on the column [^S24].

The practical consequence: a Greek NMR certificate showing 1,000+ mg/kg and a hydrolysis result showing a few hundred can describe similar oils, because intact molecules weigh 2.2 to 2.5 times the released alcohols.

### 2.4 How much is in commercial oils

- 1,239 Spanish EVOOs over two seasons ranged from 1 to 2,850 mg/kg by LC-MS/MS; the abstract's "4.6 and 23.1%" share relative to 250 mg/kg is ambiguous and is not quoted as a pass or fail rate [^S38].
- 200 premium competition oils averaged 421 and 343 mg/kg in two years, and 16 to 27 % would not qualify for the claim [^S51].
- 5,764 Greek samples analysed by qNMR over eleven years averaged 483 mg/kg (Koroneiki 434 mg/kg) with a maximum of 4,003; 74 % exceeded 250 mg/kg; the authors propose "high-phenolic" above 500 mg/kg and "exceptionally high" above 1,200 mg/kg, which are one group's proposals on samples sent for testing, not legal categories [^S45].
- Ripening and longer malaxation lower total phenolics, with variety-specific behaviour [^S39]; Chalkidiki cultivars can meet the claim, but mill practice can undermine it [^S47].

### 2.5 Refining

Total hydroxytyrosol and tyrosol "are completely removed in the early steps of the refining procedure", while tocopherols largely survive [^S42]. EUROLIVE's 2.7 mg/kg "low" oil is the clinical analogue [^S13]. EU retail "olive oil" is a refined-plus-virgin blend whose phenolic content depends on the virgin share; no cited study measured retail blends.

### 2.6 Absorption and what circulates

Tyrosol and hydroxytyrosol are absorbed dose-dependently and excreted as glucuronides [^S1]. In ileostomy and colon subjects given 100 mg of olive phenols, apparent absorption was at least 55 to 66 %, but only 5 to 16 mol per 100 mol was recovered in urine as tyrosol or hydroxytyrosol, and no aglycones were found in ileal effluent [^S7]. After 25 mL of virgin oil, plasma hydroxytyrosol peaked at 32 min with a half-life of 2.43 h and about 98 % conjugated; free forms were not detected [^S9]. Urinary tyrosol is the better biomarker of sustained intake [^S10]. A sensitive method later measured free hydroxytyrosol at a peak of 4.40 ng/mL (about 0.029 µM) after 25 mL of EVOO and "nearly undetectable" after ordinary olive oil [^S30]. Hydroxytyrosol sulfate, the main plasma metabolite, rose linearly to 1.35, 3.32 and 4.09 µmol/L after enriched oils at 250, 500 and 750 mg/kg [^S60]; a 961 mg/kg oil raised hydroxytyrosol sulfate and delayed its peak, with wide individual variability [^S18]; and EVOO was the best matrix for 5 mg of hydroxytyrosol compared with fortified refined, flax and grapeseed oils [^S43]. The main circulating glucuronides showed no antioxidant activity at 0.01 to 10 µM [^S16], and tyrosol is partly converted to hydroxytyrosol by CYP2A6 with genotype-dependent variability [^S31]. After 50 mL of EVOO, the biomarkers that appear are hydrogenated and glucuronidated secoiridoid metabolites, not parent compounds [^S73].

### 2.7 Reading the chemistry evidence

Three conclusions survive every method disagreement. First, phenolic content varies by two orders of magnitude between real oils, from near zero in refined grades [^S42] to several thousand mg/kg in exceptional early-harvest Greek samples [^S45], so "EVOO" names a production grade, not a dose. Second, the phenolic number on a certificate is a measurement at one moment by one method: it falls by roughly 40 to 46 % in the first year at room temperature [^S44] [^S45], faster in warm storage [^S27], and it can differ by a factor of two to three between a Folin result and an LC-MS or NMR result on the same oil [^S35] [^S51]. Third, absorption is real and dose-dependent [^S1] [^S14], but what circulates is a conjugate pool at low micromolar levels and a free fraction in the tens of nanomolar [^S9] [^S30], which has no measured antioxidant activity in vitro [^S16]. If the authorised LDL-oxidation effect is real, it is unlikely to come from free phenols scavenging radicals in plasma; the leading explanation is phenol metabolites carried on the LDL particle itself [^S200].

For a reader comparing bottles, the useful translation is per spoon, not per kilogram. At the claim threshold of 250 mg/kg, a 13.5 g tablespoon delivers about 3.4 mg; at the Greek qNMR mean of 483 mg/kg, about 6.5 mg; at 1,000 mg/kg, about 13.5 mg. A threshold oil therefore needs about 1.5 tablespoons to reach the 5 mg that the claim's own consumer information names, and after a year on a warm shelf even a 500 mg/kg oil drops to about 3.6 to 3.9 mg per tablespoon. The EUROLIVE high-phenolic arm, the source of the strongest oxidised-LDL effect, delivered about 8.4 mg/day from 25 mL [^S13]. The null trials used about three times more oil with a similar milligram contrast [^S2] [^S6], which suggests that oil volume and background diet, not only phenolic milligrams, shape whether an effect appears.

## 3. Oleocanthal Pharmacology and the Ibuprofen Comparison

### 3.1 What the 2005 Nature paper measured

The founding paper is a one-page brief communication from the Monell Chemical Senses Center [^S54]. Its abstract contains no numbers; it states that oleocanthal "has a potency and profile strikingly similar to that of ibuprofen" and that both inhibit the same cyclooxygenase enzymes. Synthetic oleocanthal confirmed the throat-irritant identity, and a purified-enzyme COX-1 and COX-2 assay compared it with ibuprofen. No person was dosed and no plasma was measured. The quoted figures come from an open-access review restating the paper's figure: at 25 µM, oleocanthal inhibited 41 to 57 % of COX activity and ibuprofen 13 to 18 %, and "assuming approximately 70% absorption, then 50 mL/day corresponds to approximately 10% the current Ibuprofen pain relieving dose" [^S64]. "Equimolar" in the original describes comparing the two drugs at equal molar concentrations, not equal inhibition of COX-1 and COX-2. Later cell work confirms COX inhibition in lung adenocarcinoma cells without an enzyme IC50 in the abstract [^S80].

### 3.2 The throat sting

Oleocanthal "selectively activates the hTRPA1 channel", rodent trigeminal excitation requires functional TRPA1, and TRPA1 is concentrated in the pharynx, matching where people feel the sting; ibuprofen excites sensory neurons through TRPA1 too [^S57]. Sensitivity "varied substantially across participants", and olive oil pungency also correlated with capsaicin, suggesting other TRPV1 agonists [^S59]. Egg-yolk and whey proteins "reduce or even eliminate" the pungency and bitterness [^S83]. The sting confirms that oleocanthal is present; it is not a calibrated dose meter, and a throat event says nothing about COX inhibition in joints or blood.

### 3.3 In vitro anti-inflammatory work, with the concentrations used

The cell literature is consistent in direction:

- Oleocanthal inhibited LPS-induced NO, MIP-1α, IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α and GM-CSF in murine macrophages and chondrocytes [^S61], and NOS2, COX-2, MMP-13, ADAMTS-5 and multiple cytokines in human osteoarthritic chondrocytes via MAPK and NF-κB [^S72].
- At 25, 50 and 100 µM it down-regulated iNOS, COX-2 and mPGES-1 in peritoneal macrophages without a clear dose-response across that range [^S76].
- At 25 µmol/L oleocanthal and oleacein attenuated NF-κB in human adipocytes, with oleocanthal toxic at ≥50 µmol/L after 24 h [^S74].
- At 100 µM, superoxide production in fresh human monocytes fell 40 % with hydroxytyrosol and 36 % with oleocanthal, while hydroxytyrosol raised TNF-α [^S62].
- Oleacein inhibits 5-lipoxygenase with an IC50 of 2 µM, a pathway ibuprofen does not block [^S65].
- Hydroxytyrosol inhibits NO and PGE2 in macrophages with IC50s of 11.4 and 19.5 µM [^S58]; at "physiologically relevant" 5 to 10 µM it lowers NO and PGE2 but not iNOS, TNF-α or IL-1β expression [^S71].
- In mice, an oleocanthal-enriched diet reduced collagen-induced arthritis damage and cytokines [^S82], and an EVOO extract reduced writhing pain behaviour while oleocanthal blocked uterine hypercontraction ex vivo [^S77].

Almost every positive cell study used 10 to 100 µM of the parent compound.

### 3.4 Pharmacokinetics: the parent compound does not circulate

"While several groups have investigated oleocanthal pharmacokinetics (PK) and disposition, none was able to detect oleocanthal in biological fluids"; it "could not be detected following its addition to any fluid containing amino acids or proteins such as plasma", because its two aldehydes react spontaneously with glycine to form "oleoglycine" [^S81]. In mice given 5 mg/kg intraperitoneally, oleocanthal was not detected at any of ten time points and 13 metabolites were identified; the abstract's oleocanthalic acid Cmax of 926 µg/mL is physically implausible for that dose and is treated as a unit error [^S50]. In rat intestinal perfusion it was poorly absorbed (mesenteric AUC 0.25 against 0.64 for levofloxacin) and mostly metabolised [^S78]; after oral dosing it was found only in stomach and intestine, with ten metabolites distributed through tissues [^S46]. Oleacein and oleocanthal also react "extremely rapid[ly]" with the primary amines in buffers such as Tris, so some cell effects may belong to amine adducts formed in the dish [^S90]. No published human study reports a plasma concentration of parent oleocanthal.

### 3.5 Human trials: platelets

- **Agrawal 2017** [^S70]. randomised crossover in 9 healthy men, 40 mL of each of three EVOOs matched for total phenolics (oleocanthal 310, 172 or <10 mg/kg). Both oleocanthal-rich oils reduced collagen-stimulated maximum platelet aggregation 2 h later, best correlated with oleocanthal intake (R = 0.56), but the authors concluded "cyclooxygenase blockade was not responsible", and only 5 of 9 were responders. Partly funded by an olive-oil producer.
- **Katsa 2024** [^S87]. 10 people with type 2 diabetes, five isocaloric bread meals with butter, butter plus 400 mg ibuprofen, or 40 mL of oil at <10, 250 or 500 mg/kg oleocanthal. The oleocanthal meals gave a sustained, dose-dependent reduction in platelet sensitivity to ADP (50 to 100 %) and TRAP (20 to 50 %), and the 500 mg/kg meal "was comparable to that of the ibuprofen meal" on that endpoint. The same meals lowered postprandial TBARS and raised red-cell GPx1 activity [^S88].
- **VOLOS** [^S55]. 40 mL/day of EVOO against refined olive oil for seven weeks in 22 dyslipidaemic patients lowered serum thromboxane B2 and raised plasma antioxidant capacity, with no lipid effect.

Platelet COX-1 is unusually sensitive to small repeated inhibition, the basis of low-dose aspirin, so an antiplatelet effect at a small dose does not imply analgesic potency. Both acute trials are tiny, single-dose and used 40 mL (about 2.7 tablespoons).

### 3.6 Human trials: inflammatory markers

The APRIL trial randomised 91 adults with obesity and prediabetes to a month of oleocanthal- and oleacein-rich EVOO against common olive oil; among inflammatory markers only interferon-γ differed (P = 0.041), while total antioxidant status rose and peroxides fell [^S86]. An uncontrolled 60-day study of 32 g/day of high-oleocanthal EVOO in 23 people with metabolic syndrome reported IL-6 falling from 2.9 to 2.0 pg/mL and TNF-α from 7.3 to 6.3 pg/mL, with no control group [^S79]. Fifteen mg/day of pure hydroxytyrosol for 16 weeks lowered oxidised LDL (p = 0.045) and IL-6 (p = 0.05) in prediabetes [^S91], a dose several times a tablespoon's content. At the pooled level, an EVOO-only meta-analysis of 33 trials found lower insulin (SMD −0.28) and HOMA-IR (SMD −0.19) but "no significant effect" on CRP, IL-6, IL-10 or TNF-α [^S89], while an older pool of olive oil in general, mostly inside dietary patterns, found CRP −0.64 mg/L and IL-6 −0.29 [^S67].

### 3.7 Pain

No randomised trial of EVOO or oleocanthal-rich oil on pain was found. Olive-derived extracts in osteoarthritis pool to SMD 0.40 from three trials, "interpreted cautiously" [^S92]; a six-month olive-leaf extract RCT in 124 people with knee pain was null on its primary score, with five Nestlé-employed authors [^S85].

### 3.8 Cancer

Oleocanthal inhibits breast cancer cells with IC50s of 10.9 to 58.4 µM and suppressed xenografts at 5 mg/kg intraperitoneally [^S63]; lung cancer IC50s were 10.1 to 31.3 µM with an oral 10 mg/kg mouse effect [^S80]; at 20 µM it kills cancer cells through lysosomal membrane permeabilisation within 30 min in serum-free medium [^S66] and extended median survival in a pancreatic neuroendocrine mouse model from 14 to 18 weeks [^S75]. A systematic review of in vivo secoiridoid work found five human trials, all measuring DNA damage rather than cancer, three positive [^S68]. The only human cancer study is a 20-to-22-patient leukaemia pilot at 40 mL/day of a 416 mg/kg oleocanthal oil reporting falls in white-cell counts and apoptosis-marker shifts [^S84].

### 3.9 The arithmetic

A US tablespoon is 14.8 mL, about 13.5 g of oil.

| Oil oleocanthal | mg per tablespoon | % of 200 mg ibuprofen (mass) | % of 400 mg (mass) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 mg/kg | 1.35 | 0.68 % | 0.34 % |
| 250 mg/kg | 3.38 | 1.69 % | 0.85 % |
| 355 mg/kg (survey maximum) | 4.80 | 2.40 % | 1.20 % |
| 500 mg/kg | 6.77 | 3.38 % | 1.69 % |

For 50 mL of oil to deliver an absorbed 20 mg (10 % of a 200 mg tablet) at 70 % absorption, the oil would need about 625 mg/kg of oleocanthal, above the 355 mg/kg maximum single-compound value in the 175-oil survey [^S20]. A 400 mg ibuprofen tablet gives a plasma Cmax of 30.87 to 36.62 µg/mL, about 150 to 178 µM [^S56]. Free hydroxytyrosol after 25 mL of EVOO peaks near 0.029 µM [^S30], about 400 times below its macrophage NO IC50 [^S58]. The trial doses translate as follows: Agrawal's lower-dose oil delivered about 6 mg of oleocanthal plus 11 mg of oleacein, roughly one tablespoon of a very high-phenolic oil; Katsa's 500 mg/kg arm delivered about 18 mg, 2.7 tablespoons. Body-surface scaling (human Km 37) converts an oral 10 mg/kg mouse dose to about 57 mg/day for a 70 kg adult, about 8 tablespoons of a 500 mg/kg oil [^S69].

### 3.10 Reading the oleocanthal evidence

The pharmacology splits cleanly into what is shown and what is assumed. Shown: oleocanthal inhibits COX enzymes in a purified assay [^S54] [^S64], activates pharyngeal TRPA1 [^S57], suppresses inflammatory gene expression in cells at 10 to 100 µM [^S76] [^S72], and, in two small human trials, dampens ex vivo platelet aggregation for a few hours after 40 mL of a rich oil [^S70] [^S87]. Assumed: that any of this happens in a person after a tablespoon. The pharmacokinetic record cuts against the assumption at its root. The parent compound has never been detected in human plasma, it vanishes in animal plasma, and its two aldehydes react with glycine and amines on contact [^S81] [^S50] [^S90]. The "10 % of an ibuprofen dose" estimate rests on a 70 % absorption assumption that predates those data [^S64].

Two observations keep the question open rather than closed. Oleocanthal is present in the stomach and intestine of rats after oral dosing [^S46], so local effects in the gut lumen remain plausible and untested in humans. And the antiplatelet signal is real enough to matter for people on antiplatelet drugs or anticoagulants, even though its own authors argued it does not run through COX [^S70]. The cancer and arthritis claims, by contrast, rest on cell IC50s of 10 to 58 µM and injected mouse doses [^S63] [^S75] [^S82], one to three orders of magnitude above any plausible exposure from food, and on a single 20-to-22-patient leukaemia pilot with surrogate outcomes [^S84]. The honest summary for a daily user is that oleocanthal is a well-characterised molecule whose systemic human pharmacology has not been established.

## 4. Cardiovascular Hard Outcomes, Mortality and the PREDIMED Controversy

### 4.1 PREDIMED: design, retraction and republication

PREDIMED randomised 7,447 Spaniards aged 55 to 80 at high cardiovascular risk to a Mediterranean diet with free EVOO, a Mediterranean diet with free mixed nuts, or advice to reduce dietary fat, with a primary composite of myocardial infarction, stroke and cardiovascular death. The 2013 NEJM report (PMID 23432189) is a retracted publication and is not cited as a source. Its retraction notice [^S117] and the republished trial [^S116] describe what went wrong: "enrollment of household members without randomization, assignment to a study group without randomization of some participants at 1 of 11 study sites, and apparent inconsistent use of randomization tables at another site." The revised analysis, adjusted for baseline characteristics and propensity scores, gave 96 events (3.8 %) in the EVOO arm, 83 (3.4 %) with nuts and 109 (4.4 %) in controls, HR 0.69 (0.53 to 0.91) for EVOO and 0.72 (0.54 to 0.95) for nuts, and "results were similar after the omission of 1588 participants whose study-group assignments were known or suspected to have departed from the protocol." The conclusion moved from causal wording to "the incidence ... was lower among those assigned to" the Mediterranean diets. The bodyweight reanalysis counts 866 participants (11.6 %) as affected and lists the food donors, including the olive-oil bodies Hojiblanca and Patrimonio Comunal Olivarero [^S121]; the PREDIMED design paper has no abstract [^S99].

The audit was triggered by a screen of 72,261 baseline means from 5,087 trials in which 15.2 % of non-retracted trials had p values within 0.05 of 0 or 1; that abstract does not name PREDIMED [^S110]. A BMJ analysis asked whether the republished trial is "still trusted" [^S120]. The investigators had already acknowledged "early termination and between-group differences in the intensity of the intervention" and stated that PREDIMED "cannot be used to draw firm conclusions on MedDiets and all-cause mortality" [^S112].

What the controversy changed: PREDIMED can no longer be read as a clean individually randomised trial, and the repair rests on propensity adjustment and exclusion. What it did not change: the point estimates (0.70 to 0.69 for EVOO; 0.72 for nuts). What PREDIMED never answered: whether the oil did the work, because the nut arm achieved nearly the same relative effect.

### 4.2 The control arm

Every abstract calls the control "advice to reduce dietary fat" or a "low-fat diet". The retrieved abstracts do not report achieved fat percentages, so the common critique that the control group never reached a low-fat diet could not be quantified here; the investigators' admission of unequal intervention intensity points the same way [^S112]. PREDIMED therefore shows that Mediterranean counselling plus free EVOO or nuts beat light-touch low-fat advice, not that it beats a well-executed low-fat diet.

### 4.3 Components: stroke carried the result

The Cochrane review of 30 trials, using the reanalysed PREDIMED with both Mediterranean arms combined, gives cardiovascular mortality HR 0.81 (0.50 to 1.32), total mortality HR 1.0 (0.81 to 1.24), and stroke HR 0.60 (0.45 to 0.80), "a decrease from 24/1000 to 14/1000" over 4.8 years [^S123]. It also describes Lyon's intervention as Mediterranean advice with "supplemental canola margarine". A four-trial pool including Lyon, the Indo-Mediterranean study, PREDIMED and CORDIOPREV gives MACE OR 0.52 with I² of 87 % and a non-significant all-cause OR of 0.77 [^S137]; it includes a trial that Cochrane excluded from its main analyses over data-reliability concerns, so it is read with caution.

### 4.4 PREDIMED secondary outcomes

| Outcome | n / cases | EVOO arm | Nut arm | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Atrial fibrillation (post hoc) [^S102] | 6,705; 72 / 82 / 92 | HR 0.62 (0.45 to 0.85) | HR 0.89 | Corrected [^S119] |

None of these was retracted. All are secondary or post hoc analyses at nominal p < 0.05 without multiplicity adjustment, in a trial stopped early with documented randomisation breaches. The diabetes paper notes that randomisation was not stratified by diabetes status and withdrawals were greater in controls; the breast-cancer result rests on 35 cases and the authors say it needs confirmation. The EVOO-only signals (AF, diabetes, breast cancer) are interesting because they are among the few outcomes that separate the two fats; they are hypotheses.

### 4.5 Dose within PREDIMED (observational)

Treating PREDIMED as a cohort, the top tertile of baseline EVOO intake carried HR 0.61 (0.44 to 0.85) for cardiovascular disease, and each 10 g/day of EVOO went with 10 % lower cardiovascular disease, without an all-cause association [^S100]. With annually updated intake, the top EVOO tertile (mean 49.2 g/day) carried HR 0.75 for a broad composite and the top decile (60.9 g/day) HR 0.52, while common olive oil was borderline (HR 0.93 per 10 g/day) [^S141]. MUFA's top quintile carried HR 0.50 against SFA HR 1.81 and trans fat HR 1.67 [^S108]. Total polyphenol intake tracked lower cardiovascular disease (HR 0.54), but the strongest subclasses were flavanols, lignans and hydroxybenzoic acids rather than olive tyrosols [^S106], and the all-cause trend was not significant (P = 0.12) [^S107]. Of the urinary olive-phenol biomarkers, only the methylated hydroxytyrosol metabolite homovanillyl alcohol tracked lower cardiovascular risk (HR 0.44), and it derives from both virgin olive oil and wine [^S111]; an eight-metabolite urinary phenolic signature (HR 0.80 per SD) draws on olive oil, wine, nuts, fruits and vegetables [^S139].

### 4.6 CORDIOPREV: secondary prevention against a real low-fat diet

CORDIOPREV randomised 1,002 coronary patients in Córdoba to a Mediterranean diet built on EVOO (design target 35 % fat, 22 % MUFA) or a low-fat diet (28 % fat, 12 % MUFA) for seven years [^S127]. Events were 87 against 111, 28.1 against 37.7 per 1,000 person-years, adjusted HRs 0.719 to 0.753 with an upper bound as close as 0.998; men benefited (HR 0.669) and women (n = 175) did not differ [^S128]. Two olive-oil-sector foundations co-funded it. Carotid intima-media thickness fell on the Mediterranean diet and not on the low-fat diet [^S127]. CORDIOPREV is the cleanest randomised comparison against a real low-fat diet, single-centre, unblinded for participants, and still a whole-pattern test.

### 4.7 Lyon Diet Heart is not an olive-oil trial

Lyon randomised 605 post-infarction patients to an "alpha-linolenic acid-rich" Mediterranean diet or a prudent diet; cardiac death plus non-fatal MI ran 16 + 17 against 3 + 5 (RR 0.27) [^S93], and 44 against 14 events at 46 months [^S94]. Its signature fat was an ALA-rich canola margarine [^S123], so it supports the pattern and alpha-linolenic acid, not olive oil.

### 4.8 Cohorts

- **US Nurses' Health Study and Health Professionals Follow-up Study**: more than half a tablespoon a day (>7 g) against non-use carried CVD HR 0.86 and CHD HR 0.82 over 24 years, with no association for stroke and no difference against other plant oils combined; replacing 5 g/day of margarine, butter, mayonnaise or dairy fat went with 5 to 7 % lower risk [^S125]. Over 28 years and 36,856 deaths, all-cause HR was 0.81, cardiovascular 0.81, cancer 0.83, neurodegenerative 0.71 and respiratory 0.82, again with no difference against other vegetable oils [^S130].
- **Spain**: EPIC-Spain's top quartile had 26 % lower all-cause and 44 % lower cardiovascular mortality, 13 % lower per 10 g/2,000 kcal [^S98]; across three cohorts, SUN gave CVD HR 0.57 at ≥30 g/day of virgin oil and EPIC-Spain's stroke association was U-shaped with the best band at 20 to 30 g/day, stronger for virgin than common oil [^S129]; in a national sample, virgin olive oil carried all-cause HR 0.66 and cardiovascular HR 0.43 while common olive oil was null (HR 0.96) [^S132].
- **Italy**: in 29,689 women, the top quartile carried CHD HR 0.56 on 144 events [^S96]; in Moli-sani, more than 3 against at most 1.5 tablespoons a day carried all-cause HR 0.80 and cardiovascular HR 0.75, with biomarkers explaining only 21 % of the association [^S136].
- **France**: intensive use went with 41 % lower stroke risk among 7,625 older adults, and top-tertile plasma oleic acid with 73 % lower risk on only 27 strokes [^S97].
- **Greece**: the MUFA:SFA ratio, the olive-oil proxy, explained 10.6 % of the Mediterranean diet's mortality association, behind moderate alcohol, low meat and vegetables [^S95].

### 4.9 Meta-analyses and dose-response

Per 25 g of olive oil, cohort stroke RR was 0.74 and CHD 0.96 (NS), with small-study effects [^S101]. Across 32 cohorts and 841,211 people, the inverse associations with mortality and stroke "could only be found between higher intakes of olive oil", while "the MUFA subgroup analyses did not reveal any significant risk reduction" [^S105]. Thirteen cohorts gave CVD RR 0.85 and all-cause 0.83, with "little additional or no risk reduction" beyond about 20 g/day and no modification by oil type [^S131]. Thirty articles and 2.7 million participants gave −7 % CVD and −8 % all-cause mortality per 10 g/day, with risk falling across 0 to 18 g/day for CVD and 0 to 22 g/day for mortality [^S135]. Umbrella reviews report benefits across outcomes [^S134] and an 18 % lower CVD risk with 77 % of the evidence at low or moderate GRADE [^S140]. Most cohorts did not separate oil grades, typical intakes ran 10 to 35 g/day, and capsule supplements cannot reach those doses [^S133].

### 4.10 Genetic evidence

No Mendelian randomisation study of olive-oil intake exists. Genetically higher serum oleic acid was not associated with CHD, MI or stroke [^S126]; inverse associations of plasma oleic, linoleic and α-linolenic acid with large-artery stroke and VTE were driven by SNPs near FADS1, a pleiotropic desaturase locus [^S124]. The genetic data do not show that oleic acid as such protects against heart attack.

### 4.11 Absolute numbers

| Source | Comparison | Absolute difference |
|---|---|---|
| CORDIOPREV [^S128] | Mediterranean + EVOO vs low-fat, coronary patients | 9.6 fewer events per 1,000 PY; 17.3 % vs 22.2 % over 7 years, NNT about 20 |
| PREDIMED [^S116] | Mediterranean + EVOO vs control | crude 3.8 % vs 4.4 % over 4.8 years (NNT about 150); HR-based about 1.4 points (NNT about 73) |
| PREDIMED stroke [^S123] | both Mediterranean arms vs control | 10 fewer strokes per 1,000 people over 4.8 years, about 2 per 1,000 PY |

Applying the 7 % per 10 g/day cohort slope to one 13.5 g tablespoon gives about 9 % lower relative cardiovascular risk against zero intake, an association with most of it reached in the first 10 to 20 g [^S135]. The randomised absolute effects apply to high-risk groups at 40 to 60 g/day inside a whole-diet change; a lower-risk adult would prevent far fewer events with the same relative effect.

### 4.12 Reading the outcome evidence

The randomised and observational records point the same way and measure different things. The trials test a dietary pattern that includes EVOO and compare it with weak low-fat advice (PREDIMED) or a real low-fat diet (CORDIOPREV); both found fewer events, PREDIMED mainly through stroke [^S116] [^S123] [^S128]. The cohorts test olive oil as a food inside free-living diets and find 15 to 20 % lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality across Spain, Italy, France and the US [^S98] [^S136] [^S130], with dose-response curves that flatten by about 20 g/day [^S131] [^S135]. Neither design isolates the phenolic fraction from the fat, and neither isolates the fat from what it replaces.

Three facts narrow what can be attributed to EVOO itself. In PREDIMED the nut arm did as well as the EVOO arm on the primary endpoint [^S116]. In the US cohorts the advantage disappears when olive oil is compared with other vegetable oils combined [^S125] [^S130], and the meta-analysis of 841,211 people found no signal for MUFA as a nutrient class [^S105], which fits a displacement effect (less butter, margarine and mayonnaise) plus something specific to olive oil rather than to oleic acid. And the only data that separate grades on hard outcomes are observational Spanish analyses in which virgin oil tracks lower mortality and common oil does not [^S132] [^S129] [^S141]. That pattern is consistent with the phenolic hypothesis and is exactly what residual confounding by diet quality and social class would also produce. A daily tablespoon sits on the steep part of the cohort curve, which is the most favourable observational reading; the absolute benefit for a healthy middle-aged adult is a few events per 1,000 person-years at most, and only if the association is causal.

## 5. Endothelial Function, Blood Pressure and Hemodynamics

Two anchors set the scale. Each 1 percentage-point increase in brachial flow-mediated dilation (FMD) is associated with a relative risk of 0.87 for future cardiovascular events in observational cohorts [^S156]. In drug trials, a 5 mmHg systolic reduction gives HR 0.91 for major events without prior CVD [^S182]. Both are extrapolations when applied to diet.

### 5.1 Acute, single-meal studies

- A 50 g-fat olive-oil meal cut FMD by 31 % (14.3 to 9.9 %) in ten adults, while canola, salmon and olive oil with vitamins or with salad and vinegar did not; the oil grade is not stated [^S144].
- Olive oil added to a high-fat meal worsened FMD relative to walnuts [^S148]; palmolein and olive oil in high-protein meals did not differ [^S169]; EVOO raised FMD more than butter in type 1 diabetes [^S183]; and 10 g of EVOO added to a Mediterranean lunch prevented the postprandial rise in platelet ROS, NOX2 and soluble adhesion molecules seen without it or with corn oil [^S162].
- Holding the oil constant and varying phenolics: a 400 against 80 ppm breakfast improved laser-Doppler ischaemic reactive hyperaemia with more NOx and less lipoperoxide [^S147]; high-phenol oil attenuated the lower NOx and reactivity of eNOS Glu298Asp TT carriers [^S157]; a 961 against 289 mg/kg oil raised reactive hyperaemia and lowered oxidised LDL in 13 pre- and stage-1 hypertensives [^S170]; and 50 mL of high-polyphenol EVOO against refined oil gave FMD +1.2 against −3.6 % at 2 h, a 4.8-point gap, without a blood-pressure change [^S184].
- Green olive oil improved FMD only with red wine [^S152], and all four oil-and-wine combinations lowered augmentation index regardless of oil grade [^S153].

Several acute trials measured skin microcirculation by laser-Doppler, not brachial FMD; the two are different tests.

### 5.2 Chronic endothelial trials and pools

- A four-week MUFA diet with virgin olive oil raised postprandial endothelium-dependent vasodilation compared with SFA or low-fat diets [^S151]; a Mediterranean MUFA diet lowered endothelial microparticles and raised progenitor cells in 20 elderly people [^S158].
- Thirty mL/day of olive oil, with or without EGCG, raised EndoPAT RHI from 1.59 to 1.75 in patients with early atherosclerosis, with no non-oil control and an author on the device maker's advisory board [^S161].
- Hydroxytyrosol capsules (10 mg/day in 1.65 g of oil) improved FMD from 3.7 to 6.5 % against baseline in coronary patients, with no blood-pressure effect [^S185].
- The VOHF enriched oils listed improved endothelial function without numbers [^S175].
- Pooled, olive-oil interventions raised FMD by 0.76 points over 8 trials [^S67]; Mediterranean diets raise it by 1.66 points [^S180] and by 2.32 points across 133 trials, in which tea (+3.22), cocoa (+1.68), soy (+1.58) and nuts (+1.39) show comparable effects [^S191]; Mediterranean diets with olive oil also improved inflammatory and adhesion markers against low-fat diets [^S190].
- In CORDIOPREV, FMD was 3.83 against 1.16 % after one year, with more progenitor cells and fewer microparticles [^S181]; in MedLey, a Mediterranean diet with abundant EVOO raised FMD 1.3 points at six months [^S171].

### 5.3 Blood pressure: oil against oil

- EVOO against sunflower oil for six months in 23 hypertensive patients cut the daily antihypertensive dose by 48 % against 4 % [^S143].
- Replacing usual oils with EVOO lowered systolic pressure in 41 older adults [^S168]; 40 g/day of EVOO against sunflower oil did not change blood pressure in young women and raised LDL by 5.1 mg/dL [^S192].
- **Holding fat constant**: virgin oil at 161 against refined at 14.7 mg/kg lowered systolic pressure in hypertensive coronary patients [^S146]; 366 against 2.7 mg/kg lowered systolic pressure and ACE gene expression [^S173]; OLIVAUS (60 mL/day, 360 against 86 mg/kg, 50 Australians) found no between-oil difference, a 2.5 mmHg within-arm systolic fall and no change in arterial stiffness, with producer co-funding [^S179]; polyphenol-rich oil gave −7.91/−6.65 mmHg against baseline, not against the polyphenol-free oil, in 24 young women [^S159].
- Pools: high against low phenolic gave systolic SMD −0.52 on 69 people [^S166]; another pool reported blood-pressure improvements only in subgroups [^S176]; the network meta-analysis of 13 trials found both high- and low-phenolic (E)VOO lowered systolic pressure by 2.87 to 2.99 mmHg against refined oil, with a phenolic dose-response and no diastolic effect, at low to moderate certainty [^S178].

### 5.4 Blood pressure: broad pools and diets

- Broad EVOO pools are null: 33 trials with no significant blood-pressure effect [^S89], systolic −0.48 mmHg across 17 olive and flaxseed trials [^S187], only triglycerides moving in Mediterranean diets enriched with olive oil [^S186], and systolic pressure slightly higher with olive oil than nuts inside Mediterranean diets [^S189].
- Isolated olive phenolic supplements moved lipids and insulin, not blood pressure [^S188]; a 10 mg/day oleocanthal-oleacein extract lowered systolic pressure by 7.66 mmHg against placebo in 102 adults with metabolic syndrome, a single unreplicated extract trial [^S193].
- In PREDIMED, three months of the EVOO diet lowered systolic pressure by 5.9 mmHg against low-fat advice (nuts 7.1) [^S149]; over four years only diastolic pressure differed (−1.53 mmHg) [^S160]; ambulatory systolic pressure fell 2.3 mmHg with EVOO, 2.6 with nuts and rose 1.7 in controls [^S164]; falls tracked urinary polyphenols and plasma NO [^S167], and in hypertensive women NO metabolites correlated with pressure changes [^S174].
- OmniHeart's unsaturated-fat diet lowered systolic pressure by 1.3 mmHg against a carbohydrate diet [^S145].
- A four-week uncontrolled virgin-oil diet lowered systolic pressure in elderly diabetic and healthy people [^S155].

### 5.5 Oleic acid or phenolics

MUFA-for-carbohydrate pools range from 2.6/1.8 mmHg (1.3/0.9 and non-significant in crossovers only) [^S150] to no effect at all (−0.08 mmHg across 14 trials) [^S177]. The rat case for oleic acid as the hypotensive agent [^S154] and oleate's suppression of endothelial adhesion molecules at 10 to 100 µmol/L [^S142] do not explain why human trials holding oleic acid constant still show a systolic difference between oil grades [^S178]. The two are not mutually exclusive.

### 5.6 Nitric oxide and nitro-fatty acids

NOx rose with phenol-rich oil in several small trials, but NOx is an indirect and diet-sensitive marker. Nitro-oleic acid adducts were detected in EVOO and olives and form during simulated gastric nitration [^S165]; in mice, injected 10-nitro-oleic acid lowered angiotensin-II hypertension through soluble epoxide hydrolase, with a dietary arm that used linoleic acid plus nitrite rather than olive oil [^S163]; and in the one human study of fat plus nitrite, adding conjugated linoleic acid blunted nitrite's vasodilatory action [^S172]. Key authors declared a financial interest in a nitro-fatty-acid company. There is no human evidence that EVOO lowers blood pressure through nitro-fatty acids.

### 5.7 Scale

The network meta-analysis's −3 mmHg would correspond to about 5 to 6 % lower relative cardiovascular risk if it persisted and behaved like drug-induced lowering [^S182]; the 0.76-point FMD gain maps to about 10 % lower risk through an observational association [^S156]. The positive phenolic blood-pressure trials delivered about 8 to 30 mg/day of phenolics from 25 to 60 mL of oil. One tablespoon of a 360 mg/kg EVOO gives about 5 mg.

### 5.8 Reading the vascular evidence

The vascular findings are internally consistent once the comparator is fixed. Against refined olive oil, phenolic-rich oil gives a small, reproducible systolic fall of about 3 mmHg over weeks [^S178] and better microvascular reactivity after a meal [^S147] [^S170] [^S184]. Against nothing, or against a whole diet, the effect on blood pressure disappears in the broad pools [^S89] [^S187] [^S186]. The famous large effects come from trials of 23 and 24 people [^S143] [^S159] and an unreplicated extract trial [^S193]. The acute data contain a warning that cuts both ways: a large olive-oil fat load on its own can blunt FMD for a few hours [^S144], while the same load eaten with vegetables and vinegar, or a high-phenolic oil, does not [^S184]. That makes a raw tablespoon eaten with food the setting most consistent with the positive acute data, though no trial tested a tablespoon alone.

The mechanistic stories deserve proportion. Nitric-oxide markers rise in several small trials but are diet-sensitive [^S167]; the nitro-fatty-acid pathway is a mouse and test-tube finding with a commercial interest attached [^S165] [^S163]; and the oleic-acid-only explanation rests on rats [^S154]. Seen against other foods, the FMD effect of olive oil is ordinary: tea, cocoa, soy, nuts, fruit and vegetables show effects of similar or larger size [^S191].

## 6. Lipids, LDL Oxidation, HDL Function, Glycemia and Inflammatory Markers

The comparator decides almost every answer here: high- against low-phenolic oil isolates the phenolics, olive oil against butter or seed oils isolates the fat, and Mediterranean diet against low-fat advice tests a whole package.

### 6.1 The EUROLIVE network

In EUROLIVE, the low-phenolic oil base produced most of the triglyceride effect and over half of the HDL rise; the phenolic increment from low to high was about 0.020 mmol/L of HDL cholesterol (0.8 mg/dL) and about 4.4 U/L of oxidised LDL [^S13]. The EUROLIVE pilot found the same direction for oxidised LDL and a rise in HDL after the virgin oil [^S11]. In 40 coronary patients, 50 mL/day of virgin against refined oil lowered oxidised LDL and lipid peroxides without changing lipids [^S146]. Phenol metabolites, mainly sulfated hydroxytyrosol, travel on the LDL particle, and plasma oxidised LDL correlated inversely with LDL phenol content (r = −0.296) [^S200]. Part of the antioxidant effect is the fatty acid itself: LDL oleic acid rose 1.9 % and each unit rise in the LDL oleic/linoleic ratio went with 4.2 µg/L lower isoprostanes [^S198]. Anti-oxLDL autoantibodies rose with phenolic content [^S17]; MCP-1 and the LOX-1 receptor's expression fell [^S19]. HDL cholesterol efflux capacity rose 3.05 % after the rich oil against −2.34 % after the poor oil in 47 men (P = 0.042) [^S205]. In a parallel three-month trial, a traditional Mediterranean diet with 328 mg/kg virgin oil reduced inflammation- and oxidation-related gene expression more than the same diet with washed 55 mg/kg oil [^S199], and a 961 mg/kg oil raised postprandial expression of cholesterol-efflux genes [^S204].

### 6.2 VOHF and OLIVAUS

VOHF tested enriched oils (500 ppm, one half thyme phenolics) against an 80 ppm virgin oil in 33 hypercholesterolaemic adults: most HDL gains came from the thyme-blended oil, HDL cholesterol rising 5.74 % against baseline in people not on lipid-lowering drugs [^S207], with improved NMR lipoprotein ratios [^S29]. OLIVAUS, at 60 mL/day of 320 against 86 mg/kg oil in 50 Australians, found no significant between-treatment differences: a within-arm oxidised LDL fall of 6.5 mU/mL and an hs-CRP fall of 1.9 mg/L in a subgroup with baseline CRP above 1 mg/L [^S224]; HDL efflux unchanged in both arms and LDL up 0.14 mmol/L after the high-polyphenol oil [^S226]. The oil producer Cobram Estate part-funded the trial and supplied the oil. The Spanish efflux result therefore did not replicate at more than twice the dose.

### 6.3 PREDIMED biomarkers

Three months of the EVOO diet against low-fat advice lowered glucose by 0.39 mmol/L, the cholesterol/HDL ratio by 0.38 and CRP by 0.54 mg/L, with less intense education in the low-fat arm [^S149]. Oxidised LDL fell 10.6 U/L with EVOO, 7.3 with nuts and 2.9 in controls; only the EVOO arm differed significantly from control [^S197]. At one year, LDL resistance to oxidation rose 6.46 % and LDL oxidative modifications fell 36.3 % against control, while the nut arm showed no significant LDL changes [^S214]. HDL efflux rose against baseline in both Mediterranean arms but not significantly against control; the EVOO arm improved HDL esterification, paraoxonase-1 and HDL vasodilatory capacity against control [^S213]. Each 10 g/day of virgin olive oil went with 0.7 % more efflux capacity, a within-trial association [^S219], and in a nested case-control study each SD of efflux capacity went with OR 0.58 for acute coronary syndrome independent of HDL cholesterol [^S221], which supports efflux as a biomarker without showing that olive oil's effect on it prevents events. Both Mediterranean arms cut hs-CRP, IL-6, TNF-α and MCP-1 by at least 16 % against baseline over 3 to 5 years [^S211].

### 6.4 Olive oil against other fats

Per 1 % of energy replacing carbohydrate, LDL cholesterol changes by +0.033 mmol/L for SFA, −0.006 for cis-MUFA (not different from zero) and −0.014 for PUFA [^S194]; replacing SFA with cis-unsaturated fat lowers the total/HDL ratio [^S195]. Oleic acid's LDL benefit is what it displaces.

- Against SFA: MUFA replacing 9.5 % of energy from SFA lowered LDL by 11.3 % (n-6 PUFA 13.6 %) without changing FMD, the primary outcome [^S209]; butter raised total and LDL cholesterol more than refined olive oil [^S206]; ghee raised apoB by 0.09 g/L and non-HDL by 0.53 mmol/L against olive oil [^S222]; and olive oil replacing lard lowered LDL by 7.0 % and the VLDL apoB pool by 22.5 % through faster LDL clearance [^S228].
- Against PUFA-rich oils: corn oil lowered non-HDL cholesterol by 0.39 mmol/L against 0.04 for EVOO, with more apoB and LDL-particle reduction, while apoA1 rose more on EVOO [^S215]; olive oil raised HDL by 1.37 mg/dL more than other plant oils but lowered LDL by 4.2 mg/dL less [^S218]; and in a 54-trial network every common oil lowered LDL against butter, with safflower and rapeseed ranked above olive oil [^S217].
- Added on top: per 10 g/day of added olive oil, LDL moved +0.04 mg/dL across 34 trials, which the authors call trivial [^S225]; on a whole-food vegan diet, four tablespoons of EVOO a day blunted the LDL fall seen with less than a teaspoon [^S229]; and an EVOO-only pool found no significant effect on any lipid fraction [^S89].

### 6.5 High- against low-phenolic pools

The network meta-analysis found no difference between oil types for total cholesterol, HDL or triglycerides, LDL −0.14 mmol/L (about 5.4 mg/dL) for high against low phenolic, and oxidised LDL SMD −0.68 for high-phenolic against refined [^S178]. Another pool gave oxidised LDL SMD −0.44, HDL +2.37 mg/dL and a total-cholesterol estimate printed as +4.5 mg/dL with an all-negative interval, almost certainly −4.5 [^S176]. An eight-trial pool gave oxidised LDL SMD −0.25 and no lipid effect [^S166]. A ten-trial pool funded by an olive-sector Horizon 2020 project found HDL +1.13 mg/dL and LDL −4.28 mg/dL only in the top phenolic tertile (320 to 600 mg/kg), and argued for polyphenol labelling [^S227]. A recent within-trial comparison delivered about 8.3 mg/day of phenolics either as 20 g of a 414 mg/kg oil or 8 g of a 1,021 mg/kg oil and found the smaller dose of richer oil better for total cholesterol [^S298].

### 6.6 Glycemia and type 2 diabetes

Four cohorts gave RR 0.84 for type 2 diabetes in the highest against lowest olive-oil intake, and 29 trials gave HbA1c −0.27 % and fasting glucose −0.44 mmol/L in people with diabetes, with the authors stressing that the result "regards OO as food" [^S216]. High-MUFA diets lowered HbA1c by 0.21 % in abnormal glucose metabolism without moving fasting glucose or insulin [^S202]. In the Nurses' cohorts, more than a tablespoon a day carried HR 0.90 and substitution for mayonnaise 15 % lower risk [^S208]. In PREDIMED-Reus, incidence was 10.1 % with EVOO, 11.0 % with nuts and 17.9 % in controls (HR 0.49) [^S201], and in the trial-wide subgroup HR 0.60 for EVOO against 0.82 for nuts [^S104]. Postprandially, 10 g of EVOO added to a meal lowered two-hour glucose and DPP-4 and raised insulin, GLP-1 and GIP [^S210], and in impaired fasting glucose lowered glucose, triglycerides and apoB-48 [^S212]; the comparison meals had less fat and energy, so these are not isocaloric tests.

### 6.7 Inflammatory markers and postprandial lipemia

A meta-analysis on CRP, IL-6 and TNF-α reports a beneficial direction without pooled numbers [^S220]; Mediterranean diets with olive oil lowered IL-6 (SMD −1.85) and CRP (SMD −0.96) at I² of 92 to 99 %, close to uninterpretable as a single effect [^S230]; and the EVOO-only pool is null for CRP, IL-6 and TNF-α [^S89]. An olive-oil fat load did not activate monocyte NF-κB where butter and walnut loads did [^S196]; refined olive oil and cocoa butter loads did not differ on triglycerides, hsCRP or IL-6 [^S203]; and across 23 crossover studies meal fatty-acid type "is not a relevant determinant of postprandial lipemia" in healthy adults, with SFA loads above 32 g mattering in people with risk factors [^S223]. A morning tablespoon is a 13.5 g fat load.

### 6.8 Reading the biomarker evidence

The biomarker picture rewards precision about what is being swapped. If a tablespoon of EVOO replaces a tablespoon of butter, the Mensink coefficients predict an LDL fall of roughly 0.12 mmol/L (about 4.5 mg/dL) from the fat swap alone [^S194], which is about the same size as the best phenolic-specific LDL effect at very high phenolic doses [^S178] [^S227]. If the tablespoon is simply added, the dose-response pool says LDL barely moves [^S225], and on an already low-SFA diet it may blunt an LDL fall [^S229]. Against corn, safflower or rapeseed oil, olive oil lowers LDL and apoB less and raises HDL and apoA1 more [^S215] [^S218] [^S217]. Whether that trade is a gain depends on which marker one trusts more; LDL and apoB are the causal markers.

The phenolic-specific signal is strongest for oxidised LDL, which is the endpoint of the EU claim [^S166] [^S176] [^S178]. HDL function is unsettled: one positive EUROLIVE sub-study in men [^S205], one null Australian replication at a higher dose [^S226], and a PREDIMED result that was positive against baseline but not against control [^S213]. The glycemic evidence is real in people with type 2 diabetes (HbA1c −0.27 %) [^S216] and weaker elsewhere: the EVOO-only pool lowers insulin and HOMA-IR without moving fasting glucose [^S89]. The inflammatory evidence is the clearest case of a diet-pattern effect being credited to the oil: positive inside Mediterranean diets [^S211] [^S230] and null for EVOO alone [^S89].

## 7. Neuroprotection, Cognition and Dementia

### 7.1 Cells and test tubes

Oleocanthal blocked tau fibril assembly (about 60 % at 1 µM for the K18 fragment, complete at 100 µM) by forming Schiff-base adducts with lysine; ibuprofen and other NSAIDs did not, so the effect is aldehyde chemistry, not COX inhibition [^S233]. It altered soluble Aβ oligomers at doses as low as 10 nM and protected cultured neurons at 10 µM [^S234]. At 25 µM it raised P-glycoprotein expression 2.3 to 3.0-fold in intestinal cells, which the authors flagged as a possible drug-interaction route [^S235]. Purified secoiridoids raised ATP in neuronal cells, and in aged mice ligstroside, not oleocanthal, gave the memory and lifespan findings [^S251].

### 7.2 Mice

- The clearance mechanism rests on a small set of papers. In wild-type mice, oleocanthal raised the brain efflux index of injected Aβ40 from 62.0 to 79.9 % via up-regulated P-gp and LRP1; the dose and route are not in the abstract [^S238]. In TgSwDI mice, 5 mg/kg/day intraperitoneally for four weeks, six per group, lowered hippocampal amyloid and raised P-gp and LRP1, and a human-cell barrier model showed up to 50 % more Aβ transport at 1 to 10 µM [^S242].
- The mechanism is coherent with knockout data, in which P-gp-null mice clear Aβ at half the rate [^S231], and with PET evidence of reduced barrier P-gp function in Alzheimer's patients [^S237]. No human study has measured barrier P-gp after EVOO.
- Dietary EVOO in mice:
  - A supermarket EVOO with 71 mg/kg oleocanthal lowered Aβ and tau when started before pathology but did not rescue cognition when started late [^S243].
  - A 680 mg/kg producer-donated oil added to donepezil reduced Aβ load [^S246] and in advanced-stage mice reduced NLRP3 activation and IL-1β [^S250].
  - A producer-funded study found oleocanthal-low EVOO and spiked oleocanthal both reduced Aβ [^S258].
  - A dose-ranging study found hepatic toxicity at 20 mg/kg, four times the effective 5 mg/kg [^S263].
  - An oral oleocanthal-erythritol formulation at 10 mg/kg improved memory and plaques and was developed because of the compound's "pungent, astringent, and irritant taste" [^S255].
- Funding and replication: the investigators of this network declare roles in Oleolive LLC, and a systematic review found the seven preclinical oleocanthal-amyloid reports "uniformly American" at moderate risk of bias [^S264].
- Independent laboratories also report positive results:
  - EVOO-enriched chow in 3xTg mice reduced insoluble Aβ and phospho-tau through autophagy [^S245].
  - An ordinary 253 mg/kg EVOO improved memory and reduced tau oligomers in hTau mice, with a dose stated as "50 mg/Kg diet" that reads like a unit error [^S252].
  - Oleuropein aglycone [^S239] and hydroxytyrosol [^S248] at 50 mg/kg of diet improved cognition and plaque burden in TgCRND8 mice.
  - A 25-study review found oleocanthal the most consistent anti-amyloid and anti-tau phenolic, limited by pharmacokinetics and the lack of human evidence [^S266].

### 7.3 Human trials

- **PREDIMED-Navarra**: after 6.5 years, MMSE was 0.62 points and clock drawing 0.51 higher in the EVOO arm than controls, with no baseline cognitive test, so this is a post-trial comparison [^S240]; a fuller battery in 285 participants found less MCI (OR 0.34) with mostly non-significant domain differences [^S241].
- **PREDIMED-Barcelona**: frontal and global composites favoured EVOO mainly because all control composites declined (−0.33 and −0.38), with follow-up testing in 334 of 447 [^S244]; it was corrected in 2018 [^S115]. At baseline, virgin olive oil intake was associated with delayed verbal memory [^S236].
- A review of five Mediterranean-diet RCTs found 8 of 66 cognitive outcomes favouring the diet and no benefit for incident impairment [^S249].
- **MIND trial**: 604 cognitively unimpaired adults; the MIND arm received about 14 tablespoons of EVOO a week plus berries and nuts for three years; global cognition improved +0.205 against +0.170 in the weight-loss control (P = 0.23), with similar MRI measures [^S259].
- **MCI pilots**:
  - MICOIL compared 50 mL/day of high- or moderate-phenolic EVOO with diet advice and reported the high-phenolic arm better on almost all domains; the abstract gives no n, effect sizes, phenolic content or funding, and the registry lists participant-only masking and retrospective registration [^S253]. Companion EEG [^S254] and biochemistry [^S256] papers analysed 43 and 84 participants.
  - In a 26-person US trial, 30 mL/day of a 1,200 mg/kg producer-donated oil against refined oil for six months improved the clinical dementia rating in both arms, more with refined oil, and the overall barrier-permeability change was not significant; the principal investigator holds equity in Oleolive LLC [^S257].
- **Extracts, not oil**: an olive-leaf beverage favoured MMSE in mild Alzheimer's in an open-label design [^S262], and a concentrated hydroxytyrosol product showed a borderline attention interaction [^S261].
- **Pools**: five EVOO RCTs give MMSE +0.42 and SMD 0.29 at low GRADE certainty [^S267], and an 11-study review is PREDIMED-dominated [^S260].

### 7.4 Cohorts

In the Three-City Study, intensive olive-oil use carried OR 0.83 for four-year visual-memory decline [^S232] and 41 % lower stroke risk [^S97]. In the US cohorts, ≥7 g/day carried HR 0.72 for dementia-related death, not dementia incidence, with no benefit when olive oil replaced other vegetable oils or butter and no ability to separate oil grades [^S138]; neurodegenerative mortality HR was 0.71 [^S130]. A German cohort aged 75+ found no protective single food including olive oil [^S247]. In a PREDIMED-Plus analysis, virgin olive oil tracked better two-year cognitive change and gut-microbial diversity while common olive oil tracked worse [^S265].

### 7.5 Dose translation

One tablespoon carries 0 to 4.8 mg of oleocanthal for the surveyed oils [^S20] and about 8.4 to 9.2 mg for the brand in the US trials. Body-surface scaling turns the injected 5 mg/kg mouse dose into about 28 mg/day for a 70 kg adult and the hepatotoxic 20 mg/kg into about 114 mg/day; the EVOO-chow arms equal about 4 g of oil a day by body-surface scaling, or 49 g/day by simple per-kilogram scaling, which is the scaling the authors used to call it "Greek-diet intake". Free hydroxytyrosol after 25 mL of EVOO peaks about 35 to 3,500 times below the concentrations used in the cell work [^S30].

### 7.6 Reading the neuro evidence

The neuro evidence has the widest gap between layers. In cells and mice, oleocanthal, oleuropein aglycone, hydroxytyrosol and whole EVOO reduce amyloid and tau pathology in at least three independent laboratories [^S245] [^S239] [^S243], and the barrier-clearance mechanism is biologically coherent [^S231] [^S237]. In humans, the parent molecule is not measurable in plasma [^S81], the best randomised data show small differences that come mainly from control-arm decline [^S244] [^S240], the largest trial that supplied daily EVOO was null [^S259], and the MCI trials are pilots with design and funding problems [^S253] [^S257]. The cohort association with dementia-related death is consistent but cannot separate oil grades and disappears against other vegetable oils [^S138].

The most plausible human route from olive oil to brain protection is vascular: lower stroke risk [^S97] [^S101] [^S123]. Pure oleocanthal supplements are a different proposition from food, with a narrow mouse margin between effective and hepatotoxic doses [^S263] and a P-glycoprotein induction mechanism that could alter levels of transporter-substrate drugs [^S235].

## 8. Sourcing, Adulteration, Cooking, Storage, Dose and Safety

### 8.1 Adulteration and grade failure

An FDA FT-NIR screen of 88 US retail "EVOO" products found 62.5 % failed at least one criterion, predicting refined-olive-oil or seed-oil admixture, while IOC fatty-acid ranges alone would have flagged fewer than 10 % [^S281]. Three IOC-recognised panels judged most of six commercial "extra virgin" oils not extra virgin and disagreed with each other [^S289]. Many medium- and low-priced Italian retail samples had prohibited sensory defects, and labels giving harvest year, cultivar and extraction method scored better regardless of price [^S295]. Californian oils differed from some imports mainly by absence of defects [^S277]. During a Taiwanese fraud investigation, 39 % of EVOO samples carried a copper-chlorophyll colourant [^S279]. Reviews list dilution with seed oil and soft-deodorised lower grades as the main frauds, with stigmastadienes and sterols as markers [^S290]. The 1981 toxic oil syndrome, 356 deaths from contaminated rapeseed oil, is the extreme case of oil fraud [^S272]; modern EVOO fraud is mainly economic and costs the phenolic dose rather than safety.

### 8.2 Cooking and the smoke point

Smoke point mainly tracks free acidity and relies on a subjective visual test [^S296]. Olive oils lasted 24 to 27 h of domestic deep frying before reaching the 25 % polar-compound discard limit, against 15 h for a seed-oil blend [^S276]; EVOO lasted more than 28 h against 18 to 20 h for peanut and canola, with lower anisidine values, while losing its bioactive pool in the first hours [^S284]; potatoes fried in canola carried more PUFA-derived aldehydes, EVOO-fried potatoes more phenolics, and acrylamide did not depend on oil type [^S282]. EVOO extended frying time from 21.5 to 25.25 h over a refined blend [^S291]; frying fumes from EVOO and refined olive oil carried fewer aldehydes than canola [^S269]; and MUFA-rich oils formed far fewer aldehydes than soybean or corn oil in shallow frying [^S288].

Heat costs the phenolics:

- A domestic sauté cut polyphenols by 40 % at 120 °C and 75 % at 170 °C [^S41].
- Hydroxytyrosol derivatives fell to 50 to 60 % after one 10-minute fry at 180 °C and about 10 % after six, with tyrosol derivatives more stable [^S8].
- Microwave heating cost little, and boiling moved phenols into the water [^S5].
- Retention fell from 70 to 80 % after the first household fry to 20 to 30 % after the eighth [^S4].
- Ten deep-frying cycles removed 70 to 93 % [^S52].
- Tocopherols and polyphenols degraded fastest at 180 °C while oleic acid and sterols stayed constant [^S274].
- Oleocanthal lost at most 16 % by HPLC under single heating but up to 31 % of its throat-sting activity [^S15], and it fell with water-oil boiling while appearing to rise after sauté in one study [^S48].
- Part of the loss migrates into the food [^S28].

### 8.3 Storage and packaging

- Losses:
  - 46 % over 12 months at room temperature, 92 % retained at 4 °C after six months [^S45].
  - 42.0 ± 24.3 % in 160 oils, greater in oleacein- and oleocanthal-rich oils [^S44].
  - Pseudo-first-order kinetics, similar at 5 and 15 °C and faster at 25 °C [^S27].
  - 98 % loss of oleuropein derivatives over 22 months in low-phenolic oils [^S40].
  - 15 and 32 % in a year for very high-phenolic Calabrian oils [^S49].
  - Oleocanthal oxidises to oleocanthalic acid [^S33].
  - About 75 % (oleacein) and 60 % (oleocanthal) over 24 months, in light and dark alike [^S294].
- Cold storage and argon kept oils within limits for up to 36 months [^S287].
- Packaging: under light, a multilayer pack outperformed green and UV-absorbing glass over ten months [^S286], and in simulated household use oil in green PET exceeded the legal peroxide limit within 30 days while bag-in-box and tin held up [^S303].

### 8.4 Dose, calories and weight

- PREDIMED allocated 1 L of virgin olive oil a week per household [^S149]; a measured individual intake of about 50 g/day was not found in any retrieved abstract.
- Recent raw-oil trials used 8 to 20 g/day [^S298]; two tablespoons a day of EVOO against refined oil for four weeks lowered Rome III constipation scores from 11.27 to 3.47 against 11.37 to 7.74 [^S297].
- Weight evidence:
  - PREDIMED's reanalysed weight change was −0.41 kg (p = 0.056) and waist −0.47 cm (p = 0.154) for EVOO, so "no weight gain" is supported and "weight loss" is not [^S121].
  - Olive-oil-enriched diets lowered weight by 0.92 kg across 11 RCTs [^S283].
  - Olive oil did not alter fat distribution across 52 RCTs [^S292].
  - The EVOO-only pool found no BMI or waist effect [^S89].
- A tablespoon is about 120 kcal; added without compensation, that is about 44,000 kcal a year.

### 8.5 Timing, gallbladder, allergy, drugs

No trial compared a fasted morning shot with the same oil taken with food. A 50 mL EVOO bolus is used as a test meal because it reliably contracts the gallbladder, peaking at 50 to 70 min [^S300]; high MUFA intake carried RR 0.83 for symptomatic gallstones in US men [^S271]; and the "gallbladder flush" rests on one uncontrolled case report in a patient also taking ursodeoxycholic acid [^S304]. Two olive-fruit-allergic patients tolerated olive oil on oral challenge [^S275] [^S280], and the raw-olive allergen Ole e 13 is degraded by processing [^S278]. No warfarin or INR interaction study exists; the antiplatelet data are acute 40 mL doses [^S70] [^S87], and in PREDIMED the EVOO arm was less likely to start vitamin K antagonists (HR 0.68) [^S285].

### 8.6 Contaminants

Acrylamide is a table-olive issue driven largely by lipid-oxidation products [^S301], and oil type did not change acrylamide in fried potatoes [^S282]. Olive pomace oils produced before mid-2001 were highly PAH-contaminated [^S268] [^S270]. Italian market olive oils averaged 33.1 mg/kg MOSH and 3.3 mg/kg MOAH against 160.7 and 36.1 mg/kg in pomace oils [^S299], and virgin oils can exceed the 2.0 mg/kg MOAH limit through harvesting-machinery lubricants [^S302]. Bound 3-MCPD was below 300 µg/kg in virgin oils and up to 2,462 µg/kg in refined oils [^S273]. Pesticide residues appeared in 34 % of 35 Italian EVOOs at levels judged no consumer risk [^S293].

### 8.7 Reading the practical evidence

The practical record is chemistry, not clinical outcomes, and it is reassuring on safety and sobering on dose. EVOO is a sound cooking fat by every oxidation measure tested [^S276] [^S284] [^S269], but heat removes most of the phenolics that justify paying for it [^S41] [^S52]. Storage removes much of the rest unless the oil is fresh, cool, protected from light and finished soon after opening [^S45] [^S303]. Fraud is common enough that the grade on the label is not a guarantee [^S281] [^S289], and its main cost is a lower phenolic dose. The safety signals are minor: a gallbladder-contracting bolus [^S300], a measurable but unquantified antiplatelet effect at large doses [^S87], no documented allergy to the oil itself [^S280], and contaminant burdens concentrated in pomace oil rather than EVOO [^S299] [^S268].

## 10. The Daily Tablespoon, Worked Through

This section collects the arithmetic for one 13.5 g tablespoon a day, the dose that motivated the review.

### 10.1 What the spoon contains

About 120 kcal, roughly 10 g of oleic acid, and a phenolic payload that depends on the bottle. At the EU claim threshold of 250 mg/kg the spoon carries about 3.4 mg of hydroxytyrosol-family and tyrosol-family phenolics [^S36]; at the Greek qNMR mean of 483 mg/kg, about 6.5 mg [^S45]; at the premium-competition means of 343 to 421 mg/kg, about 4.6 to 5.7 mg [^S51]. The oleocanthal share is smaller still: 0 to 4.8 mg across 175 monovarietal oils [^S20], about 1 mg in the supermarket EVOO used in one mouse study [^S243], and 8 to 9 mg in the very high-phenolic brand used in the US clinical and mouse work [^S257]. A refined or "light" olive oil carries essentially none [^S42].

### 10.2 What happens to it before the spoon

Phenolics fall continuously from bottling. A 500 mg/kg oil kept a year at room temperature drops to about 270 to 290 mg/kg [^S44] [^S45]; in green PET with daily opening the oil can exceed the legal peroxide limit within a month [^S303]; at 4 °C it keeps about 92 % for six months [^S45]. Oleocanthal-rich oils lose more than others [^S44], and oleocanthal oxidises to oleocanthalic acid [^S33]. Heating the spoon's worth of oil in a pan at 170 °C removes about three-quarters of its phenolics [^S41]. The morning raw spoon, taken from a recent-harvest bottle kept closed and cool, is the configuration that delivers the most of what the label promises.

### 10.3 What the spoon can be expected to do

- **Against the authorised biomarker.** The EUROLIVE arm that lowered oxidised LDL used about 1.7 tablespoons of a 366 mg/kg oil [^S13]. One tablespoon of a 500 mg/kg oil kept fresh delivers a similar phenolic dose; one tablespoon of an average oil delivers half to two-thirds of it.
- **Against blood pressure.** The −3 mmHg systolic effect came from 25 to 60 mL/day [^S178] [^S179]; a single tablespoon is below every positive dose tested. A higher-phenolic oil, not a larger volume, is the lever that raises the phenolic dose without more calories [^S298].
- **Against cholesterol.** If the spoon replaces butter or another saturated fat, LDL should fall by a few mg/dL [^S194] [^S206]; if it is added, LDL barely moves [^S225].
- **Against cardiovascular risk.** The cohort curves put one tablespoon on their steep segment [^S135] [^S131], with the strongest contrast against animal fats [^S130]. This is an association, and the absolute benefit for a low-risk adult is small.
- **Against inflammation, pain, cancer and dementia.** No human trial at a tablespoon dose supports an effect. The EVOO-only inflammatory pool is null [^S89]; no pain trial exists; the cancer evidence is cellular; and the brain evidence that is human is either null at two tablespoons a day [^S259] or a small pilot [^S257].

### 10.4 What it costs

About 120 kcal a day, or about 44,000 kcal a year if nothing else changes. Trials in which olive oil replaced other fats show no weight gain [^S121] [^S283]; no trial tested an uncompensated added shot. Two tablespoons a day measurably loosen stool [^S297]. A fasted bolus contracts the gallbladder [^S300], which is a reason to take it with food for anyone with known gallstones. On antiplatelet drugs or anticoagulants, a switch to a very high-oleocanthal oil is worth mentioning to the prescriber [^S87].

### 10.5 Procurement checklist, with the evidence for each item

1. A stated harvest date, and the most recent harvest available: phenolics fall about 40 to 46 % a year [^S44] [^S45].
2. A stated phenolic value with its method and date: Folin, hydrolysis-HPLC and qNMR differ by a factor of two to three [^S35] [^S37].
3. Cultivar and early harvest: early harvest and some cultivars give more secoiridoids [^S20] [^S39].
4. Opaque packaging (tin, bag-in-box, multilayer) over PET or clear glass [^S286] [^S303].
5. Detailed labelling as a quality signal: oils with harvest year, cultivar and extraction method scored better regardless of price [^S295].
6. Bitterness and a throat catch as crude presence signs of secoiridoids and oleocanthal [^S53] [^S57], not as dose meters [^S59].
7. Avoid pomace oil for its contaminant burden [^S299] [^S268].

## 11. Funding and the Shape of the Evidence Base

Industry money and producer-donated oil run through this literature, and they cluster in identifiable places. PREDIMED's oil was donated by Hojiblanca and Patrimonio Comunal Olivarero and its nuts by nut-sector bodies [^S121]; CORDIOPREV was co-funded by two olive-oil-sector foundations [^S128]; OLIVAUS was part-funded and supplied by Cobram Estate [^S224]; the founding antiplatelet trial was part-funded by an olive-oil producer [^S70]; an Australian producer funded a mouse amyloid study [^S258]; and the US oleocanthal-brain network declares roles and equity in Oleolive LLC [^S250] [^S257] [^S263]. A supportive phenolic meta-analysis was funded by an olive-sector EU project and argued for labelling [^S227]. On the other side of the ledger, the Cretan null trial was sponsored by Unilever [^S6], and the olive-leaf pain trial had Nestlé-employed authors [^S85].

Two other structural features matter as much as funding. Much of the phenolic-specific human data comes from a small network of Spanish and Greek groups [^S22], and much of the oleocanthal analytical work comes from one Athens laboratory that also characterised the oils in several clinical studies [^S20] [^S87]. And the US cohort data, which are NIH-funded and point in the same direction as the Spanish data, differ on the key point: they find no advantage for olive oil over other vegetable oils [^S125] [^S130]. Funding does not make a finding wrong. It does mean the most commercially useful claims (the specific value of high-phenolic oil, the brain mechanism, the ibuprofen comparison) have the least independent replication.

## 12. Method and Limits of This Review

Seven schema-free evidence sweeps were run in parallel, each instructed to pull every identifier from NCBI E-utilities rather than from memory, to read abstracts before citing, to quote numbers as published, to record funding where stated, and to flag wrong premises in its own brief. Each sweep ended in an identifiers table; the tables, not the prose, were parsed into 306 unique PMIDs, every one resolved against the PubMed esummary record and author- and year-matched to its claim. Two retracted papers (the 2013 PREDIMED primary report and the 2016 PREDIMED bodyweight paper) were identified and excluded as sources; five published correction notices are cited only as correction records. The bibliography was generated from the verified records and every citation in this report was converted from a PMID token, so no source outside the verified set can be cited.

The briefs' own premises were wrong in several places, and the agents flagged them: Beauchamp 2005 compared oleocanthal and ibuprofen at equal molar concentrations rather than showing equal COX-1 and COX-2 inhibition [^S54]; the "MIF" target is MIP-1α [^S61]; "Valls 2015 VOHF" conflates two trials [^S170] [^S175]; the "Hernáez VOHF" papers are EUROLIVE and PREDIMED sub-studies [^S205] [^S213]; "1,588 at 11 sites" misreads a sensitivity-analysis exclusion [^S116]; the PREDIMED breast-cancer paper was corrected, not retracted [^S115]; MICOIL is a Greek trial, not a Kaddoumi trial [^S253]; and "typical 100 to 500 mg/kg oleocanthal" overstates normal oils [^S20].

The limits are those of abstract-level synthesis. Several numbers exist only in full texts that could not be retrieved (PREDIMED's per-protocol hazard ratios, its achieved control-arm fat intake, the Nature paper's oil concentration, Abuznait 2013's dose), and they are not stated here. The EFSA opinion and the EU regulation were read through their quotation in the analytical literature, not from the source pages. Abstract errors were found and flagged rather than corrected silently: swapped oil labels [^S26], a sign error [^S176], an implausible Cmax [^S50], an ambiguous pass rate [^S38] and a dose printed in milligrams that is almost certainly grams [^S67]. Evidence grades follow the shared methodology.

Scope boundaries were set deliberately. Olive-leaf extracts, isolated hydroxytyrosol capsules and oleocanthal-oleacein extract tablets appear only where they clarify a mechanism or a dose, because they are not culinary oil [^S85] [^S185] [^S193] [^S262]. Table olives appear only for acrylamide [^S301]. Topical use, olive-oil-based infant skin care and the olive-pollen allergy literature beyond the two oral-challenge cases were not reviewed. Whole Mediterranean-diet trials were included because they are the source of most hard-outcome claims, and their results are attributed to the diet pattern throughout, never to the oil alone. Where a study tested a dose far above one tablespoon, the dose is stated next to the result so that the reader can judge transfer. Industry funding is reported where an abstract, PubMed grant list or open-access acknowledgement stated it; a missing funding statement is recorded as not stated, never inferred.

## 13. Evidence by Population

The same oil behaves differently across the populations in which it has been tested, and the findings do not transfer automatically from one to another.

**Older adults at high cardiovascular risk.** This is PREDIMED's population (55 to 80, diabetes or three risk factors) [^S116]. The randomised benefit of the Mediterranean package was about 1.4 percentage points over five years on the primary composite, mainly fewer strokes [^S123], with secondary signals for atrial fibrillation and diabetes in the EVOO arm [^S102] [^S104]. The within-trial dose analyses favour high EVOO intakes of 40 to 60 g/day [^S141]. The cognitive sub-studies in this group show small differences driven by control-arm decline [^S244].

**Patients with established coronary heart disease.** CORDIOPREV gives the largest absolute effect, 9.6 fewer events per 1,000 person-years, against a real low-fat diet, seen in men [^S128], with regression of carotid intima-media thickness [^S127] and better FMD and endothelial repair markers [^S181]. Virgin oil against refined oil lowered oxidised LDL and, in the hypertensive subgroup, systolic pressure in 40 coronary patients [^S146]. Hydroxytyrosol capsules improved FMD in chronic coronary syndrome [^S185].

**People with type 2 diabetes or prediabetes.** HbA1c falls by about 0.27 % with olive-oil interventions [^S216]; oleocanthal-rich oil dampens postprandial platelet reactivity [^S87] and oxidative markers [^S88]; 10 g of EVOO added to a meal lowers the glucose excursion in impaired fasting glucose [^S212]; and in prediabetes, oleocanthal-rich EVOO against common oil moved interferon-γ and oxidative status [^S86] while pure hydroxytyrosol at 15 mg/day lowered oxidised LDL [^S91].

**People with hypertension.** The drug-sparing result against sunflower oil came from 23 patients [^S143]; virgin oil lowered systolic pressure in hypertensive coronary patients [^S146]; and PREDIMED's ambulatory data in a mostly treated hypertensive subsample show a 4.0 mmHg systolic contrast with control that the nut arm matched [^S164]. Anyone on antihypertensive drugs who makes a large fat switch should monitor pressure rather than change medication unilaterally.

**Healthy younger and middle-aged adults.** EUROLIVE (healthy men) shows the phenolic dose-response on oxidised LDL and a sub-millimolar HDL increment [^S13]; OLIVAUS (healthy Australians, two-thirds women) found no between-oil differences at 60 mL/day [^S224] [^S226]; the cohort associations apply [^S130] but with low baseline risk, so the absolute effect is small. This is the population closest to a daily-tablespoon user in good health, and it is the one in which the phenolic-specific effects are least robust.

**People with mild cognitive impairment.** The trials are pilots with no no-oil control in one and an unblinded diet arm in another [^S257] [^S253], and the 2026 pool rates the evidence low certainty [^S267].

## 14. Olive Oil and the Seed-Oil Debate

Olive oil is often framed as the "safe" alternative to seed oils, and the evidence in this review lets the comparison be stated by endpoint rather than by reputation.

**Blood lipids.** On LDL cholesterol, apoB and LDL particle number, linoleic-rich oils outperform olive oil. Corn oil lowered non-HDL cholesterol nearly tenfold more than EVOO in a controlled-feeding crossover [^S215]; across 27 trials olive oil lowered LDL by 4.2 mg/dL less than other plant oils [^S218]; and in the 54-trial network safflower and rapeseed ranked above olive oil for LDL [^S217]. Olive oil raises HDL cholesterol and apoA1 more [^S215] [^S218]. The Mensink regression gives the same ordering per percent of energy: PUFA lowers LDL more than MUFA against carbohydrate [^S194].

**Oxidation in the pan.** Under heat the order reverses. EVOO and other olive oils outlast PUFA-rich oils before reaching the 25 % polar-compound discard limit [^S276] [^S284] [^S291], produce fewer aldehydes in fumes and in the oil [^S269] [^S288], and leave fewer PUFA-derived aldehydes in fried food [^S282]. The vegetable-oil blend in one study carried more vitamin E and still oxidised faster [^S276]. None of these are human outcome studies, and in every oil the natural antioxidants were largely gone within 8 to 12 h of frying [^S284].

**Hard outcomes.** In the US cohorts, olive oil was associated with lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality against butter, margarine, mayonnaise and dairy fat, and showed no significant difference against other vegetable oils combined [^S125] [^S130]. The dementia-mortality analysis likewise found no benefit when olive oil replaced other vegetable oils [^S138]. Lyon's positive secondary-prevention trial used an ALA-rich canola margarine, not olive oil [^S93] [^S123]. Genetic instruments for plasma oleic, linoleic and α-linolenic acid share a pleiotropic FADS1 signal and cannot adjudicate between the oils [^S124]. A dietary MUFA-rich pattern did not reduce LDL as much as n-6 PUFA in the DIVAS trial but also reduced E-selectin and night-time systolic pressure [^S209].

**Phenolics.** The one dimension on which EVOO has no seed-oil counterpart is its phenolic fraction, which is also the dimension refining removes [^S42]. The phenolic-specific benefits (oxidised LDL, about 3 mmHg systolic, a small HDL increment) are measured against refined olive oil, not against seed oils [^S178]. A refined or "light" olive oil therefore shares the fatty-acid profile and frying stability of EVOO and none of its phenolic case.

For a daily user the comparison resolves into two uses. For cooking at high temperature, EVOO is at least as stable as the common seed oils by every oxidation measure tested. For blood lipids, a seed oil replacing butter lowers LDL more than olive oil replacing butter. The cohort data suggest that the large gain in either case comes from moving away from animal and processed fats, and that the choice between olive oil and other vegetable oils is a smaller decision than the choice to make that move.

## 15. Open Questions

1. Does intact oleocanthal, or a defined metabolite such as oleoglycine, reach human plasma or brain at any measurable level after food-dose EVOO?
2. Is there a randomised hard-outcome trial in which EVOO is compared with refined olive oil or a seed oil at equal fat, rather than with low-fat advice?
3. Does the phenolic systolic effect of about 3 mmHg persist beyond 12 weeks at a one-tablespoon dose?
4. Which analytical method should define the EU claim threshold, and how much of the claimed dose survives the months between bottling and the last spoon?
5. Do HDL-efflux gains replicate outside the EUROLIVE and PREDIMED networks?
6. Does a tablespoon-scale oleocanthal-rich oil change platelet function in people on antiplatelet drugs or anticoagulants?
7. Does a fasted bolus provoke biliary symptoms in people with gallstones?
8. Can the brain findings be reproduced in humans with direct barrier measures (verapamil PET) and an independent laboratory?

## The full findings — all 45

Every conclusion this review reached, grouped by how strongly the evidence backs it. Each finding is distilled from the fully-cited evidence above.

??? note "Tier 1 — Settled (High Confidence) (13 findings)"

    **1. Parent oleocanthal has never been measured in human plasma, and in animals it is not detected because its two aldehydes react with glycine and proteins.**

    "None was able to detect oleocanthal in biological fluids"; it disappears on addition to plasma, forming oleoglycine. In mice dosed intraperitoneally it was absent at every time point with 13 metabolites found, and in rats dosed orally it appeared only in stomach and intestine.

    **2. "Ibuprofen-like" describes the mechanism in a test tube, not the dose: a tablespoon of even a high-phenolic oil holds 0.3 to 3.4 % of a 200 to 400 mg ibuprofen tablet by mass.**

    The 2005 Nature brief compared the two compounds at equal molar concentrations in a purified COX assay, with no human dosing; at 25 µM oleocanthal inhibited 41 to 57 % of COX activity and ibuprofen 13 to 18 %. A 13.5 g tablespoon at 100 to 500 mg/kg carries 1.35 to 6.8 mg, and a 400 mg tablet reaches 150 to 178 µM in plasma.

    **3. The throat sting comes from oleocanthal activating TRPA1 in the pharynx, the same channel ibuprofen activates, and it signals presence rather than dose.**

    Oleocanthal selectively activates hTRPA1, concentrated in the pharynx; the throat-burning fraction is deacetoxy-ligstroside aglycone while oleacein stings mainly the tongue. Sensitivity varies widely between people with a possible TRPV1 contribution, and egg or whey protein can abolish the sting.

    **4. The only authorised EU health claim for olive-oil phenolics is a biomarker claim, "protection of blood lipids from oxidative stress", at ≥5 mg of hydroxytyrosol and derivatives per 20 g of oil (≥250 mg/kg).**

    The regulation names no analytical method, which led the sector to limit use of the claim; the 250 mg/kg conversion is universal in the literature, and the wording counts tyrosol as a "derivative", which pulls oleocanthal's family under the claim. The claim says nothing about heart disease, inflammation, pain or the brain.

    **5. One 13.5 g tablespoon reaches the claim's 5 mg only if the oil holds about 370 mg/kg at the moment you eat it; a threshold oil gives about 3.4 mg.**

    The arithmetic follows from the condition of use (5 mg per 20 g) and the tablespoon mass; the EUROLIVE arm that lowered oxidised LDL delivered about 8.4 mg/day from 25 mL of a 366 mg/kg oil.

    **6. The phenolic number on a label depends on the laboratory and the method: the same 30 oils qualified for the claim 1 to 21 times out of 30 across protocols.**

    Correction factors alone moved results 2.2 to 2.5-fold, and qNMR read 1.77 mg/20 g higher than the reference UHPLC on average. Summed LC-MS values ran 1.9 to 3.0 times the global methods, and Folin put 59 % of oils below 250 mg/kg against 26 % by LC-MS/MS.

    **7. Refining removes essentially all hydroxytyrosol and tyrosol, so refined and "light" olive oils share EVOO's fatty acids and none of its phenolic case.**

    Total hydroxytyrosol and tyrosol "are completely removed in the early steps of the refining procedure", while tocopherols survive; EUROLIVE's control oil held 2.7 mg/kg, and free hydroxytyrosol was "nearly undetectable" after ordinary olive oil.

    **8. Phenolics fall by roughly 40 to 46 % in the first year at room temperature, faster in warm storage and in oleocanthal-rich oils.**

    5,764 Greek samples lost 46 % in 12 months, with 92 % kept at 4 °C over six months; 160 oils lost 42.0 ± 24.3 % with larger losses in oleacein- and oleocanthal-dominated oils; loss follows pseudo-first-order kinetics that speed up at 25 °C; over 24 months oleacein fell about 75 % and oleocanthal 60 %.

    **9. About 98 % of absorbed hydroxytyrosol circulates as conjugates, and free hydroxytyrosol peaks near 0.03 µM after 25 mL of EVOO.**

    Plasma hydroxytyrosol peaked at 32 min with a 2.43 h half-life and no free form detected; a sensitive method measured free hydroxytyrosol at 4.40 ng/mL. Apparent absorption is 55 to 66 % with heavy transformation, and the main glucuronides show no antioxidant activity at 0.01 to 10 µM.

    **10. PREDIMED's 2013 report was retracted for randomisation failures and republished with almost unchanged estimates: HR 0.69 for the EVOO arm and 0.72 for the nut arm.**

    Household co-enrolment, non-random assignment at one site and inconsistent tables at another were disclosed, and results were similar after excluding 1,588 participants; the bodyweight paper counts 866 (11.6 %) as affected. The investigators acknowledged early termination and unequal intervention intensity.

    **11. Olive oil lowers LDL cholesterol only when it replaces saturated fat; added on top of a diet it is close to neutral, and linoleic-rich oils lower LDL and apoB more.**

    Per percent of energy against carbohydrate, cis-MUFA's LDL coefficient (−0.006 mmol/L) is not different from zero, against +0.033 for SFA and −0.014 for PUFA; replacing lard, butter or ghee lowers LDL or apoB; adding 10 g/day moves LDL +0.04 mg/dL; corn oil beat EVOO on non-HDL cholesterol, apoB and LDL particles.

    **12. Under heat EVOO is among the most oxidation-stable common oils, but heat strips 40 to 75 % of its phenolics in a domestic sauté.**

    Olive oils lasted 24 to 28+ hours of frying before the 25 % polar-compound limit against 15 to 20 h for seed oils, with fewer aldehydes in fumes and food; a sauté cut polyphenols 40 % at 120 °C and 75 % at 170 °C, and ten frying cycles removed 70 to 93 %.

    **13. The randomised hard-outcome evidence tests a Mediterranean dietary package, not EVOO: no trial has compared EVOO with refined olive oil or a seed oil on heart attacks, strokes or death.**

    PREDIMED compared Mediterranean counselling plus free EVOO or nuts with low-fat advice; CORDIOPREV compared an EVOO-based Mediterranean diet with a low-fat diet; Lyon used an ALA-rich canola margarine.


??? note "Tier 2 — Well-supported (Moderate Confidence) (13 findings)"

    **14. PREDIMED's benefit was carried mainly by stroke; cardiovascular death and total mortality were not clearly reduced.**

    With both Mediterranean arms combined, stroke fell from 24 to 14 per 1,000 over 4.8 years (HR 0.60), while cardiovascular mortality HR was 0.81 (0.50 to 1.32) and total mortality 1.0; the investigators stated that PREDIMED cannot settle all-cause mortality.

    **15. CORDIOPREV gave the largest absolute effect, 9.6 fewer events per 1,000 person-years in coronary patients against a real low-fat diet, seen in men and co-funded by olive-oil foundations.**

    Events ran 87 against 111 (28.1 vs 37.7 per 1,000 person-years), adjusted HRs 0.719 to 0.753, men HR 0.669, women no difference; carotid intima-media thickness regressed only on the Mediterranean diet.

    **16. Cohorts consistently associate olive oil with 15 to 20 % lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, with most of the association reached by about 18 to 22 g/day.**

    Per 10 g/day, CVD fell 7 % and all-cause mortality 8 % across 2.7 million participants, with risk falling over 0 to 18 g/day and 0 to 22 g/day; 13 cohorts showed little extra benefit beyond about 20 g/day; the Spanish, Italian and US cohorts agree in direction.

    **17. In the US cohorts the olive-oil advantage is over butter, margarine, mayonnaise and dairy fat, not over other vegetable oils.**

    More than half a tablespoon a day carried CVD HR 0.86 and all-cause mortality HR 0.81, and replacing 5 to 10 g/day of animal or processed fats went with 5 to 34 % lower risk, but olive oil showed no significant difference against other plant oils combined; the meta-analysis of 841,211 people found the signal for olive oil and none for MUFA as a nutrient class.

    **18. Holding the fat constant, high-phenolic oil lowers oxidised LDL most consistently, which is the endpoint behind the EU claim.**

    EUROLIVE's 366 mg/kg oil lowered oxidised LDL by 3.21 U/L with an interval excluding zero; pools give SMD −0.25, −0.44 and −0.68 against refined oil; phenol metabolites travel on the LDL particle itself.

    **19. EVOO lowers systolic blood pressure by about 3 mmHg against refined olive oil over weeks, with no diastolic effect.**

    The network meta-analysis of 13 trials found −2.87 to −2.99 mmHg for high- and low-phenolic (E)VOO against refined oil, with a phenolic dose-response, at low to moderate certainty; within-oil trials point the same way. Sustained, that would map to about 5 to 6 % lower relative cardiovascular risk on the drug-trial slope.

    **20. Broad EVOO pools find no significant effect on blood pressure, and the headline large drops come from trials of 23 and 24 people.**

    33 EVOO trials showed no blood-pressure effect; olive and flaxseed trials pooled to −0.48 mmHg. The 48 % drug-dose reduction came from 23 hypertensive patients against sunflower oil, and the −7.9 mmHg figure is a change from baseline, not a between-oil contrast.

    **21. The phenolic effect on cholesterol is small: HDL rises 1 to 2 mg/dL, and LDL falls about 4 to 5 mg/dL only at high phenolic doses.**

    EUROLIVE's phenolic HDL increment was about 0.8 mg/dL; pools give HDL +1.13 to +2.37 mg/dL, LDL −0.14 mmol/L for high against low phenolic and LDL −4.28 mg/dL only in the 320 to 600 mg/kg tertile.

    **22. An EVOO-only meta-analysis of 33 trials found no effect on CRP, IL-6, IL-10 or TNF-α; inflammatory reductions appear inside Mediterranean diets.**

    EVOO lowered insulin (SMD −0.28) and HOMA-IR (SMD −0.19) and nothing inflammatory; the positive pools mix olive oil into dietary patterns or carry I² of 92 to 99 %; the largest oleocanthal-rich trial differed from common oil only on interferon-γ.

    **23. The most reproducible human effect of oleocanthal-rich oil is an acute dampening of ex vivo platelet aggregation after 40 mL, in two trials of 9 and 10 people.**

    Collagen-stimulated aggregation fell 2 h after 40 mL of oleocanthal-rich oil, correlated with oleocanthal intake, with the authors concluding COX blockade was not responsible; in type 2 diabetes the 500 mg/kg oil (about 18 mg of oleocanthal) matched a 400 mg ibuprofen meal on ADP and TRAP aggregation only.

    **24. In people with type 2 diabetes, olive-oil interventions lower HbA1c by about 0.27 percentage points.**

    29 trials gave HbA1c −0.27 % and fasting glucose −0.44 mmol/L, with four cohorts at RR 0.84 for incident diabetes; high-MUFA diets lowered HbA1c by 0.21 %. PREDIMED's diabetes HR of 0.60 is a secondary-outcome subgroup against low-fat advice.

    **25. A typical premium EVOO holds about 340 to 420 mg/kg of phenolics by LC-MS/MS, and one in six to one in four would not qualify for the claim.**

    200 competition oils averaged 421 and 343 mg/kg with 16 to 27 % below 250 mg/kg; Greek qNMR samples averaged 483 mg/kg (Koroneiki 434) with 74 % above 250; oleocanthal and oleacein each ranged from non-detectable to 355 mg/kg across 175 monovarietal oils.

    **26. A large share of retail "EVOO" fails at least one grade criterion, mostly through refined-oil admixture or sensory defects, and the rate depends on the method.**

    An FDA FT-NIR screen flagged 62.5 % of 88 US products while IOC fatty-acid ranges would flag fewer than 10 %; three accredited panels judged most of six oils not extra virgin and disagreed with each other; better-labelled oils scored better regardless of price.


??? note "Tier 3 — Preliminary / Contested (8 findings)"

    **27. HDL efflux improved with high-phenolic oil in one EUROLIVE sub-study of 47 men and did not replicate in OLIVAUS at more than twice the dose.**

    Efflux rose 3.05 % against −2.34 % (P = 0.042); OLIVAUS at 60 mL/day found no efflux change in either arm; PREDIMED's efflux gain was against baseline, not control.

    **28. EVOO-only secondary signals in PREDIMED (atrial fibrillation HR 0.62, diabetes HR 0.60, breast cancer HR 0.32) are post hoc or secondary analyses with few events; all were corrected, none retracted.**

    The nut arm was null on each; corrections were published in 2018 and 2019; the breast-cancer result rests on 35 cases.

    **29. Virgin and extra-virgin oils track with lower mortality than common refined olive oil in Spanish cohorts, but no trial has compared grades on hard outcomes.**

    Virgin oil carried all-cause HR 0.66 against 0.96 for common oil; within PREDIMED the top EVOO tertile carried HR 0.75 while common oil was borderline; associations were stronger for virgin oil across three Spanish cohorts.

    **30. Higher-phenolic oil gives better microvascular and conduit-artery reactivity after a meal, but large olive-oil fat loads alone can blunt FMD for hours.**

    50 mL of high-polyphenol EVOO against refined oil gave a 4.8-point FMD gap at 2 h; phenol-rich breakfasts improved reactive hyperaemia; a 50 g olive-oil fat load cut FMD by 31 % while the same oil with salad and vinegar did not.

    **31. Olive oil raises fasting FMD by about 0.8 percentage points, an effect similar to tea, cocoa, soy, nuts and vegetables.**

    Eight trials gave +0.76 points; Mediterranean diets give +1.66 to +2.32 points, against tea +3.22 and cocoa +1.68; CORDIOPREV gave 3.83 against 1.16 % in coronary patients.

    **32. The oleocanthal amyloid-clearance mechanism (P-glycoprotein and LRP1 at the blood-brain barrier) is a mouse finding from one commercially linked network, with no human measurement.**

    Oleocanthal raised Aβ brain efflux from 62.0 to 79.9 % in mice and lowered amyloid at 5 mg/kg/day injected; the in vivo reports are "uniformly American" at moderate risk of bias, and investigators declare roles in Oleolive LLC.

    **33. Small MCI trials of high-phenolic oil are pilots; in the US trial refined olive oil improved the dementia rating at least as much as EVOO.**

    CDR-SOB fell from 2.1 to 0.38 with refined oil and from 1.88 to 1.0 with EVOO, and the overall barrier-permeability change was not significant; MICOIL's abstract gives no n, effect size or phenolic content; a five-trial pool rates the evidence low certainty (SMD 0.29).

    **34. Cohort associations with dementia-related death are consistent but cannot separate oil grades and vanish against other vegetable oils.**

    ≥7 g/day carried HR 0.72 for dementia-related death, with no benefit when olive oil replaced other vegetable oils or butter; a German cohort aged 75+ found nothing for olive oil; virgin oil tracked better and common oil worse cognitive change in one Spanish analysis.


??? note "Tier 4 — Refuted Claims & Myths (6 findings)"

    **35. "A spoon of EVOO is a natural painkiller": no randomised trial of EVOO or oleocanthal-rich oil on pain exists.**

    The closest evidence is olive extracts in osteoarthritis (SMD 0.40 from three trials) and a null six-month olive-leaf trial; the cell anti-inflammatory work used 10 to 100 µM of a compound not detected in plasma.

    **36. "Oleocanthal clears amyloid from the human brain": the largest trial that supplied daily EVOO found no cognitive or MRI benefit.**

    The MIND arm received about two tablespoons of EVOO a day for three years with global cognition +0.205 against +0.170 in controls (P = 0.23) and similar MRI measures; the PREDIMED cognitive differences came mainly from control-arm decline.

    **37. "PREDIMED's retraction means the olive-oil evidence collapsed" and "PREDIMED proved EVOO prevents heart attacks" are both wrong.**

    The republished estimates barely moved (0.70 to 0.69), while myocardial infarction and cardiovascular death were not clearly reduced and the nut arm did as well.

    **38. "Never cook with EVOO because of its smoke point" is not supported: smoke point mainly tracks free acidity and is a poor guide to oxidation products.**

    Smoke point relies on subjective visual assessment and is predicted by free fatty acid content; EVOO formed fewer polar compounds and aldehydes than seed oils, and oil type did not change acrylamide in fried potatoes.

    **39. "Oleic acid is the protective agent" lacks genetic and human support.**

    Genetically higher serum oleic acid was not associated with CHD, MI or stroke; stroke and VTE associations were driven by FADS1 pleiotropy; MUFA-for-carbohydrate trials move blood pressure between 0 and 2.6 mmHg; the rat oleic-acid hypothesis cannot explain differences between oils with identical oleic acid.

    **40. "EVOO lowers blood pressure through nitro-oleic acid" has no human evidence.**

    Nitro-fatty acids occur in EVOO and form under simulated gastric nitration; the mouse blood-pressure study injected nitro-oleic acid and used linoleic acid plus nitrite in its dietary arm; in humans, adding a fat to nitrite blunted its vasodilatory action.


??? note "Practical — what to actually do (5 findings)"

    **41. Buy for freshness and a stated method, not for the word "extra virgin": a recent harvest date, a phenolic value with its method and date, and opaque packaging predict what reaches the spoon.**

    Phenolics fall 40 to 46 % a year; methods differ two- to threefold; multilayer packs, tin and bag-in-box outperformed green glass and PET; early harvest and some cultivars give more secoiridoids.

    **42. Use the daily tablespoon raw and let it replace butter, margarine or mayonnaise rather than adding to them.**

    Raw use keeps the phenolics that a 170 °C sauté removes; the LDL and cohort benefits are largest against saturated and processed fats; an uncompensated added tablespoon is about 44,000 kcal a year, while fat-swap trials show no weight gain.

    **43. A higher-phenolic oil, not a larger volume, raises the phenolic dose without more calories; the positive phenolic trials used 25 to 60 mL/day.**

    8 g of a 1,021 mg/kg oil delivered the same 8.3 mg/day as 20 g of a 414 mg/kg oil and did better on total cholesterol; the blood-pressure effects came from 25 to 60 mL/day.

    **44. No trial supports taking olive oil as a fasted morning shot rather than with food, and a fasted bolus contracts the gallbladder.**

    No timing trial exists; EVOO is the best matrix for its own hydroxytyrosol; a 50 mL EVOO bolus is a clinical gallbladder-contraction test meal, and the "gallbladder flush" rests on one uncontrolled case report.

    **45. Safety at food doses is good: no documented allergy to the oil, no warfarin interaction study, a measurable antiplatelet effect at 40 mL, and the contaminant burden concentrated in pomace oil.**

    Olive-fruit-allergic patients tolerated the oil on challenge; acute 40 mL oleocanthal-rich doses dampen platelet aggregation; pomace oils carry the PAH and mineral-oil load and virgin oils the lowest refining contaminants. Tell the prescriber before switching to a very high-oleocanthal oil while on antiplatelet drugs or anticoagulants.
