# Vitamin C: What It Actually Does vs What People Think

> A 200 mg nutrient sold as a gram-dose medicine: the body fills at food doses, excretes the rest, and the gram-dose trials are null where they are not harmful. Plasma is 68 µmol/L on 200 mg a day and 85 on 2,500; daily vitamin C does not prevent colds and shortens them by about 8 %; 500 mg a day for eight to nine years in 22,812 people changed no cardiovascular, cancer or mortality outcome; men taking a gram a day have 40 to 90 % more kidney stones; and intravenous vitamin C harmed septic and COVID-19 patients in the largest trials.

*Literature reviewed to 8 November 2026. 329 sources, every identifier resolved against PubMed.*

## Start here

    **Vitamin C is a 200 mg nutrient sold as a gram-dose medicine: plasma saturates at food doses (68 µmol/L on 200 mg a day, 85 on 2,500), white cells are full at 100 to 400 mg, anything above a 500 mg dose is increasingly excreted, and the gram-dose trials are null for colds, hearts, cancer and training while raising men's kidney-stone risk and, by vein, harming septic and COVID-19 patients.**

**What you actually learn**

- **The curve is the whole story:** the steep part of the dose-to-plasma curve lies between 30 and 100 mg a day, 200 mg is the first dose beyond it, and 2,500 mg buys 17 µmol/L more than 200. The renal threshold sits at 72 to 86 µmol/L, and above it clearance approaches the glomerular filtration rate.
- **Oral and intravenous are different drugs:** 1.25 g by mouth peaks at 135 µmol/L and the same dose by vein at 885; 50 g by vein reaches 13,400. Nothing a pill does happens at the concentrations the intravenous literature depends on.
- **The requirement debate is 75 to 200 mg on the same data:** leukocyte saturation gave the Institute of Medicine 75 to 90 mg, a 50 µmol/L mass balance gave the German societies 110 and 95 mg, and the plasma curve gave the NIH group 200 mg. Every camp's number is a piece of fruit.
- **Status has bands and one valid test:** deficiency below 11.4 µmol/L, marginal at or below 23, adequate at or above 50, saturating above 65; fasting plasma in a promptly stabilised EDTA tube is the measure, and baseline status and body weight shift the whole curve.
- **The one large cold effect is in people under brief heavy exertion:** RR 0.48 in 598 marathon runners, skiers and sub-arctic soldiers at 0.6 to 1 g a day, from small old trials; a 674-recruit trial at 2 g was null.
- **Low plasma ascorbate is the population a pill moves:** men in the bottom tenth gained 5.4 mL/kg/min of VO2max and 18.7 Nm of torque from 1 g for 30 days, men below 45 µmol/L had fewer colds, and low-baseline office workers lost fatigue. All of it is repletion, and 200 mg a day does it in two weeks.
- **The stone mechanism is quantified and not an assay artefact:** 1.2 to 1.8 % of a dose's millimoles become oxalate, the controlled studies used preservatives, EDTA and isotopes to exclude in-vitro conversion, and 40 % of people are high responders. On HPFS rates the men's excess is about one extra stone per 80 men a decade.
- **Oxalate nephropathy needs two of three:** intravenous grams, kidneys that cannot clear oxalate, or a gut that over-absorbs it. Oral cases carry the second factor, at doses as low as 680 mg with chronic diarrhoea.
- **Iron is a meal-level effect:** about 50 mg of vitamin C in the same meal maximises non-haem iron absorption, the whole-diet effect is small, adding it to iron tablets adds nothing, and food ascorbate equals crystals.
- **The claims that fail outright:** rebound scurvy (two subjects' urine dipped for ten days), B12 destruction (an assay artefact refuted in 1976), the warfarin interaction (one 1971 case, one 1975 negative study), prevention of cataract, macular degeneration or dementia, and collagen-building in replete adults.
- **Vitamin C alone is cleared of the antioxidant mortality signal:** across 29 low-bias trials RR 1.02 (0.98 to 1.07), while β-carotene, vitamin A and vitamin E raised mortality.

!!! tip "What this means for you"
    - **Keep vitamin C in the food column:** if you do not smoke and eat fruit or vegetables several times a day, your plasma sits at 50 to 80 µmol/L and your cells are full; a pill buys about 10 µmol/L and a few hours of higher urine.
    - **Know your 200 mg:** two gold kiwifruit, or a red pepper with an orange. Boiling vegetables costs 20 to 60 % of their vitamin C; raw fruit and pepper cost nothing.
    - **Bend the rule for status, not dose:** if you smoke, weigh over about 100 kg, eat little produce or test below 50 µmol/L, you are a repletion case at 100 to 200 mg a day from food or a small tablet, not a gram.
    - **The one defensible pill use case:** a short course of 0.5 to 1 g a day around an ultramarathon, a ski camp or similar exertion in cold or crowded conditions.
    - **If you take a pill anyway, keep it to 200 to 400 mg:** that is the range in which no renal signal appeared in any cohort band, and do not pair it with vitamin E at 1 g during a training block.
    - **Men: a gram a day is a stone risk with no offsetting benefit** at that dose in a non-deficient adult.
    - **Never gram doses if you have:** a prior calcium-oxalate stone, kidney disease or dialysis, gastric bypass or other malabsorption, chronic diarrhoea, G6PD deficiency (for infusions), iron overload, or chemotherapy without your oncologist's agreement.
    - **Do not expect it to prevent or treat a cold:** daily use shortens a cold by about half a day; starting at onset has no consistent effect.
    - **Stop supplements for a few days before a guaiac stool test:** ascorbate causes false negatives.
    - **To know rather than guess:** ask for a fasting plasma ascorbate in a promptly stabilised EDTA tube; at or above 50 µmol/L is adequate by every definition in these sources, above 65 saturating.

## 1. Executive Summary and the Chain of Claims

The owner's supplement stack lists vitamin C under "Avoid: standalone vitamin C supplement, get it from food". This dossier tests whether that rule survives the evidence, and what exactly a vitamin C pill does that food does not. The popular case for the pill runs as a chain of seven claims. First, that Linus Pauling proved gram doses prevent and cure colds and that medicine buried the result. Second, that "more is better": a 1,000 mg tablet keeps your levels high and your immune system armed. Third, that antioxidants help you recover from training. Fourth, that vitamin C protects the heart and the arteries. Fifth, that intravenous vitamin C treats cancer and sepsis, so a pill must be doing something in the same direction. Sixth, that whole-food or "Ester-C" or liposomal vitamin C is absorbed far better than plain ascorbic acid. Seventh, that 1,000 to 2,000 mg a day is harmless because the excess is simply excreted. This synthesis tests each link against 329 PubMed-verified records drawn from seven evidence sweeps: pharmacokinetics and requirements, the Pauling legacy and infection, exercise adaptation and recovery, cardiovascular and endothelial outcomes, renal toxicology and other safety signals, intravenous ascorbate in oncology and critical care, and food versus synthetic forms together with deficiency prevalence and the remaining consumer claims.

The short version has seven parts.

**The body fills up at food doses.** In the NIH depletion-repletion study, seven men hospitalised for four to six months showed a sigmoid dose-to-plasma curve whose steep part lay between 30 and 100 mg a day; 200 mg was the first dose beyond it, complete plasma saturation came at 1,000 mg, and neutrophils, monocytes and lymphocytes were saturated at 100 mg a day at concentrations at least 14 times plasma [^S11]. A reanalysis of the same data found that raising the dose from 200 to 2,500 mg lifts steady-state plasma only from 68.1 to 85.2 µmol/L [^S12]; in women, plasma and cells saturated at 400 mg [^S21]. Bioavailability is complete for a 200 mg dose and falls at 500 mg and above, with the absorbed surplus excreted [^S11]; the renal threshold sits near 86 µmol/L in men and 72 in women [^S8], and the plateau is as much tissue saturation as renal loss [^S13]. A 1.25 g oral dose peaks at 134.8 µmol/L while the same dose intravenously peaks at 885 µmol/L, and the maximum tolerable oral regimen is modelled to top out at 220 µmol/L against 13,400 for 50 g by vein [^S23]. No camp in the requirement debate argues for more than 200 mg on pharmacokinetic grounds: the Institute of Medicine landed at 75 to 90 mg on leukocyte saturation, the German-speaking societies at 95 to 110 mg on a 50 µmol/L plasma target with mass balance [^S42], and the NIH group at 200 mg from the plasma curve [^S16]. Two gold kiwifruit a day reach the saturation range [^S305].

**Pauling's case was an inference, and the cold data say "a little, with daily use, not at onset".** Pauling's 2.3 g optimum came from an evolutionary argument [^S56], his 1971 confidence levels from a pooled re-analysis of four small trials [^S57], and his 1974 band of 250 to 4,000 mg from the width of his own uncertainty [^S61]; he ran no trial. The 2,349-person Toronto trial found no significant effect in any of its eight arms and no dose-response from 0.25 to 2 g, with its authors putting the optimum below 250 mg [^S60]. The Cochrane review pools 10,708 community participants to an incidence rate ratio of 0.97 (0.94 to 1.00), a duration reduction of 8 % in adults and 14 % in children with daily use, and no consistent effect when started after onset; the one subgroup with a large effect is 598 marathon runners, skiers and sub-arctic soldiers, RR 0.48 (0.35 to 0.64) [^S83]. An independent Harvard meta-analysis gives RR 0.96 and a 9 % shorter illness [^S91]. The 6 to 8 g dose-response argument rests on two 1970s trials whose own authors read them as negative [^S89] [^S64] [^S60], and the two meta-analyses that supported extra-dose or paediatric benefit have been retracted (PubMed flags PMID 30069463 and PMID 30465062 as Retracted Publication). The one outpatient trial of a true megadose, 8 g a day for COVID-19, was null [^S95].

**The famous "antioxidants blunt training" results are vitamin C plus vitamin E results, and the outcome-level harm is small.** Ristow's 2009 trial used 1,000 mg of C with 400 IU of E [^S112]; Paulsen's endurance and strength trials, Bjørnsen's elderly trial and Morrison's interval trial all combined C with E [^S128] [^S129] [^S132] [^S131]. The vitamin-C-only human training trials are Gomez-Cabrera with five supplemented men [^S111], Roberts with no blunting at 1 g [^S116], Braakhuis with a 1.3 ± 1.3 % training-speed effect [^S126] and Khassaf at 500 mg [^S106]. Pooled across 18 RCTs, vitamin C and/or E did not reduce VO2max gains (SMD −0.14, −0.43 to 0.15), endurance (−0.01), lean mass (−0.07) or strength [^S143]. Marker-level blunting of PGC-1α, COX4 and SOD is real for the combination; the one outcome signal is a 1.4 % versus 3.9 % lean-mass gain in 60- to 81-year-old men on C plus E [^S132]. Men selected for low plasma ascorbate gained 5.4 mL/kg/min of VO2max and 18.7 Nm of torque from 1 g a day, a repletion effect [^S146]. Antioxidant supplements of any kind shave 0.2 to 0.5 cm off a 10 cm soreness scale, below the 1.4 cm a person can notice [^S138].

**Five hundred milligrams a day for eight to nine years did nothing to hearts, strokes or deaths.** The Physicians' Health Study II gave 14,641 men 500 mg a day for eight years: major cardiovascular events HR 0.99 (0.89 to 1.11), total mortality 1.07 (0.97 to 1.18) [^S176], total cancer 1.01 [^S178], cataract 1.02 [^S303]. The Women's Antioxidant Cardiovascular Study gave 8,171 high-risk women the same dose for 9.4 years: RR 1.02 (0.92 to 1.13) [^S172]. The Heart Protection Study raised plasma vitamin C by a third in 20,536 people for five years with a vascular-event rate ratio of 1.00 [^S163]. Acute 2 g doses and intra-arterial infusions improve endothelial dilation in sick arteries for a couple of hours [^S150] [^S152], but 1 g a day for eight weeks in smokers held plasma at 92 µmol/L and left flow-mediated dilation unchanged [^S157], and the chronic-only meta-analysis effect is SMD 0.25 with a lower bound of 0.02 [^S185]. Blood pressure falls about 4/1.5 mmHg in eight-week trials [^S181] and not at all over five years [^S164]. The observational signal, about 20 % lower mortality per 20 µmol/L of plasma ascorbate [^S162], is strongest when measured in blood and weakest when measured as intake [^S189], and Mendelian randomisation finds nothing for coronary disease [^S193] [^S194].

**The one safety signal with converging evidence is kidney stones in men at a gram a day.** Of a 1,000 mg dose, 1.2 to 1.8 % of the millimoles end up as urinary oxalate, 6 to 13 mg a day [^S205]; 2 g a day raised urinary oxalate 20 % in healthy people and 33 % in stone formers in a placebo-controlled crossover [^S223]; 1,000 mg twice daily raised oxalate and the Tiselius risk index in 40 % of participants [^S227]; oxalate and urate excretion were already up at 1,000 mg in the NIH study [^S11]. In 45,619 men followed 14 years, 1,000 mg or more a day carried RR 1.41 (1.11 to 1.80) for symptomatic stones [^S225]; in the pooled Harvard cohorts, men at 500 to 999 mg had HR 1.29 and at 1,000 mg or more HR 1.43, while women showed nothing at any band and food-only intake nothing in either sex [^S241]; Swedish supplement users had HR 1.92 [^S236]. Vitamin C alone carries no mortality signal across 29 low-bias trials, RR 1.02 (0.98 to 1.07) [^S180]. Rebound scurvy, B12 destruction and a warfarin interaction do not survive their own literature [^S202] [^S199] [^S197].

**Intravenous vitamin C is a different drug, and its trials do not favour it.** Oral dosing cannot reach the millimolar concentrations on which the entire pro-drug mechanism depends [^S23] [^S255]. The only randomised blinded cancer trials of oral megadoses, 10 g a day, were null [^S253] [^S254]. Fifty years on, the oncology IV literature is phase I trials with no single-agent responses [^S26] [^S40], a 38-patient single-arm phase II [^S282] and one open-label 34-patient randomised trial with a survival signal that awaits replication [^S286]. In sepsis the 872-patient LOVIT trial found more death or persistent organ dysfunction with vitamin C, RR 1.21 (1.04 to 1.40) [^S249]; in COVID-19 the harmonised LOVIT-COVID and REMAP-CAP trials in 2,590 patients were stopped for harm and futility [^S284].

**The molecule is the same whatever the label, and deficiency lives in identifiable groups.** Every steady-state human comparison finds kiwifruit-derived and synthetic vitamin C identical in plasma, white cells and muscle [^S35] [^S37]; the "bioflavonoids add 35 %" claim is one eight-person single-dose study from 1988 [^S291]. Ester-C matches plain ascorbate in plasma and is gentler on an acid-sensitive stomach [^S299]; liposomal forms raise a single dose's plasma area under the curve, and no study has measured whether the extra is kept or excreted [^S55]. About 7 % of US adults are deficient, unchanged since 2003 [^S30] [^S325]; smokers need roughly double the intake [^S323]; in Glasgow, fruit, vegetables or juice three or more times a day kept people above the threshold [^S294]. The food pattern that raises plasma ascorbate tracks with 20 to 50 % lower mortality in cohorts [^S162] [^S317], and that association has never been reproduced by giving the isolated molecule [^S178] [^S180].

The one-line verdict: **vitamin C is a 200 mg nutrient sold as a gram-dose medicine; the body fills at food doses, excretes the rest, and the gram-dose trials are null where they are not harmful.** The "get it from food" rule holds for a non-smoking adult who eats fruit or vegetables several times a day. The situations in which a pill changes anything are status questions, answerable with a plasma test: smokers, people eating almost no produce, people over about 100 kg, and anyone below roughly 50 µmol/L.

## 2. Pharmacokinetics: Saturation, Transport, Bioavailability and Requirements

### 2.1 The dose-to-plasma curve

The reference experiment is the NIH depletion-repletion study. Seven healthy men were hospitalised for four to six months on a diet containing less than 5 mg of vitamin C a day and then repleted at seven daily doses from 30 to 2,500 mg, with steady-state plasma and cell concentrations measured at each dose. The curve was sigmoid: the steep portion lay between 30 and 100 mg a day, the then-RDA of 60 mg sat on the lower third of the curve, 200 mg was the first dose beyond the sigmoid, and complete plasma saturation occurred at 1,000 mg. Neutrophils, monocytes and lymphocytes saturated at 100 mg a day and held concentrations at least 14-fold higher than plasma. Bioavailability was complete for a single 200 mg dose; no vitamin C appeared in the urine of six of the seven men until the 100 mg dose; at single doses of 500 mg and higher bioavailability declined and the absorbed amount was excreted; and oxalate and urate excretion were elevated at 1,000 mg a day. The authors proposed an RDA of 200 mg, obtainable from fruit and vegetables, and wrote that "vitamin C daily doses above 400 mg have no evident value" and that safe doses are below 1,000 mg [^S11]. A pharmacokinetic model fitted to the same men needed saturable absorption, saturable distribution and saturable renal reabsorption to describe the data, and concluded that the plateau from 200 to 2,500 mg "is largely due to apparent saturation of tissue uptake and less a function of oral bioavailability and renal excretion than previously thought" [^S13]. A separate reanalysis put numbers on the plateau: increasing the daily dose from 200 to 2,500 mg raises the mean steady-state plasma concentration only from 68.1 to 85.2 µmol/L, with saturable gut absorption and non-linear renal clearance acting additively, and concluded that "there is no pharmacokinetic justification for the use of megadoses of ascorbic acid" [^S12].

The women's study followed. Healthy young women hospitalised for 186 ± 28 days and dosed from 30 to 2,500 mg a day again showed a sigmoid curve; only doses above 100 mg were beyond its linear portion, plasma and circulating cells saturated at 400 mg a day with urinary elimination of higher doses, and plasma and urinary F2-isoprostanes, the standard marker of lipid peroxidation, were unchanged by vitamin C at every dose. The authors proposed an RDA of 90 mg for young women and noted that the newly released 75 mg figure had been "based on data for men" [^S21]. Reconciling the two reports: plasma saturates between 400 and 1,000 mg a day, cells by 100 to 400 mg, and the honest statement of the plateau is 68 to 85 µmol/L across the whole 200 to 2,500 mg range rather than a single number.

The kidney explains the ceiling from the other side. In 20 older and eight younger adults the tubular maximum for ascorbate reabsorption was 1.54 mg/min per 100 mL of glomerular filtrate in men and 1.39 in women, and the renal threshold 1.51 mg/dL in men and 1.26 in women, neither affected by age [^S8]; converted, the thresholds are about 86 and 72 µmol/L, which is where the Levine plateau sits. At pharmacological plasma levels clearance approaches the glomerular filtration rate: in ten prostate-cancer patients a 60 g infusion reached 20.3 mmol/L and cleared with a half-life of 1.87 hours at 100 mL/min [^S43]; in a 15-patient phase I series the half-life was 2.0 ± 0.6 hours and peak concentration plateaued at 49 mmol/L above 70 g/m² [^S40]. At nutritional intakes the kinetics are different in kind: a radiotracer study in healthy men found the half-life inversely related to dose and a body pool that saturates at about 20 mg/kg, reached in 95 % of the population at an intake of roughly 100 mg a day [^S1]. A review of the field states the transition plainly, "going from physiological to pharmacological doses, vitamin C pharmacokinetics change from zero to first order", and warns that this has "often been overlooked in the design of intervention studies" [^S48].

### 2.2 Oral versus intravenous

Seventeen healthy hospitalised volunteers received oral and intravenous doses from 0.015 to 1.25 g, with pharmacokinetic modelling extended to 100 g. At 1.25 g the oral route produced a mean peak of 134.8 ± 20.6 µmol/L and the intravenous route 885 ± 201.2 µmol/L. For the maximum tolerated oral regimen of 3 g every 4 hours the model predicted a peak of 220 µmol/L; for 50 g intravenously it predicted 13,400 µmol/L, with peak urine concentrations 140-fold higher than from maximal oral dosing. The authors conceded that patient data were not available to confirm the model at high doses, and concluded that "efficacy of vitamin C treatment cannot be judged from clinical trials that use only oral dosing" [^S23]. A three-patient case series from the same group restated the figures, 220 µmol/L from 18 g a day by mouth against about 14,000 µmol/L from 50 to 100 g by vein [^S25]. A phase I trial at 0.4 to 1.5 g/kg three times a week found minimal toxicity and no anticancer response [^S26]. The practical point for a healthy adult is arithmetic: a 1.25 g pill reaches about 135 µmol/L for a few hours, and whatever intravenous ascorbate does or does not do, no pill regimen reproduces those concentrations.

### 2.3 Transporters

Two sodium-dependent transporters were cloned from rat in 1999: SVCT1, confined mainly to epithelial systems such as intestine, kidney and liver, and SVCT2, serving metabolically active cells and specialised tissues in brain and eye; both mediate concentrative, stereospecific transport driven by the sodium gradient. The same paper judged that glucose transporters, which carry the oxidised form dehydroascorbic acid, "are unlikely to allow significant physiological amounts of vitamin C to be taken up in the presence of normal glucose concentrations" because plasma vitamin C is almost entirely in the reduced form [^S17]. Knocking out the SVCT2 gene in mice left fibroblasts with under 5 % of normal uptake, undetectable ascorbate in blood and tissues, and death within minutes of birth from respiratory failure and brain haemorrhage [^S22]. Knocking out SVCT1 raised fractional urinary excretion up to 18-fold, with the mice losing as much as 70 % of their body stores in urine daily while intestinal absorption was only "marginally affected" [^S31]. That last observation points at the second route: mammalian hexose transporters carry dehydroascorbate [^S9], GLUT1 and GLUT3 do so with Km values of 1.1 and 1.7 mmol/L while no isoform transports ascorbate itself [^S14], and in rodents gavaged dehydroascorbate doubled plasma ascorbate through intestinal GLUT2 and GLUT8, a route inhibited by glucose, fructose, phloretin and quercetin [^S38]. The highest tissue concentrations sit in brain and neuroendocrine tissue, and the brain is the hardest organ to deplete [^S29]; homeostatic tissue levels run from about 0.2 mmol/L in muscle and heart to 10 mmol/L in brain and adrenal [^S48]. Ascorbate is a cofactor for fifteen mammalian enzymes, and the same NIH authors insist that mechanistic claims must use physiological concentrations to be clinically meaningful [^S47]. Transporter genetics moves status a little: in 1,046 young non-smokers an SVCT1 variant shifted serum ascorbate from 24.4 to 29.7 µmol/L across genotypes [^S28].

### 2.4 Body pool, turnover and smokers

The body pool is about 20 mg/kg, roughly 1,500 mg for a 75 kg adult, and 100 mg a day fills it in 95 % of non-smoking men [^S1]. Smokers turn the vitamin over faster: the same radiotracer method in men smoking more than 20 cigarettes a day found that "a daily intake of at least 140 mg is required for smokers to reach steady state concentrations and total body pools comparable to nonsmokers" [^S2]. In 11,592 NHANES II adults, heavy smokers ate 79 mg and had serum 46.6 µmol/L, never-smokers ate 109 mg and had 65.3 µmol/L, the inverse association held after adjustment for intake, and unsupplemented smokers had odds of 3.0 for serum at or below 11.4 µmol/L [^S6]. A three-month trial in smokers and non-smokers matched for dietary antioxidant intake showed that only ascorbate was depleted by smoking itself and that a 272 mg cocktail repleted it [^S19]. In NHANES 2003-2004 the age-adjusted mean serum was 51.4 µmol/L, deficiency below 11.4 µmol/L stood at 7.1 %, and smokers' means were one-third lower than non-smokers' [^S30].

### 2.5 Requirements: three camps on the same data

The 2000 Institute of Medicine report is a book, not a PubMed record; its published summary carries no abstract [^S20], and only its 75 mg figure for women is verifiable in a PubMed abstract [^S21]. The NIH group translated its 1996 data into an Estimated Average Requirement of 100 mg and an RDA of 120 mg, or an Adequate Intake of 200 mg from five servings of fruit and vegetables, with an upper level below 1 g, using nine criteria from dietary availability to prevention of deficiency [^S16]. The Linus Pauling Institute argued from antioxidant and epidemiological evidence that 90 to 100 mg a day is needed for "optimum reduction of chronic disease risk", proposing 120 mg [^S15], later defended 200 mg as the optimum intake on the grounds that phase III drug-style trials are ill suited to nutrients [^S33], and catalogued the design flaws it sees in vitamin C research, including cell culture at high oxygen with redox-active metals and trials enrolling already-replete people [^S39]. The German-Austrian-Swiss derivation is the one whose arithmetic is in a PubMed abstract: metabolic losses of 50 mg a day, 80 % absorption, 25 % urinary loss and a target fasting plasma of 50 µmol/L give an average requirement of 91 mg and a reference intake of 110 mg for men and 95 mg for women, with 155 and 135 mg for smokers whose turnover runs 40 % higher [^S42]. A review of the global picture notes that recommendations vary more than five-fold on "more or less the same" data and that body weight has never been taken into account [^S50]. A meta-analysis of 36 intake-to-plasma data sets found that free-living adults at 60 mg a day sit at a median 42.4 µmol/L and that 100 mg a day reaches the 50 µmol/L consensus target [^S18]. The pharmacological reason most supplement trials are null is stated by a Copenhagen pharmacologist who also co-wrote the 200 mg paper: "supplementation with vitamin C is likely to be without effect for the majority of the Western population due to saturation through their normal diet", while a sub-population with a real shortfall "remains uninvestigated" [^S32].

### 2.6 Status definitions and biomarkers

The cut-offs used across the sources are: deficiency below 11.4 µmol/L (0.2 mg/dL) [^S30] [^S6]; marginal or hypovitaminosis at or below 23 µmol/L [^S4] [^S44]; adequate at or above 50 µmol/L [^S44] [^S42]; saturating above 65 µmol/L in one Otago analysis [^S44] and 68 to 85 µmol/L across the Levine range [^S12]. In a 14-week metabolic-unit study of 11 young men, plasma ascorbate tracked intake with less variability than red-cell or leukocyte ascorbate, 41 mg a day held plasma at 23 µmol/L, 138 mg maximised the body pool, and urine and saliva were "not useful indicators" [^S4]. Which leukocyte fraction best reflects status was never settled [^S5], and sample handling changes the number: ascorbate oxidises rapidly after sampling, so unstabilised samples under-read, and K3-EDTA tubes proved superior [^S34]. Starting point and body mass shift the whole curve: among 70 young men, those with hypovitaminosis at baseline reached only about 30 µmol/L on an extra 50 mg a day while the rest reached about 50, and 200 mg a day took the replete to above 65 µmol/L within a week and the depleted two weeks [^S44]. Status is set by intake and supplements first and then by sex, age, weight, pregnancy, genetics, smoking, infection, disease, pollution, season and deprivation [^S49]. In eight healthy men, 2 × 500 mg a day for 42 days raised skeletal-muscle vitamin C from 3.0 to 3.9 mg/100 g wet weight and plasma from 61 to 111.5 µmol/L, without changing muscle redox measures or citrate synthase activity [^S41]; note that the baseline plasma of these unsupplemented Australian men was already in the adequate-to-saturating band.

### 2.7 Forms

Synthetic and food-derived vitamin C behave identically at steady state. Thirty-six young men randomised to half a gold kiwifruit a day or a 50 mg chewable tablet for six weeks showed no difference in plasma, urine, mononuclear cells, neutrophils or skeletal muscle [^S35]; nine men given 200 mg as a tablet or as kiwifruit had identical plasma curves over 8 hours and excreted about 40 and 50 % of the dose in urine respectively [^S36]; the review of the field states that "all steady state comparative bioavailability studies in humans have shown no differences between synthetic and natural vitamin C" [^S37]. Calcium ascorbate with metabolites ("Ester-C") gave the same plasma curve as ascorbic acid after 1,000 mg in 15 men, with higher leukocyte ascorbate at 24 hours [^S27]; a 36-person crossover by the manufacturer found no plasma difference beyond a higher Cmax and a higher leukocyte percentage change at 8 and 24 hours [^S46]; an independent 93-person pair of crossovers found no difference at all at 250 mg and preliminary differences at 500 mg [^S52]; and the systematic review of 13 alternative-form studies, by authors advising Bayer, Haleon and dsm-firmenich, reported that "no study reported retention in tissues" [^S54]. A 1994 trial of three commercial forms carries no PubMed abstract and cannot be quoted [^S10]. Liposomal products raise single-dose plasma exposure: 1.77-fold by Cmax and AUC in a manufacturer study [^S51]; plasma Cmax +27 % and AUC +21 % at 500 mg in a 27-person crossover with product-maker involvement [^S53]; across 10 studies, 1.2 to 5.4-fold higher Cmax and 1.3 to 7.2-fold higher AUC, but "none of the studies assessed ascorbate elimination" [^S55]; and the 11-person 4 g trial that launched the category had a manufacturer co-author and no plasma numbers in its abstract [^S45]. Slow-release tablets flatten the plasma curve in smokers by an amount the authors called "small and unlikely to be of significant clinical importance" [^S24]; in four subjects, urinary recovery of a 1 g dose was 85 % after intravenous dosing, 30 % from solution or tablet and 14 % from a timed-release capsule [^S3]; and in a Roche study of 500 mg tablets, those disintegrating over 60 minutes gave the highest bioavailability while fast tablets equalled solution [^S7]. In a system capped by renal threshold and tissue saturation, a higher single-dose peak mostly means more urinary loss unless the person was depleted.

## 3. The Pauling Legacy, the Common Cold and Infection

### 3.1 What Pauling actually claimed

Pauling's 1970 argument was evolutionary: because few species need dietary ascorbate and raw plant diets supply less than the synthesis rate of animals that make their own, he put "the optimum daily intake" at "about 2.3 g or more" for an adult [^S56]. In 1971 he identified four independent double-blind studies of more than 100 mg a day and reported that his pooled analysis rejected the null hypothesis "at the 99.86 % level of confidence for the incidence of colds and the 99.9978 % level of confidence for the integrated morbidity"; the abstract names no trial, no sample size and no effect size [^S57]. He published the same argument in a nutrition journal that year, without an available abstract [^S58]. In 1974, with the RDA newly set at 45 mg, he argued that this "suffices to prevent scurvy in most people" but is "much less than the optimum intake", and proposed an intake band of 250 to 4,000 mg a day, a 16-fold interval that is the honest measure of how little dose-response data existed [^S61]. He ran no cold trial. No 1976 common-cold paper by Pauling is indexed; the 1975 JAMA paper with the title that circulates is by his critics [^S63].

### 3.2 The 1972 to 1975 trials and reviews

The first Toronto trial of 1 g a day found fewer colds and sick days in the vitamin group but "the differences were smaller than have been claimed and were statistically not significant", with about 30 % fewer days of disability (P < 0.001) attributed to milder constitutional symptoms [^S59]. The second Toronto trial randomised 2,349 volunteers to eight regimens: prophylactic 0.25, 1 or 2 g a day, therapeutic 4 or 8 g on day one, a combination, and two placebo groups. "None of the groups receiving vitamin C showed a difference in sickness experience that was statistically significant", there was "no evidence of a dose-related effect" across the eightfold prophylactic range, and the authors suggested the optimum daily dose "is less than 250 mg, except possibly at the time of acute illness" [^S60]. The NIH trial gave 311 employees 3 g a day with an extra 3 g at onset; 190 completed, dropouts were 44 % on placebo and 34 % on ascorbate, and the authors concluded that the minor effects on duration and severity "might be explained equally well by a break in the double blind", because lactose and ascorbic acid taste different [^S64]. Chalmers pooled eight trials to 0.09 ± 0.06 fewer colds a year and 0.11 ± 0.24 days shorter duration, "minor and insignificant", and judged the benefits "not worth the potential risk" [^S62]; Dykes and Meier found "no clear, reproducible pattern of efficacy" [^S63].

Hemilä's later audits are the bridge from the 1975 consensus to the Cochrane position. He showed that Chalmers had included a trial at 25 to 50 mg a day and used values inconsistent with the originals, and recalculated the 1 to 6 g trials to 0.93 ± 0.22 days shorter, a 21 % reduction [^S70]; he argued that Karlowski's placebo explanation "is inconsistent with their results" and that the data show a 17 % shorter cold at 6 g a day with linear dose dependency [^S71]; in 1994 he reported that in each of 21 trials at 1 g or more, duration and severity fell by an average of 23 % [^S68]; and in 1999 he noted that adults at 1 g a day showed a median 6 % shorter cold while two children's studies at 2 g showed 26 % [^S74]. His 2022 review frames the whole history as bias against vitamin C in mainstream medicine and should be read as one side's statement [^S96].

### 3.3 The Cochrane synthesis

The 2004 Cochrane review pooled 29 comparisons in 11,077 participants to an incidence RR of 0.96 (0.92 to 1.00), with a subgroup of six trials in 642 "marathon runners, skiers, and soldiers on sub-arctic exercises" at RR 0.50 (0.38 to 0.66); duration under regular prophylaxis fell 8 % in adults and 13.5 % in children, therapeutic trials started after onset showed no significant difference, and volunteers infected by nasal instillation showed little benefit while naturally infected ones reported milder symptoms [^S78]. The 2013 update, with 11,306 participants, gives the numbers that matter: community incidence RR 0.97 (0.94 to 1.00) in 10,708 people; RR 0.48 (0.35 to 0.64) in 598 people under heavy acute physical stress; duration reduced 8 % (3 to 12) in adults and 14 % (7 to 21) in children, 18 % in children at 1 to 2 g; severity "also reduced"; and no consistent effect in seven therapeutic comparisons covering 3,249 episodes. Its conclusion is that "routine vitamin C supplementation is not justified", that it "may be useful for people exposed to brief periods of severe physical exercise", and that people might test therapeutic use individually [^S83]. The first author is the same Hemilä; Cochrane's two-extractor process and the unfavourable incidence result argue against author bias driving the headline. An independent Harvard meta-analysis of micronutrients for adult respiratory infection gives RR 0.96 (0.93 to 0.99) and a 9 % (2 to 16) shorter illness [^S91], and a 2023 meta-analysis of ten trials at 1 g or more found severity reduced 15 % (9 to 21), with the effect concentrated on severe-symptom days and absent for mild ones [^S98].

### 3.4 Who the exercise subgroup is, and who it is not

The archetype is the Comrades ultramarathon trial: 600 mg a day, and 68 % of placebo runners versus 33 % of supplemented runners reported upper-respiratory symptoms in the fortnight after the race, with non-running controls on vitamin C also having shorter, milder symptoms [^S67]. Pooling three such trials at 0.6 to 1 g a day in skiers, Canadian soldiers in the north and 90 km runners gives RR 0.50 (0.35 to 0.69) [^S72]; widening to seven military, three student and two marathon trials, five small ones found 45 to 91 % fewer colds under heavy exertion [^S79]. The pattern is not universal among soldiers: 674 marine recruits on 2 g a day for eight weeks had higher blood ascorbate and "no difference between the two groups in the incidence or duration of colds" [^S65]. Thirty-nine adolescent swimmers on 1 g a day showed an incidence rate ratio of 1.01 and a duration reduction only in males [^S81]. Among 28 men selected for plasma below 45 µmol/L, 1 g a day gave cold RR 0.55 (0.33 to 0.94), a marginal-status result with a supplement-company co-author [^S85]; a five-year Japanese trial of 500 versus 50 mg in people with atrophic gastritis found fewer repeated colds and no effect on severity or duration [^S80]; and a 168-person product trial from a "Herbal Health Centre" reported fewer colds without stating its dose [^S77].

### 3.5 Therapeutic dosing and the 6 to 8 g argument

The best modern therapeutic trial recruited 400 Australian university volunteers to 0.03, 1, 3 or 3 g with additives taken at onset for three days: "no significant differences were observed in any measure of cold duration or severity", and the placebo group had the shortest symptoms [^S76]. Against this, Hemilä's 2017 review states that "two controlled trials found a statistically significant dose-response, for the duration of common cold symptoms, with up to 6 to 8 g/day", so that "the negative findings of some therapeutic common cold studies might be explained by the low doses of 3 to 4 g/day" [^S89]. The two trials are Karlowski, whose authors attributed their 17 % effect to a broken blind [^S64], and the second Toronto trial, whose authors found no dose-response and put the optimum below 250 mg [^S60]. No blinded 6 to 8 g therapeutic trial has been run since. Two meta-analyses that supported extra-dose or paediatric benefit, Ran 2018 on extra doses on top of daily use and Vorilhon 2019 in 3,135 children, are flagged Retracted Publication in PubMed (PMID 30069463, PMID 30465062) and their numbers must not be used; the published comment that the paediatric analysis "may be misleading" is a letter, cited only as the record [^S90]. The pharmacokinetics cap what oral grams can do: above 500 mg the surplus is excreted, and oxalate and urate excretion are already raised at 1,000 mg [^S11].

### 3.6 Pneumonia and COVID-19

The Cochrane pneumonia review found three prophylactic trials with 37 cases of community-acquired pneumonia in 2,335 people, each reporting a reduction of 80 % or more, only one adequately blinded, in 1940s to 1970s recruits and schoolboys on institutional diets, and two treatment trials in 197 patients; it called the evidence "too weak to advocate prophylactic use" while allowing therapeutic use in pneumonia patients with low plasma levels because "its cost and risks are low" [^S84]. The one well-run treatment trial gave 57 elderly inpatients with acute respiratory infection 200 mg a day: plasma and white-cell ascorbate rose even during infection, and patients "fared significantly better" on a clinical score, particularly those admitted most severely ill with very low levels, a repletion result at a tenth of a 2 g tablet [^S69]; a short piece on intake and pneumonia susceptibility has no abstract [^S73]. For COVID-19, the COVID A to Z trial randomised 214 outpatients to 10 days of zinc 50 mg, ascorbic acid 8,000 mg, both, or usual care, open-label; it was stopped for low conditional power with days to 50 % symptom reduction of 5.5 on vitamin C against 6.7 on usual care, P = .45 [^S95]. A reanalysis by the pro-vitamin-C authors claims a 70 % higher recovery rate; it has no abstract and is a disputed re-reading of a null trial [^S92]. A 100-patient Iranian trial of 500 mg orally in critically ill patients reported survival duration of 8 versus 4 days with unequal, apparently unblinded arms [^S93]. Meta-analyses conflict: six RCTs give mortality RR 0.73 (0.42 to 1.27) with no route or dose effect [^S94]; 19 mixed trials give in-hospital mortality OR 0.59 with a longer ICU stay [^S97]; 44 RCTs across indications give all-cause RR 0.87 "not validated by sequential trial analysis" and a COVID monotherapy RR of 0.84 needing more trials [^S99]. The ambulatory healthy adult has one relevant trial, and it was null at 8 g a day.

### 3.7 Mechanism and saturation

The immune-mechanism literature is real and bounded. Ascorbate accumulates in neutrophils and supports chemotaxis, phagocytosis, oxidant generation, microbial killing and the clearance of spent cells, and infection lowers plasma levels; the review that states this also states that prevention needs intakes giving "adequate, if not saturating plasma levels (i.e., 100 to 200 mg/day)" while treatment of established infection "requires significantly higher (gram) doses", and its conflict statement records that one author is a Bayer Consumer Care employee and the other a Bayer key opinion leader [^S88]. Fourteen young men with plasma below 50 µmol/L who ate two gold kiwifruit a day reached above 70 µmol/L within a week, raised neutrophil ascorbate and increased neutrophil chemotaxis by 20 % [^S86]. A 1980 ex vivo study found neutrophil motility enhanced only at 2 to 3 g a day and lymphocyte mitogen responses up from 1 g, with immunoglobulins and complement unchanged, a small assay study without clinical endpoints that conflicts with the cellular saturation data [^S66]. The dose at which white cells fill, 100 mg in men [^S11] and 400 mg in women [^S21], is one-twentieth to one-sixth of Pauling's lower optimum.

### 3.8 Why the belief persists

Two in three care-seeking adults in a 1997 Minnesota survey believed vitamin C reduces cold symptoms, a quarter-century after the 1975 reviews [^S75]. NHANES shows 52 % of US adults using some supplement in 2011-2012 with no vitamin C-specific figure [^S87], and most supplement use is self-directed, with only 23 % of products recommended by a clinician [^S82]. The belief lives in self-directed health maintenance where nothing falsifies it.
## 4. Exercise Adaptation, Antioxidant Blunting, Recovery and Hypertrophy

### 4.1 The hypothesis and its chemistry

The claim that a daily antioxidant pill aids recovery met its counter-claim after 2008: exercise-generated reactive oxygen species are a signal, and swallowing an antioxidant blunts the adaptation you trained for. The blunting camp states its own position carefully. Its review concedes that the attenuation of antioxidant capacity, mitochondrial biogenesis and insulin sensitivity "is not a universal finding, potentially indicating that there is redundancy in the mechanisms controlling skeletal muscle adaptation", that "there is no convincing evidence to suggest that antioxidant supplementation enhances exercise-training adaptions", and that the effect is more consistent for overload and high-intensity training than for steady endurance [^S134]. A chemical analysis by authors who themselves ran blunting trials concludes that "direct evidence for interference of ascorbate and α-tocopherol with exercise-induced ROS/RNS production is lacking", that neither vitamin reacts appreciably with hydrogen peroxide, the main signalling species, and that redox-independent mechanisms deserve consideration [^S130]. A Deakin review ties the impairment language to "high dose vitamin C (1 g/day) and E (≥260 IU/day)" together and calls the human evidence limited [^S133]; a later narrative review from the same group recommends against chronic 1,000 mg C plus E during heavy training and notes that vascular effects of vitamin C do not translate to performance [^S145]. An intravenous N-acetylcysteine infusion during cycling blocked JNK phosphorylation and MnSOD transcription but left PGC-1α, IL-6 and HSP70 induction intact, so even a strong thiol antioxidant did not switch off the master regulator [^S123]. The best human evidence that oxidative stress tracks adaptation without any pill is a six-week training study in which the participants with the lowest exercise-induced oxidative stress gained the least in VO2max, time trial and Wingate performance [^S141]. A Greek review of the whole field found the animal and human data "conflicting" and recommended against "a permanent intake of non-physiological dosages of vitamin C and/or E" on the basis of absent benefit as much as demonstrated harm [^S122].

### 4.2 The combination trials that the conclusions rest on

The most-cited human paper is a four-week controlled trial of 1,000 mg of vitamin C plus 400 IU of vitamin E in 19 untrained and 20 pretrained young men: exercise raised insulin sensitivity by clamp and plasma adiponectin "only in the absence of antioxidants", and muscle PGC-1α, PGC-1β, SOD1, SOD2 and GPx induction was blocked, with P values but no effect sizes in the abstract and no randomisation tag in PubMed [^S112]. Its direct test did not reproduce it: 21 young men on 500 mg of C plus 400 IU of E through 12 weeks of supervised endurance training, five sessions a week, gained insulin-stimulated glucose uptake of 17.2 % on vitamins and 18.9 % on placebo, with VO2max, GLUT4, hexokinase II and Akt rising equally and plasma vitamin levels confirming compliance [^S119]. The same cohort's performance paper found VO2max, peak power, lactate threshold, glycogen, citrate synthase and β-HAD all improved "in both groups" with "no differences between the two groups" [^S114]; its redox paper was not entirely null, with plasma protein carbonyls higher in the vitamin group and the training-induced dampening of the IL-6 response present in placebo only, interaction P = 0.13 [^S124].

The Norwegian trials are the cleanest. Fifty-four young men and women took 1,000 mg of C plus 235 mg of E through 11 weeks of mixed interval and continuous running: VO2max rose 8 ± 5 % in both groups and the shuttle run 10 ± 11 % against 14 ± 17 %, while COX4 protein changed +59 ± 97 % on placebo against −13 ± 54 % on vitamins and cytosolic PGC-1α +19 ± 51 % against −13 ± 29 %, between-group P ≤ 0.03 for both markers; the authors "advocate caution" although "this did not translate to the performance tests" [^S128]. The standard deviations show how noisy the markers are, and 11 weeks produced no detectable performance penalty. The same cohort's heat-shock and antioxidant-enzyme paper found no influence of supplementation on long-term adaptations and a larger, not smaller, acute stress response in the vitamin group [^S127]. A separate trial of 11 young men on 2 × 500 mg of C plus 400 IU of E through four weeks of 10 × 4-minute cycling intervals found that supplementation did not prevent the rise in VO2peak, citrate synthase or COX IV but attenuated the increase in SOD activity, SOD2 and TFAM [^S131]. Eleven weeks of C plus E around four weeks of eccentric training in 28 people produced "the complete lack of any effect" on muscle damage, performance or redox status [^S118]. The mechanistic root of the myokine claim is a 28-day trial of 500 mg of C plus 400 IU of E in 14 men: the leg's release of IL-6 during three hours of knee-extensor exercise was "completely blunted", arterial IL-6 reached 7.9 against 19.7 pg/mL, and cortisol, CRP and IL-1ra rose only in controls, while muscle IL-6 transcription rose in both groups [^S107].

In resistance training, 32 recreationally trained adults on 1,000 mg of C plus 235 mg of E through ten weeks of heavy lifting showed no effect on muscle mass or on acute protein synthesis, a hampered biceps-curl strength gain, and blunted post-exercise phosphorylation of p38 MAPK, ERK1/2 and p70S6K [^S129]; the same cohort showed no supplement effect on heat-shock proteins or antioxidant enzymes over the ten weeks [^S137]. The one outcome-level signal comes from 34 men aged 60 to 81 who took 500 mg of C and 117.5 mg of E before and after each of three weekly sessions for 12 weeks: total lean mass rose 1.4 % (95 % CI 0 to 5.4) on vitamins against 3.9 % (3.0 to 5.2) on placebo, P = 0.04, rectus femoris thickness 10.9 % against 16.2 %, while one-repetition maximum improved 15 to 21 % in both groups [^S132]; the bone sub-study of the same men found total-hip density up 1.0 % and sclerostin down 9.9 % on placebo only, with several intervals crossing zero [^S139]. In 42 young women training twice a week for ten weeks on 1 g of C plus 400 IU of E, peak torque rose from 146 to 170 Nm on vitamins and from 159 to 183 on placebo with no direct difference; the "may attenuate" conclusion rests on only the placebo group exceeding an untrained control [^S140]. The quantitative synthesis of nine aerobic and nine resistance trials gives VO2max SMD −0.14 (−0.43 to 0.15), endurance −0.01 (−0.38 to 0.36), lean mass −0.07 (−0.36 to 0.23) and strength P = 0.35, and concludes that vitamin C and/or E "does not inhibit exercise-induced changes in physiological function" while calling for larger trials [^S143].

### 4.3 The vitamin-C-only trials

Any conclusion about a vitamin C pill has to rest on a small set. The paper that launched the idea trained 14 men for eight weeks with five of them on 1 g a day and reported that "the administration of vitamin C significantly (P = 0.014) hampered endurance capacity", without saying whether that refers to the men or to the 24 rats whose muscle supplied the PGC-1, NRF-1, TFAM, cytochrome c, SOD and GPx findings [^S111]. Fifteen men on 1 g a day through four weeks of interval running improved VO2max, 10 km time, running economy and intermittent-recovery tests equally in both groups, between-group P from .24 to .76 [^S116]. Twenty-three trained women took 1 g, a blackcurrant drink supplying 15 mg of vitamin C with 300 mg of anthocyanins, or placebo in three-week crossover blocks: vitamin C produced a "likely harmful" 1.3 % (±1.3 %) effect on training speed with "unclear" performance-test effects and raised resting catalase and protein carbonyls, while the food-level dose showed no harm and a possible benefit in faster runners [^S126]. The same author's review claims that doses above 1 g impaired performance in four of four studies and proposes about 0.2 g a day from five servings of fruit and vegetables as the level that does not impair adaptation, a judgement rather than a tested dose [^S120]. At 500 mg a day for eight weeks, serum vitamin C rose about 50 %, the exercise-induced rise in muscle HSP70 was abolished, and baseline HSP70, SOD and catalase were higher, which the authors read as protective systems already induced [^S106]. No human training trial at 200 mg of supplemental vitamin C alone exists.

The rat literature cuts both ways and may not transfer. Vitamin C at 500 mg/kg for seven days did not block the exercise-induced induction of PGC-1α, NRF-1, TFAM, GPx1, MnSOD or GLUT4 in rats and raised basal NRF-1 and NRF-2 transcription [^S113]; 750 mg/kg of C plus 150 mg/kg of E, about 50 times the human per-kilogram dose, left the roughly twofold training rises in GLUT4, mitochondrial enzymes and SOD intact [^S115]; and the trial often lumped into the vitamin C canon used vitamin E plus lipoic acid with no vitamin C at all [^S117]. A direct rat-versus-human comparison at 16 mg/kg found that vitamin C lowered oxidative stress only in humans and induced an anti-inflammatory state only in rats, with 10 of 19 species-by-vitamin interactions, because rats synthesise their own ascorbate [^S136]. A letter titled "Antioxidant supplements in exercise: worse than useless?" is the blunting camp's opinion piece and is cited here only as the record [^S121].

### 4.4 Low plasma ascorbate: the population a pill moves

The Greek group screened hundreds of men. Among 100, the lowest ten by blood vitamin C had lower VO2max and higher isoprostanes and carbonyls than the highest ten, and 30 days of supplementation "marginally increased" their VO2max [^S135]. In a falsification design, 12 men lowest in plasma vitamin C, 12 lowest in erythrocyte glutathione and 12 controls took 1 g of vitamin C or 1.2 g of N-acetylcysteine for 30 days; VO2max and peak torque improved only in the group whose specific deficiency was targeted, and the authors concluded that "the presence of oxidative stress per se does not rationalize the use of antioxidants" [^S144]. In 200 men sorted by plasma vitamin C, the low group sat 25 µmol/L below controls with VO2max 8.2 mL/kg/min and peak torque 41.5 Nm lower; 30 days of 1 g raised plasma only 11.6 µmol/L yet raised VO2max 5.4 mL/kg/min (2.7 to 8.2) and torque 18.7 Nm [^S146]. These are the largest performance effects in the whole cluster and they occur in people selected for low status; the control group was never supplemented. An aggregated n-of-1 series in eight inadequate men found plasma up 20.6 µmol/L and isoprostanes down, but cycle-to-cycle variation within a person exceeded the variation between people, so the "responder" idea did not survive a design built to test it [^S147]. The group's position paper argues for antioxidant supplementation tailored to measured deficiency [^S142].

### 4.5 Soreness and recovery

The Cochrane review of antioxidants for muscle soreness pooled 50 RCTs and 1,089 participants, 47 of the trials at high risk of bias: soreness SMDs ran from −0.30 at 6 hours to −0.05 at 96 hours, which on a 10 cm scale is −0.52 to −0.03 cm, "all well below the minimal important difference of 1.4 cm", and one trial had all six supplemented participants develop diarrhoea [^S138]. The vitamin-C-only trials split. The oldest, a 19-person chiropractic-college crossover, reported 25 to 44 % soreness abatement with no untreated control [^S100]. Four hundred milligrams a day for 14 days before a 90-minute shuttle run left creatine kinase and myoglobin unchanged and gave "modest beneficial effects" on soreness, function and malondialdehyde [^S104]. Three grams a day around eccentric elbow work reduced soreness for the first 24 hours only, with range of motion and force loss unaffected [^S108]; 3 g a day in 24 people produced no between-group difference in strength loss, tenderness, range of motion or pain [^S110]; and 1 g two hours before and for 14 days after downhill running left soreness unchanged while "a delayed recovery was noted" in muscle function, a secondary observation in a small study [^S109]. Vitamin C protected neither force nor range of motion in any of them.

### 4.6 Heavy acute exertion

This is the one setting with a plausible benefit, and it belongs to infection rather than performance. The Comrades trial gave 600 mg a day and found post-race respiratory symptoms in 33 % against 68 % [^S67]; the pooled heavy-stress trials give RR 0.50 [^S72] and the Cochrane subgroup RR 0.48 [^S83]; three tiny trials in people with exercise-induced bronchoconstriction gave a 48 % smaller post-exercise FEV1 fall [^S125]. The cortisol story is weaker than it sounds. Among 29 ultramarathon finishers, pre-race serum vitamin C was 128 µmol/L on 500 mg, 153 on 1,500 mg and 83 on placebo, and post-race cortisol was lower only at 1,500 mg, an attenuation the authors called "transient" [^S102]; the cytokine arm found no group-by-time interaction for any cytokine until placebo and 500 mg were pooled against 1,500 mg [^S101]; a 16-runner trial of 1,000 mg found cortisol 30 % lower and CRP higher [^S103]; and the largest, best-controlled attempt, 28 carbohydrate-fed runners randomised double-blind to 1,500 mg, found no effect on lipid hydroperoxides, isoprostanes, immune cell counts, any cytokine or lymphocyte function [^S105]. The plasma arithmetic is the same as in the pharmacokinetics chapter: well-fed runners already sit at 83 to 86 µmol/L, and a gram lifts them to 118 to 153.

## 5. Cardiovascular and Endothelial Health, Blood Pressure, Lipids and Hard Outcomes

### 5.1 The acute vasodilation studies

The 1990s produced dramatic acute results in sick arteries. In 46 patients with coronary disease, a single oral 2 g dose raised plasma from 46 to 114 µmol/L within two hours and, in those with abnormal baseline flow-mediated dilation, lifted dilation from 2.0 to 9.7 % against 1.1 to 1.7 % on placebo, with no effect on nitroglycerin-mediated dilation [^S150]. Intra-arterial vitamin C restored the impaired acetylcholine response in hypertensives but not controls, and the effect was abolished by nitric oxide synthase and cyclooxygenase inhibition, pointing at superoxide inactivation of nitric oxide [^S152]. In ten smokers an intra-arterial infusion "markedly improved" the acetylcholine response while doing nothing in controls [^S149]; a follow-up showed that tetrahydrobiopterin restored smokers' responses and that prior vitamin C abolished the additional biopterin effect, placing both on the same uncoupled-synthase pathway [^S154]. In 15 heart-failure patients an infusion restored radial flow-dependent dilation from 8.2 to 13.2 % and four weeks of oral therapy to 11.9 %, with the nitric-oxide-mediated portion rising from 4.2 to 9.1 and 7.3 % against 8.9 % in healthy people [^S151]. The mechanism has cell-culture and mouse support: intracellular ascorbate raised nitric oxide bioactivity 70 % and eNOS Vmax about 50 % in porcine endothelial cells through tetrahydrobiopterin [^S155], and 26 to 28 weeks of dietary vitamin C raised aortic biopterin and restored eNOS activity in apoE-deficient mice while vitamin E did neither [^S167].

The chronic oral trials test whether any of this generalises. Twenty young smokers given a single 2 g dose moved plasma from 42 to 120 µmol/L and flow-mediated dilation from 2.8 to 6.3 %; after eight weeks of 1 g a day, plasma stayed at 92 µmol/L and dilation was 3.9 %, P = 0.26, "no sustained" effect [^S157]. Thirty-nine hypertensives given 2 g acutely and 500 mg a day for a month reached 149 and then 99 µmol/L; blood pressure fell, and brachial flow-mediated dilation changed neither acutely nor chronically [^S161]. The meta-analyses move with the inclusion rule. Pooling 44 RCTs of any route and duration gives SMD 0.50 (0.34 to 0.66), 0.84 in atherosclerotic patients, 0.52 in diabetics and 0.48 in heart failure, with a dose meta-regression of 0.00011 per mg rather than a 500 mg threshold [^S183]; restricting to supplementation beyond two weeks gives SMD 0.25 (0.02 to 0.49) for vitamin C alone and a non-significant 0.12 for C plus E [^S185]; and the same Newcastle group's umbrella review of ten syntheses in 6,409 participants reports "contrasting results" for endothelial function, no overall effect on arterial stiffness, total cholesterol, HDL or insulin, and "the weakness of the current evidence base" [^S190]. The arterial-stiffness meta-analysis sometimes cited for vitamin C pooled vitamins C, E, A and β-carotene together to SMD −0.17, larger in people with low baseline vitamin C or E [^S184].

### 5.2 Blood pressure and lipids

Twenty-nine RCTs of oral vitamin C, median dose 500 mg and median duration eight weeks in trials of 10 to 120 people, pool to systolic −3.84 mmHg (−5.29 to −2.38) and diastolic −1.48 (−2.86 to −0.10); in hypertensives systolic −4.85 and diastolic −1.67, the latter not significant [^S181]. Eight trials in 614 essential hypertensives give −4.09/−2.30, with −5.01 systolic at 500 mg or more [^S191]. Forty people aged 60 to 80 on 500 mg a day for three months showed no clinic change and a 2.0 mmHg fall in daytime ambulatory systolic pressure [^S153]. The only long trial is a five-year Japanese comparison of 500 against 50 mg a day in 439 adults with atrophic gastritis: systolic pressure rose 5.88 mmHg on 500 mg and 5.73 on 50 mg, "no reduction in blood pressure with long-term moderate doses" [^S164]. Observationally, hypertensives carry serum vitamin C 15.13 µmol/L lower than normotensives [^S192]. For lipids, a 13-trial meta-analysis in hypercholesterolaemic patients at 500 mg or more, published in a chiropractic journal, found LDL −7.9 mg/dL and triglycerides −20.1 with HDL unchanged [^S174]; the larger synthesis of trials beyond two weeks found that "vitamin C supplementation did not change blood lipids concentration significantly", with subgroup effects in people under 52, healthy participants and diabetics that grew with higher baseline lipids and lower baseline vitamin C [^S187]. In the HATS trial, 160 coronary patients with low HDL were randomised to simvastatin-niacin, an antioxidant cocktail of vitamins E and C, β-carotene and selenium, both or placebo; the HDL2 rise from the drugs "was attenuated by concurrent therapy with antioxidants", stenosis regressed 0.4 % on drugs alone and progressed 0.7 % with antioxidants added, and events ran 3 % against 14 % [^S159]; the lipoprotein sub-study found HDL2 up 42 % on drugs alone and 0 % with antioxidants [^S160]. The design never tested vitamin C alone.

### 5.3 Hard outcomes

Two factorial trials tested exactly the decision in question. The Physicians' Health Study II randomised 14,641 US male physicians aged 50 and over to 500 mg of vitamin C a day or placebo for a mean of eight years: major cardiovascular events 10.8 against 10.9 per 1,000 person-years, HR 0.99 (0.89 to 1.11); total myocardial infarction 1.04; total stroke 0.89 (0.74 to 1.07); cardiovascular death 1.02; total mortality 1.07 (0.97 to 1.18) [^S176]. The same trial found total cancer HR 1.01 (0.92 to 1.10) and prostate cancer 1.02 [^S178]. The Women's Antioxidant Cardiovascular Study randomised 8,171 women with prior cardiovascular disease or three or more risk factors to 500 mg a day for a mean of 9.4 years: RR 1.02 (0.92 to 1.13) for the composite, no effect on any individual outcome, and fewer strokes only in the group on both C and E [^S172]. Together they supply 22,812 randomised participants over eight to nine years and upper confidence bounds of 1.11 and 1.13, which exclude the 20 to 40 % protective effects the plasma cohorts suggest. The Cochrane review of vitamin C alone for primary prevention found eight trials and 15,445 participants with only PHS II contributing events, HR 0.99 for major events and 1.07 for mortality, low and very low quality, "no evidence to suggest that vitamin C supplementation reduces the risk of CVD" [^S188].

The cocktail trials agree. The Heart Protection Study gave 20,536 high-risk adults 600 mg of vitamin E, 250 mg of vitamin C and 20 mg of β-carotene for five years, raising plasma vitamin C by a third: major vascular events 22.5 % in both arms, rate ratio 1.00 (0.94 to 1.06), mortality 14.1 against 13.5 % [^S163]. SU.VI.MAX gave 13,017 French adults 120 mg of C with vitamin E, β-carotene, selenium and zinc for 7.5 years: ischaemic cardiovascular disease 2.1 % in both arms, no overall cancer or mortality effect, and a male-only cancer RR of 0.69 attributed to men's lower baseline status [^S168], a benefit that had disappeared five years after the capsules stopped [^S179]. HOPE and HOPE-TOO are often cited as vitamin C trials and were not: vitamin E alone, 400 IU a day, gave a primary-outcome RR of 1.05 [^S158] and, over seven years, more heart failure, RR 1.13 (1.01 to 1.26) [^S171]. The one positive chronic surrogate result is the six-year ASAP trial of 136 IU of vitamin E plus 250 mg of slow-release vitamin C twice daily in 520 hypercholesterolaemic Finns: carotid intima-media progression was 26 % slower overall and 33 % in men, larger with low baseline vitamin C, and inseparable from the vitamin E [^S166]. The WAVE trial of 400 IU of E plus 500 mg of C twice daily in 423 postmenopausal women with coronary stenosis found no angiographic benefit and 16 deaths against 6, HR 2.8 (1.1 to 7.2) on 22 events [^S165]. Pooling 50 RCTs and 294,478 participants, vitamin and antioxidant supplements gave major cardiovascular events RR 1.00 (0.98 to 1.02) with no benefit in any subgroup and benefits "seen only in randomised controlled trials in which the supplements were supplied by the pharmaceutical industry" [^S182]. The Cochrane mortality review found antioxidants as a class raised mortality in low-bias trials, RR 1.04 (1.01 to 1.07), driven by β-carotene and vitamin E, while vitamin C across 29 trials and 66,000 participants gave RR 1.02 (0.98 to 1.07) [^S180].

### 5.4 The observational signal and the confounding argument

EPIC-Norfolk measured plasma ascorbate in 19,496 adults aged 45 to 79 and followed them about four years: top-quintile mortality was about half that of the bottom quintile, the relation was continuous, and a 20 µmol/L rise, "equivalent to about 50 g per day increase in fruit and vegetable intake", tracked about 20 % lower all-cause mortality independent of age, blood pressure, cholesterol, smoking, diabetes and supplement use [^S162]. The same cohort's stroke analysis of 20,649 people over 9.5 years gave RR 0.58 (0.43 to 0.78) for the top against bottom quartile, and its authors wrote that plasma vitamin C "may serve as a biological marker of lifestyle" [^S175]. NHANES I follow-up found standardised mortality ratios of 0.65 for all causes and 0.58 for cardiovascular death in men with the highest intake index, which mixed food and supplements [^S148]; the serum-based NHANES II analysis found all-cause RR 1.57 in men in the lowest quartile, no cardiovascular association in any quartile, a threshold rather than a gradient, and nothing in women [^S156]. A pooled analysis of nine cohorts and 293,172 people found dietary vitamin C unprotective, top quintile RR 1.23, and supplemental vitamin C above 700 mg a day protective, RR 0.75 (0.60 to 0.93) [^S169]; a 15-cohort meta-analysis found the opposite pattern, dietary protective and supplemental not, with a flat dose-response of RR 1.01 per 30 mg a day [^S177]. Among 1,923 diabetic women followed 15 years, the top quintile of total vitamin C intake carried cardiovascular mortality RR 1.84 and stroke 2.57, only supplemental intake was associated, and non-diabetics showed nothing [^S170]. The dose-response meta-analysis of 69 prospective studies gives per 100 mg a day of dietary vitamin C coronary RR 0.88 and mortality 0.89, and per 50 µmol/L of blood vitamin C coronary 0.74 and mortality 0.72, which its authors read as support for fruit and vegetables "but not antioxidant supplement use" [^S189]. The gap between the intake-based and plasma-based estimates is what you expect if plasma is partly a marker of health rather than only of intake.

Mendelian randomisation tries to break the tie. In 97,203 Danes, an SVCT1 variant raising plasma vitamin C 11 % gave ischaemic heart disease OR 0.90 (0.75 to 1.08) for a genetically determined 25 % higher level, a point estimate matching the fruit-and-vegetable effect with an interval including harm, so the authors "cannot exclude" that part of the produce benefit runs through vitamin C [^S186]. Nine genome-wide SNPs showed no association with coronary disease, atrial fibrillation, heart failure or ischaemic stroke [^S193]; ten SNPs across 15 cardiovascular diseases showed nothing after multiple-testing correction, with uncorrected suggestive signals in both directions [^S194]. Heart failure has no hard-outcome trial; in 39 patients, 4 g a day for four weeks improved an endothelial surrogate and worsened skeletal-muscle metabolism in the same people [^S173].

## 6. Renal Toxicology, Kidney Stones and Other Safety Signals

### 6.1 Ascorbate to oxalate

About 60 mg of ascorbate breaks down in the body each day, non-enzymatically, ending in oxalate; complete conversion could make up to 30 mg of oxalate, more than the measured endogenous production of 10 to 25 mg a day, so non-oxalate pathways exist and the fraction of urinary oxalate that comes from vitamin C at ordinary intakes is unmeasured [^S242]. Ascorbate is nonetheless "the main dietary precursor for endogenous production of oxalate" [^S248], and with controlled diets the dietary contribution to urinary oxalate runs 24 to 42 %, rising to 53 % on low calcium, so endogenous synthesis supplies half to three-quarters [^S220]. The controlled studies agree on the increment. In 15 patients with nephrostomy tubes, urine collected into preservative with ascorbate removed before assay showed that at 500 mg and above, 1.2 to 1.8 % of the administered millimoles became urinary oxalate, "an increase in urinary oxalate excretion of 6 to 13 mg per day per 1,000 mg ascorbic acid supplement" [^S205]. In a placebo-controlled crossover on a metabolic diet, 1 g twice daily raised urinary oxalate from 28.5 to 34.7 mg a day in 12 normal subjects and from 30.5 to 41.0 in 12 stone formers, with urinary pH unchanged and the authors recommending that stone formers stay below 2 g [^S223]. In 47 stone formers and 20 healthy subjects, 1 g for three days raised oxalate from 25 to 39 mg in the healthy and from 31 to 50 in stone formers, and spot urines collected with and without EDTA showed no difference, excluding in-vitro conversion [^S222]. Isotope studies in 29 stone formers and 19 controls showed stone formers absorb 9.9 % of an oxalate load against 8.0 % and that 2 g a day of ascorbate raised both total and endogenous oxalate [^S224]; in the same 48 people, 1,000 mg twice daily raised the Tiselius risk index from 0.76 to 1.10, absorption 31 % and endogenous synthesis 39 % in the 19 "responders", "both stoneformers and non-stoneformers" [^S227]. A Cape Town man who took large doses for eight days raised his oxalate excretion about 350 % and developed haematuria with calcium-oxalate aggregates, the high-converter phenotype [^S210]. The counter-paper argued that the modest rises at 5 and 10 g a day "could be entirely accounted for by oxalate production during analytical procedures" [^S207]; the artefact is real for unpreserved samples and does not explain the later studies that controlled for it. Intravenously, with acidified samples, urinary oxalate reached about 80 mg at 100 g, under 0.5 % of the dose [^S232].

### 6.2 The stone cohorts

Exposure in every cohort is a food-frequency questionnaire. In 45,251 men followed six years, 1,500 mg or more against under 250 mg gave RR 0.78 (0.54 to 1.11), and the authors wrote that the data "do not support an association" [^S209]; in 85,557 women followed 14 years, RR 1.06 (0.69 to 1.64), "routine restriction of vitamin C to prevent stone formation appears unwarranted" [^S215]. The men's cohort turned positive with power: at 14 years and 1,473 stones, total vitamin C of 1,000 mg or more against under 90 mg gave RR 1.41 (1.11 to 1.80), P for trend 0.01, against magnesium RR 0.71, potassium 0.54 and fluid 0.71 at the top quintiles [^S225]. Pooling 156,735 women and 40,536 men with 6,245 incident stones, men at 500 to 999 mg a day of total vitamin C had HR 1.29 (1.04 to 1.60) and at 1,000 mg or more 1.43 (1.15 to 1.79), supplemental intake of 1,000 mg or more gave 1.19 (1.01 to 1.40), women showed no association at any band, and dietary vitamin C showed none in either sex "although few participants had dietary intakes >700 mg/d" [^S241]. The Swedish men's cohort is a research letter without a PubMed abstract; the Ferraro full text summarises it as supplement users versus non-users HR 1.92 (1.33 to 2.77), with a suggested dose-response of 1.66 for fewer than seven capsules and 2.23 for seven or more, and no recorded dose [^S236]. A four-study meta-analysis found men more affected than women, OR 1.62, with women null at every band and band-specific estimates too narrow to trust [^S247]. In absolute terms, HPFS ran about 3.1 stones per 1,000 man-years, so RR 1.4 is roughly 1.2 extra symptomatic stones per 1,000 men a year, about one per 80 men a decade, a derivation from the published rates rather than a figure the papers report. The AUA guideline on medical stone management carries no vitamin C statement in its abstract [^S238].

### 6.3 The upper level

The 2,000 mg Tolerable Upper Intake Level was set in 2000 on gastrointestinal tolerance. The year before, a review stated that intakes of 2 to 4 g a day "are well tolerated biologically" and that "strong scientific evidence to define and defend a UL for vitamin C is not available" [^S217]. The level's principal published defence states that numerous trials "have provided no pattern of evidence to support concerns about safety other than occasional gastrointestinal upset or mild diarrhea" and that iron-related harm "has not been confirmed in clinical trials"; its first author is at the Council for Responsible Nutrition, the supplement-industry trade association [^S226]. The upper level predates Taylor 2004, Massey 2005, Thomas 2013 and Ferraro 2016, and the stone signal in men sits below it.

### 6.4 Iron

In 11 iron-depleted women, 1,500 mg a day taken with meals raised apparent iron absorption from 27 to 38 % over 5.5 weeks and improved haemoglobin and serum iron without changing ferritin [^S204]. Across a complete diet, with vitamin C intake varied from 51 to 247 mg a day in 12 subjects, "there was no significant difference in mean iron absorption", and the single-meal effect is "far less pronounced" over a whole diet [^S219]. A Roche-authored review states that high intakes "do not cause iron imbalance in healthy persons", that homozygous haemochromatosis "has not been studied", and that such patients should avoid facilitated iron absorption [^S216]. One young man with autopsy-diagnosed haemochromatosis died of cardiomyopathy after a year of large ascorbate doses, a single case with no dose given [^S201]; the concern was articulated in a 1981 editorial without data [^S200]. No trial of vitamin C in diagnosed iron overload exists.

### 6.5 G6PD deficiency, laboratory interference and interactions

Acute haemolysis after high-dose ascorbate in G6PD deficiency has been reported since 1993 [^S206], including in a man receiving infusions for rheumatoid arthritis [^S243]. A survey of 172 complementary practitioners who treated 11,233 patients in 2006 with an average of 28 g every four days found 101 side effects in 9,328 patients, mostly minor, and concluded that "other than the known complications of IV vitamin C in those with renal impairment or glucose 6 phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency", the practice "appears to be remarkably safe" [^S233]; phase I oncology dosing at up to 1.5 g/kg three times a week produced minimal toxicity [^S26]. Ascorbic acid interfered with glucose readings on every handheld meter tested in vitro [^S218], but at 1.5 g intravenously every six hours in five septic patients the point-of-care bias was 8.9 mg/dL and "would not have resulted in a change in clinical action" [^S244]. Ascorbate inhibits the heme pseudoperoxidase reaction of guaiac stool tests and produced false negatives in a woman with unexplained anaemia [^S198]. The claim that vitamin C destroys B12 in meals was not reproduced when two laboratories repeated the exact meals with standardised assays [^S199]. The warfarin interaction is a 1971 case report [^S195] followed by a 1975 study titled "lack of a clinically important interaction" [^S197]. Dehydroascorbate pretreatment blunted the cytotoxicity of doxorubicin, cisplatin, vincristine, methotrexate and imatinib in leukaemia and lymphoma cells and reduced doxorubicin's efficacy in mice [^S230]; the opposite direction appears in a small ovarian-cancer study where intravenous ascorbate with carboplatin and paclitaxel "reduced chemotherapy-associated toxicity" [^S237]. Oncologists' advice to avoid high-dose antioxidants during chemotherapy rests on the preclinical signal plus the absence of benefit, not on a trial showing harm.

### 6.6 Pro-oxidant chemistry, mortality and rebound

A 1998 Nature letter reported that 500 mg a day raised 8-oxoadenine in lymphocyte DNA while lowering 8-oxoguanine [^S211]; its methodological rebuttal is also a letter [^S212]. The Linus Pauling Institute's review found "compelling evidence for antioxidant protection of lipids", "sparse and inconclusive" protein data, "inconsistent and conflicting" DNA data and in-vivo pro-oxidant mechanisms "uncertain" [^S214]. The most direct human test gave 38 volunteers 14 mg of ferrous iron with 60 or 260 mg of vitamin C: in those with higher baseline ascorbate several oxidised DNA bases rose at six weeks and returned to normal by 12, while in those with lower baseline damage fell [^S213]. In the test tube, ascorbate decomposes lipid hydroperoxides to DNA-reactive aldehydes without any metal [^S221]; in women held at steady state for six months, F2-isoprostanes were unchanged at every dose [^S21]. The mortality meta-analyses answer the vitamin C question in the negative: in 68 RCTs and 232,606 participants, "vitamin C and selenium had no significant effect on mortality" while β-carotene, vitamin A and vitamin E raised it [^S228], and the 2012 Cochrane update gives vitamin C RR 1.02 (0.98 to 1.07) across 29 low-bias trials [^S180]. The uricosuric effect that raises urinary urate also lowers serum urate by 0.35 mg/dL at a median 500 mg a day [^S234]. "Rebound scurvy" rests on two subjects whose urinary ascorbate dipped below baseline for 10 to 12 days after stopping 10 g a day [^S202] and a 1973 paper without an abstract [^S196]; no clinical case was found. In diabetic women, supplemental vitamin C tracked higher cardiovascular mortality, an observational signal in one cohort [^S170].

### 6.7 Oxalate nephropathy: who it happens to

Every renal-failure case involves intravenous gram dosing, pre-existing kidney disease or dialysis, or a malabsorptive gut, and often two of the three. A single 45 g infusion in a patient with amyloid nephrotic syndrome caused oliguric renal failure and fatal intratubular calcium oxalate [^S203]; a massive intravenous dose caused acute oxalate nephropathy in Australia [^S208]; a New Zealand patient died of renal failure with intra-renal oxalate after high-dose oral use disclosed only after death [^S231]; a lupus patient developed biopsy-proven nephropathy after intravenous vitamin C [^S235]; burn patients developed oxalate nephropathy after continuous high-dose infusions used as resuscitation [^S240]; a 74-year-old woman given 100 g weekly for six weeks by an alternative provider needed permanent dialysis [^S250]. The oral cases carry the second risk factor: a 73-year-old on 680 mg a day with chronic diarrhoea and furosemide went from creatinine 1.2 to 8.4 mg/dL and recovered to 1.8 after stopping [^S229]; a woman with remote gastric bypass and chronic kidney disease needed dialysis [^S239]; a man in his eighties with exocrine pancreatic insufficiency needed renal replacement within 14 days [^S246]; and a peritoneal-dialysis patient on up to 4 g a day for years developed systemic oxalosis with plasma oxalate 187 µmol/L against a reference below 1.7 [^S245]. In people with normal kidneys and a normal gut, oral vitamin C at 1 to 2 g produces a 20 to 60 % rise in urinary oxalate and a higher stone rate in men, not nephropathy. The largest placebo-controlled trial of gram-scale intravenous ascorbate, in 872 septic adults, found more death or persistent organ dysfunction, RR 1.21 (1.04 to 1.40), one severe hypoglycaemia and one anaphylaxis in the vitamin arm, ending the assumption that gram-scale ascorbate is harmless because it is a vitamin [^S249].
## 7. Intravenous Vitamin C in Oncology and Critical Care

### 7.1 The Pauling era and the Mayo replications

At the Vale of Leven Hospital, 100 "terminal" cancer patients given supplemental ascorbate were compared with 1,000 patients matched from case records: mean survival was "more than 4.2 times as great" for the ascorbate group, more than 210 days against 50 [^S251]. The 1978 re-evaluation, with the dose given as "usually 10 g/day", recounted survival from first hospital attendance and reported about 300 days longer survival, with 22 % against 0.4 % alive a year after being declared untreatable [^S252]. The design was retrospective and unblinded, and the moment of "untreatability" was a clinical judgement made prospectively for the treated and reconstructed from records for the controls. The Mayo Clinic ran the randomised trials. One hundred and fifty patients with advanced cancer were randomised double-blind to 10 g a day of oral vitamin C or flavoured lactose: "no appreciable difference" in symptoms, performance status, appetite or weight, and survival curves that "essentially overlapped" at about seven weeks [^S253]. Pauling objected that prior chemotherapy had damaged these patients' immunity, so the second trial enrolled 100 chemotherapy-naive colorectal patients in good condition: vitamin C "showed no advantage over placebo" for progression or survival, and no patient with measurable disease improved [^S254]. The remaining objection, that the Mayo trials were oral while Cameron had used an intravenous phase, stayed rhetorical until the NIH pharmacokinetics made it quantitative [^S23].

### 7.2 Mechanism: a pro-drug for hydrogen peroxide

Ten cancer and four normal cell lines were exposed to ascorbate for an hour: "normal cells were unaffected by 20 mM ascorbate, whereas 5 cancer lines had EC50 values of less than 4 mM, a concentration easily achievable i.v."; killing depended on extracellular ascorbate and was "absolutely dependent on H2O2 formation", and ascorbate added to whole blood generated no detectable peroxide, so the proposal was that blood delivers the pro-drug and peroxide forms in the tissue interstitium [^S255]. In mice, microdialysis showed that a single parenteral dose produced ascorbate radical and hydrogen peroxide "selectively within interstitial fluids of tumors but not in blood", and daily dosing slowed the growth of ovarian, pancreatic and glioblastoma xenografts [^S257]. A second mechanism was described for colorectal cancers with KRAS or BRAF mutations, which over-express the GLUT1 transporter: dehydroascorbate enters, its reduction depletes glutathione, reactive oxygen species inactivate GAPDH and the glycolytic cells hit an energetic crisis, with impaired tumour growth in mutant mice [^S266]. In lung cancer and glioblastoma cells, disrupted iron metabolism and labile iron plus peroxide explain the differential susceptibility; the abstract asserts clinical feasibility and "potential efficacy" without any clinical numbers [^S270]. The mechanism is coherent and reproducible at millimolar concentration, which a healthy adult eating fruit sits two orders of magnitude below.

### 7.3 Phase I and phase II

The phase I trials established safety and found nothing else. Sequential cohorts at 0.4 to 1.5 g/kg three times a week: "adverse events and toxicity were minimal at all dose levels. No patient had an objective anticancer response" [^S26]. Fourteen metastatic pancreatic patients given escalating ascorbate with gemcitabine and erlotinib: seven of nine completers with stable disease by RECIST and no added toxicity [^S259]. Nine stage IV pancreatic patients titrated to a post-infusion plasma above 20 mmol/L alongside gemcitabine: rare drug-attributable events and a mean survival of 13 months among completers, who by definition did not die early [^S260]. Fifteen patients at 30 to 110 g/m²: first-order elimination, half-life 2.0 hours, peak plateau at 49 mmol/L, "no patient demonstrated an objective antitumor response" [^S40]. Fourteen patients with poor-prognosis cancers given infusions with chemotherapy: safe, no rise in urinary oxalate, three with transient stable disease, and a study that "neither proves nor disproves" the therapy's value, with the ascorbate donated by a manufacturer [^S264]. In 2006 and 2008 the complementary-medicine practitioners who answered an NIH survey were treating about 11,000 patients a year with an average of 28 g every four days, and US manufacturers were selling 750,000 to 855,000 vials a year, against a randomised literature that by 2015 totalled 322 patients [^S233] [^S265].

The phase II literature is thin and mostly uncontrolled. The ovarian-cancer paper is largely mechanism, with its clinical result stated in one clause, reduced chemotherapy toxicity, and no sample size or survival figure in the abstract [^S237]; the NIH-affiliated systematic review describes it as the only randomised comparison and reports "an 8.75 month increase in progression-free survival" and an improved trend in overall survival [^S272], while the Ottawa review covering the same period states that "no RCTs reported any statistically significant improvements in overall or progression-free survival or reduced toxicity" [^S265]. A pancreatic mechanism paper with a phase I/IIa pharmacokinetic arm reports that the infusion "showed the possibility to prolong patient survival" without an n or a number [^S269]; a phase I of ascorbate during chemoradiation for locally advanced pancreatic cancer reports median survival of 21.7 months against an institutional 12.7 (P = 0.08), with historical rather than concurrent comparators [^S271]. A single-arm phase II of 75 g twice weekly with carboplatin and paclitaxel in 38 chemotherapy-naive lung-cancer patients gave a response rate of 34.2 % against a 20 % historical benchmark, median survival 12.8 months, one treatment-related death and five grade-4 cytopenias [^S282]. The one randomised trial with a survival endpoint randomised 36 metastatic pancreatic patients, 34 treated, to gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel with or without 75 g of ascorbate three times a week: median overall survival 16 against 8.3 months, HR 0.46 with a 90 % confidence interval of 0.23 to 0.92, progression-free survival 6.2 against 3.9 months, no added toxicity and no detriment to quality of life [^S286]. It was open-label, used 90 % rather than 95 % intervals whose upper bounds approach 1.0, and is the kind of result that shrinks on replication; its authors call for a larger trial and none has been published.

### 7.4 Quality of life and the reviews

Thirty-nine terminal patients given 10 g intravenously twice and then 4 g a day orally for a week improved on every quality-of-life scale over that week, with no control group [^S256]. A retrospective German cohort of 125 breast-cancer patients reported symptom intensity "nearly twice as high" in the 72 who did not receive the manufacturer's 7.5 g product than in the 53 who did; the first author works for that manufacturer [^S258]. A mini-review summarises these few small studies and their methods [^S261]. Four systematic reviews split on tone and agree on facts. A naturopathic review of 37 studies found "limited high-quality clinical evidence" and results "preliminary" [^S263]; the Ottawa oncology review of 34 studies found "no high-quality evidence" and called for placebo-controlled trials "given the high financial and time costs to patients" [^S265]; the NIH and Hopkins review of 23 trials and 385 patients called the results "promising" [^S272]; and a Maastricht haematology review of 19 trials, four of them randomised, "does not prove that there is a clinically relevant positive effect" because "the quality of the studies published is low" [^S274].

### 7.5 Sepsis: from Marik to LOVIT

Septic patients are biochemically deficient: in a phase I trial of 24 patients, mean plasma ascorbate on entry was 17.9 µmol/L against a normal range of 50 to 70, and 50 or 200 mg/kg a day for four days was safe and lowered SOFA scores, CRP and procalcitonin [^S262]. A 28-patient Tehran trial of 25 mg/kg every six hours reported norepinephrine requirement halved and 28-day mortality of 14 % against 64 % [^S267]. The before-and-after study that launched the "HAT" protocol compared 47 consecutive septic patients given vitamin C, hydrocortisone and thiamine with 47 from the preceding seven months: hospital mortality 8.5 % against 40.4 %, vasopressors off at 18 against 55 hours, findings the authors called preliminary [^S268]. The randomised trials followed. CITRIS-ALI randomised 167 patients with sepsis and ARDS to 50 mg/kg every six hours for 96 hours or placebo: the modified SOFA change was −0.10 (−1.23 to 1.03), and CRP and thrombomodulin were unchanged, all three primary outcomes null, with study materials from an injectable-ascorbate maker [^S273]. VITAMINS randomised 216 septic-shock patients to the HAT combination or hydrocortisone alone: time alive and vasopressor-free 122.1 against 124.6 hours, 90-day mortality 28.6 % against 24.5 % [^S275]. ACTS randomised 205 to HAT or placebo: SOFA change −4.7 against −4.1, 30-day mortality 34.7 % against 29.3 %, more hyperglycaemia and hypernatraemia, "these data do not support routine use" [^S278]. ORANGES found faster vasopressor withdrawal, 27 against 53 hours, and no difference in SOFA, mortality or length of stay [^S277]. ATESS, vitamin C plus thiamine without steroid in 111 patients, found SOFA change 3 against 3 and 18 null secondaries "despite improvements in vitamin C and thiamine levels" [^S276]. LOVIT randomised 872 ICU adults on vasopressors to 50 mg/kg every six hours or placebo: death or persistent organ dysfunction at day 28 in 44.5 % against 38.5 %, RR 1.21 (1.04 to 1.40), P = 0.01; death 35.4 % against 31.6 %; the dose was the high arm of the earlier phase I, so this was not underdosing [^S249].

The meta-analyses disagree with each other because they pool different things. Eleven RCTs and 1,737 patients before LOVIT give mortality RR 0.88 (0.73 to 1.06), shorter vasopressor use and a larger SOFA fall [^S280]; a component network meta-analysis of 33 trials attributes shock resolution to the steroid and finds ascorbic acid alone RR 0.74 for short-term mortality only, with "no strong evidence supporting the routine use" [^S279]; 22 RCTs including LOVIT give 28-day mortality RR 0.92 (0.81 to 1.04), a monotherapy subgroup of 0.69 driven by small trials, and septic shock RR 1.01 [^S285]; 44 RCTs across indications give a sepsis subgroup of 0.74 [^S99]; and the HAT-specific synthesis finds a SOFA difference of −1.16 at 72 hours, 18.8 fewer vasopressor hours, a longer hospital stay and "unclear" advantage over hydrocortisone alone [^S287]. When one 872-patient placebo-controlled trial showing RR 1.17 for death is pooled with a dozen trials of 28 to 200 patients showing RR 0.3 to 0.8, the heterogeneity is the finding. The consistent, low-value signals are faster shock reversal and a slightly faster SOFA decline that never became survival in an adequately powered trial.

### 7.6 COVID-19

A 56-patient pilot of 12 g every 12 hours in critically ill COVID patients found ventilation-free days of 26 against 22 (P = 0.57), no mortality reduction, and better day-7 oxygenation and IL-6 [^S281]. The decisive data are two harmonised Bayesian trials across 130 sites: enrolment "was terminated after statistical triggers for harm and futility were met", with 1,568 critically ill patients showing a median 7 against 10 organ-support-free days, adjusted OR 0.88 and a posterior probability of harm of 91.4 %, and 1,022 non-critically-ill patients OR 0.80 with probability of harm 97.1 %; survival to discharge was 61.9 % against 64.6 % in the critically ill [^S284]. The oral outpatient megadose trial was null [^S95]. Nothing in this chapter bears on a healthy adult's pill, because oral dosing cannot reach the concentrations the mechanism needs, and the two regimens that have randomised evidence of harm, 6 to 14 g a day intravenously for four days in the critically ill, show what gram-scale ascorbate does when it does reach them.

## 8. Whole Food Versus Synthetic Ascorbic Acid, Bioflavonoids, Deficiency and the Remaining Claims

### 8.1 The molecule is the same

Ascorbic acid is one molecule; the question is whether the food matrix changes absorption. In 15 subjects with a perfused jejunal segment, orange juice and a synthetic vitamin solution did not differ in ascorbate or folate absorption, both "avidly absorbed in the first 30 cm of jejunum" [^S288]. The study behind the "bioflavonoids add 35 %" marketing gave eight fasting subjects 500 mg as synthetic ascorbic acid or in a citrus extract: plasma area under the curve was 35 % higher and absorption slower with the extract, and urinary excretion went up in saturated and down in unsaturated subjects, a single-dose result in eight people never reproduced at steady state [^S291]. The Otago trials settle the steady state [^S35] [^S36] [^S37]. Whole fruit brings fibre, potassium, folate and polyphenols that a tablet does not, which is a reason to eat fruit, not a bioavailability argument. Fifty healthy volunteers sensitive to acidic foods had 33 epigastric complaints after three days of 1 g of Ester-C against 55 after ascorbic acid, the one solid human finding for buffered forms [^S299]. In 48 male smokers, slow-release 500 mg a day with vitamin E lowered oxidised DNA bases in mononuclear cells without changing steady-state plasma [^S297].

### 8.2 Deficiency in rich countries

NHANES 2003-2004 found 7.1 % of Americans aged six and over deficient, down from about 13 % in 1988-1994, with smokers a third lower and deficiency falling with socioeconomic status [^S30]; in 2017-2018 the adult figure was 6.8 % against 7.0 % twelve years earlier, with means 15 µmol/L lower in smokers, 11 lower in the obese, 10 lower in men, 8 lower in low-income adults and 27 higher in users of vitamin C-containing supplements [^S325]. Fitting sigmoidal intake-to-serum curves to 2,828 non-supplementing NHANES adults, smokers needed about twice the intake to reach 50 µmol/L and so did people over 91 kg, translating to about 110 mg a day for the general population, 165 for smokers and 155 for people of 100 kg or more [^S323]. In the UK's low-income survey, 25 % of men and 16 % of women were deficient and a further fifth depleted [^S300]; in Glasgow in 1992, 26 % of men and 14 % of women had low status, optimal status required fruit, vegetables or juice "three times a day or more", and older male smokers needed more than that to reach even a marginal level [^S294]. In EPIC-Norfolk, deficiency tracked older age, male sex, inactivity, smoking, deprivation and lower education, and the lowest quartile carried adjusted odds of 1.43 for the lowest decile of physical function, which the authors suggest "may reflect symptoms of latent scurvy" in a cross-sectional design that cannot separate cause from effect [^S319]. A self-selected German clinic sample showed 17.4 % insufficient and 3.3 % at potentially scorbutic levels [^S315]. Deficiency is "not uncommon in high income settings" and poorly surveyed outside the US and UK [^S321]. Among hospital inpatients in OECD countries the cumulative prevalence is 27.7 %, with scurvy present in half to two-thirds of the deficient in two studies, a sick and malnourished group [^S326]. Scurvy itself persists in identifiable people: 147 patients in 25 high-income studies, clustered in children with neurodevelopmental disorders, restrictive diets and psychosocial vulnerability [^S327], and 280 adult cases since 2000 marked by purpura, corkscrew hairs and anaemia, in restrictive diets, homelessness and isolation, often misdiagnosed [^S329]. Smokers need at least 140 mg a day to match non-smokers' pools [^S2]; the Nordic review points to 50 µmol/L as the target plasma level and proposes 140 mg a day for people of 100 kg or more and 40 mg more for smokers [^S324]. Low dietary vitamin C carried an odds ratio of 1.19 for periodontal disease in NHANES III, higher in smokers, with the first author at an oral-care company [^S292]. A 2026 commentary argues that the UK's 40 mg reference ignored six pre-1991 trials, the Pauling-tradition position stated against the saturation argument [^S328].

### 8.3 Skin, iron, urate, eyes, brain, mood, glucose

Ascorbate raised collagen synthesis about eightfold in cultured fibroblasts that had none to begin with, the cell-culture basis for the collagen claim [^S289]; the skin review funded in part by a kiwifruit marketer concludes that the efficacy of topical treatment "as opposed to optimising dietary vitamin C intake, is poorly understood" [^S313]. No trial of oral vitamin C alone improving skin or wound closure in well-nourished adults was found. For iron, crystalline and food ascorbate had "the same effect in promoting absorption", about 50 mg per main meal is enough, and the effect is largest in phytate- and tannin-rich meals [^S290]; over a whole diet it is small [^S219]; and in 440 anaemic adults, iron with or without 200 mg of vitamin C raised haemoglobin 2.00 against 1.84 g/dL, equivalent [^S320]. For urate, 13 RCTs at a median 500 mg give −0.35 mg/dL with 77 % heterogeneity [^S234]; in 40 gout patients 500 mg a day lowered urate 0.23 mg/dL against 1.9 with allopurinol, "no clinically significant urate-lowering effects" [^S309]; and in 46,994 men followed 20 years, intake of 1,500 mg or more carried gout RR 0.55 (0.38 to 0.80), an observational finding [^S301]. For eyes, 500 mg of vitamin C with E and β-carotene for 6.3 years did nothing to lens opacity in 4,629 people, OR 0.97 [^S295], while the same antioxidants with zinc slowed advanced macular degeneration in established disease, OR 0.72, and antioxidants alone did not, OR 0.80 [^S296]; PHS II gave cataract HR 1.02 [^S303]; the Cochrane cataract review of 117,272 people found no effect of any antioxidant vitamin and recommended no further trials [^S306]; the Cochrane AMD prevention review gives vitamin C RR 0.96 (0.79 to 1.18) with high certainty [^S312]; and in 24,593 Swedish women, users of roughly 1,000 mg supplements had HR 1.25 (1.05 to 1.50) for cataract extraction, 1.97 if also on corticosteroids [^S304]. For cognition, a review of 50 studies found higher vitamin C in intact than impaired groups and no within-group correlation with MMSE scores, with two authors funded by industry [^S314]; the Cache County cohort found a protective association for combined E plus C and "no evidence of a protective effect with use of vitamin E or vitamin C supplements alone" [^S298]. For fatigue, 10 g intravenously reduced fatigue in 141 office workers at two hours and one day, only in those with low baseline vitamin C [^S307]; two gold kiwifruit a day improved mood only in young men with high baseline mood disturbance [^S308]. For glucose, 22 RCTs in 937 people show that "vitamin C did not modify glucose, HbA1c and insulin concentrations" overall, with a glucose fall of 0.44 mmol/L in the type 2 diabetes subgroup [^S310]; 31 people with type 2 diabetes on 1,000 mg a day for four months cut postprandial glucose area by 36 % and systolic pressure by 7 mmHg [^S318]; and the GRADE meta-analysis of 28 trials in 1,574 diabetics gives systolic −6.27 mmHg with moderate certainty and HbA1c −0.54 % with very low certainty, concluding that the supplement "cannot currently be recommended as a therapy" [^S322].

### 8.4 Cancer and mortality: diet versus pill

The pill has been tested in about 37,000 people for a decade. PHS II gave total cancer HR 1.01 [^S178]; WACS gave cancer incidence RR 1.11 (0.95 to 1.30) and cancer mortality 1.28 (0.95 to 1.73) in 7,627 high-risk women, both pointing the wrong way without reaching significance [^S302]; SU.VI.MAX's nutritional-dose cocktail was null overall [^S168]; and vitamin C carries no mortality signal [^S180]. The food pattern that raises plasma ascorbate tracks the opposite way: top-quintile plasma ascorbate with about half the mortality [^S162]; dietary vitamin C RR 0.79 and circulating vitamin C RR 0.60 for cardiovascular death across 320,548 people [^S317]; and per 200 g a day of fruit and vegetables, all-cause mortality RR 0.90 with benefit continuing to 800 g [^S311]. Whether the pill fails because vitamin C is only a marker, because the whole plant matters, or because trial participants were already replete cannot be decided from these data; all three are plausible.

### 8.5 Food portions and cooking

The PubMed-anchored portion is the gold kiwifruit: half a fruit matched a 50 mg tablet [^S35], one fruit a day took young men with below-average status above 50 µmol/L and two a day saturated them above 70 µmol/L with rising urinary output [^S305]. Fruit, vegetables or juice three or more times a day kept Glasgow adults above the deficiency threshold [^S294]. Pepper cultivars range from 223 to 1,025 mg/100 g of dry weight and lose ascorbate with roasting [^S316]. Boiling frozen vegetables cost 18 to 60 % of their vitamin C depending on vegetable, pan and whether they were thawed first, with spinach losing 46 to 60 % [^S293]. A working rule: budget a 20 to 60 % loss for boiled vegetables and none for raw fruit and pepper; two gold kiwifruit, or a red pepper with an orange, reach the 200 mg "first dose beyond the curve".

## 10. The Avoid-List Entry, Worked Through

The owner's rule is "no standalone vitamin C supplement; get it from food". The pharmacokinetics say that a non-smoking adult who eats fruit or vegetables several times a day sits at or near the plateau: 100 to 200 mg a day fills leukocytes and brings plasma to 50 to 70 µmol/L [^S11] [^S18], two gold kiwifruit a day take a young man with below-average status above 70 [^S305], and a pill on top of that buys about 10 µmol/L and a few hours of higher urinary output [^S12]. The trials say that the dose a pill would add, 500 mg a day, did nothing to cardiovascular events, cancer, cataract or death over eight to nine years in 22,812 people [^S176] [^S172] [^S178] [^S303], and that in men a gram a day raises the stone rate by about 40 % [^S225] [^S241]. The exercise data say that nothing in a replete, training adult improves with a vitamin C pill, and that the combination with vitamin E at 1 g carries a reproducible marker cost and one outcome cost in older men [^S143] [^S132]. The infection data say that the pill shortens a cold by about half a day if taken all season and does not prevent it [^S83]. The rule holds.

The cases in which the rule should bend are status cases, not dose cases. A smoker needs roughly double the intake to reach the same plasma level [^S323] [^S2]. A person over about 100 kg needs about 155 mg a day [^S323]. Anyone eating almost no produce, or with a plasma ascorbate below 50 µmol/L, gains from repletion: the men selected for low status in the Greek trials gained 5.4 mL/kg/min of VO2max and 18.7 Nm of torque from 1 g a day [^S146], the men below 45 µmol/L in Arizona had fewer colds [^S85], the office workers with low baseline lost fatigue [^S307]. In all of these the fix is 100 to 200 mg a day from food or a small tablet, not a gram. The one pill use case with real effect sizes in a healthy person is a short course of 0.5 to 1 g a day around an ultramarathon, a ski camp or similar exertion in cold or crowded conditions [^S67] [^S83]. If a pill is ever taken, the safety data favour the 200 to 400 mg range in which no renal signal appeared in any cohort band [^S241] and the oxalate increment is within noise [^S205].

What to know rather than assume: a fasting plasma ascorbate in an EDTA tube, promptly stabilised [^S34], at or above 50 µmol/L is adequate by every definition in these sources and above 65 is saturating [^S44].

## 11. Funding and the Shape of the Evidence Base

The big null trials are public: PHS II and WACS were NIH-funded [^S176] [^S172], the Heart Protection Study by the MRC and British Heart Foundation [^S163], the NIH pharmacokinetic work intramural [^S11] [^S23]. The industry footprint sits in the formulation literature: the Ester-C crossover was authored by its manufacturer [^S46], the alternative-forms systematic review by authors advising Bayer, Haleon and dsm-firmenich and holding Bountiful contracts [^S54], the liposomal trials by product makers and their consultants [^S51] [^S53] [^S45], the Roche tablet study by Roche [^S7], the Roche iron review by Roche [^S216], the upper-level defence by the Council for Responsible Nutrition [^S226], the immune-mechanism review by a Bayer employee and a Bayer key opinion leader [^S88], and the breast-cancer quality-of-life cohort by the infusion's manufacturer [^S258]. The kiwifruit trials come from a group part-funded by a kiwifruit marketer [^S313]. The Linus Pauling Institute is an interested tradition rather than a commercial interest [^S15] [^S33] [^S214]. The advocacy literature on colds is largely one author's, inside and outside Cochrane [^S83] [^S89] [^S96] [^S328]; the Cochrane headline numbers are unfavourable to his thesis, which argues against bias driving them. Two supporting meta-analyses have been retracted. The pooled trial analysis of antioxidants found benefits for other vitamins "only in randomised controlled trials in which the supplements were supplied by the pharmaceutical industry" [^S182].

## 12. Method and Limits of This Review

Seven parallel evidence sweeps searched PubMed through the NCBI E-utilities during the run, read abstracts before citing, and ended in identifier tables. The tables were parsed into 331 unique candidate PMIDs, each resolved against the esummary record and matched on first author, year and publication type: 324 matched, none was wrong or missing, two were flagged Retracted Publication and excluded from the bibliography while being named in the text as retracted, and five letters, comments and editorials were kept only as records, one of them a research letter carrying original cohort data whose figures are quoted from the Ferraro 2016 full text because the record has no abstract [^S236]. The bibliography was generated from the verified records, and this report and the findings were written with PMID tokens and converted against it, so no citation can point at a source the gate rejected. The limits are those of an abstract-level review. Several numbers that circulate, the per-dose plasma values in the 1996 NIH table and its bioavailability fractions at 500 and 1,250 mg, live in full text and are reported here only through the abstract wording and the Blanchard reanalysis [^S11] [^S12]. The Institute of Medicine and EFSA documents are not PubMed records, so their values are verified only where a PubMed abstract restates them [^S21] [^S42]. Food composition values are database entries, not PubMed records, and the only PubMed-anchored portion is the gold kiwifruit [^S35]. The exposure in every stone cohort is a food-frequency questionnaire. Several 1970s and 1980s case reports and letters have no abstract and are cited for title and existence only [^S196] [^S208] [^S206] [^S195] [^S197] [^S200] [^S212].

## 13. Evidence by Population

**Healthy, non-smoking, produce-eating adults.** At or near plasma saturation from food; a pill adds about 10 µmol/L and urinary loss [^S12]; 500 mg a day for eight years changed no hard outcome [^S176]; a gram a day raises men's stone risk about 40 % [^S225]. No benefit shown; one cost in men.

**Smokers.** Turnover 40 % higher; at least 140 mg a day to match non-smokers, about 165 by the NHANES curve [^S2] [^S323]; plasma a third lower at population level [^S30]; a 272 mg cocktail repletes it [^S19]; acute 2 g improved endothelial dilation while 1 g a day for eight weeks did not [^S157].

**People with low plasma ascorbate.** Lower VO2max and torque, restored by 1 g for 30 days [^S146]; fewer colds at 1 g in men below 45 µmol/L [^S85]; less fatigue in low-baseline office workers [^S307]; 200 mg a day saturates in two weeks [^S44]. This is repletion, and food does it.

**Athletes and heavy exertion.** Colds halved under brief heavy exertion in small old trials [^S83] [^S67]; no performance, strength or lean-mass benefit in replete people and a marker-level cost at 1 g with vitamin E [^S143] [^S128]; the cortisol attenuation did not replicate in the largest trial [^S105].

**Older adults.** Lean-mass gain blunted on 1 g C plus E in 60- to 81-year-olds [^S132]; a 2 mmHg ambulatory fall at 500 mg [^S153]; more cataract surgery in Swedish women on about 1 g [^S304]; 200 mg helped depleted elderly inpatients with respiratory infection [^S69].

**Hypertensives.** About 4 to 5 mmHg systolic in eight-week trials [^S181] [^S191], nothing at five years [^S164], no conduit-vessel endothelial change [^S161]; no outcome trial.

**People with type 2 diabetes.** Postprandial glucose and systolic pressure fall in small short trials at 1,000 mg [^S318], HbA1c with very low certainty [^S322]; one cohort of diabetic women shows higher cardiovascular mortality with supplemental vitamin C [^S170]; no outcome trial.

**Stone formers, CKD, dialysis, malabsorption.** Oxalate up 20 to 60 % at 1 to 2 g [^S223] [^S222]; nephropathy and oxalosis in the case literature at oral doses as low as 680 mg when malabsorption is present [^S229] [^S245]; avoid gram doses.

**G6PD deficiency, haemochromatosis.** Haemolysis with gram-scale, mostly intravenous ascorbate [^S283] [^S243]; no trial in iron overload and a precautionary consensus to avoid [^S216].

**Cancer and sepsis patients.** Intravenous ascorbate is a research drug with a good safety record in screened patients [^S233], one small positive randomised trial [^S286], no high-quality evidence of benefit [^S265], a harm signal in sepsis [^S249] and in COVID-19 [^S284].

## 14. Vitamin C and the Other Dossiers

The collagen dossier's gelatin-plus-vitamin-C trial and its ligament-graft bundles depend on ascorbate as the hydroxylase cofactor; this dossier shows the cofactor role saturates at tissue concentrations reached by food and that no oral trial links extra vitamin C to collagen outcomes in replete adults [^S289] [^S313]. The zinc dossier cites the COVID A to Z trial for its zinc arm; here the same trial is the one outpatient test of an oral vitamin C megadose, and it was null [^S95]. The electrolytes dossier's lesson that a morning routine is sold on a rationale the physiology does not support repeats: the pharmacokinetic ceiling plays the role the natriuresis-of-fasting arithmetic played there [^S11]. The vitamin D, zinc, magnesium and vitamin K2 dossiers all ended at "deficiency treatment, not booster"; vitamin C ends there too, with the added twist that its deficiency is rarer in a produce-eating adult than any of theirs and its saturation dose is a piece of fruit [^S305]. The lycopene dossier's cofactor logic, that a carotenoid needs fat and time to reach skin, has a parallel in the collagen claim: a true cofactor at saturation does not become a drug at higher dose. The creatine dossier's finding that the "kidney damage" scare was a measurement artefact has an inverse here: the vitamin C stone risk is not an assay artefact, because the modern studies controlled for in-vitro conversion and still found it [^S222] [^S227].

## 15. Open Questions

1. Does repleting a measured hypovitaminosis change a hard outcome? The low-status trials show performance and symptom effects at 30 days [^S146]; no trial has selected for low plasma ascorbate and followed events.
2. Is the stone risk in men real at 500 to 999 mg? The pooled cohorts show HR 1.29 in that band [^S241]; a controlled-diet oxalate study at 500 mg in free-living men would settle whether it is dose or confounding.
3. Why women show no stone signal in two large cohorts [^S215] [^S241] when their urinary oxalate rises as much in the metabolic studies.
4. Whether a blinded therapeutic trial at 6 to 8 g a day at cold onset confirms or ends the dose-response hypothesis [^S89] [^S76].
5. Whether the pancreatic-cancer survival signal survives a placebo-controlled trial of adequate size [^S286].
6. Whether liposomal ascorbate's higher single-dose area under the curve is retained or excreted; no study has measured elimination [^S55].
7. Whether the 200 mg plasma-curve argument or the 90 to 110 mg leukocyte and mass-balance arguments should set the recommendation; the three camps use the same data [^S50].
8. What the lean-mass blunting in older men on C plus E means for a vitamin C pill alone; no C-only resistance-training trial with body composition exists [^S132].

    **1. Plasma vitamin C saturates at food doses: the dose-to-plasma curve is sigmoid, 200 mg a day is the first dose beyond its steep part, and raising intake from 200 to 2,500 mg lifts fasting plasma only from 68 to 85 µmol/L.**

    In the NIH depletion-repletion study the steep portion lay between 30 and 100 mg a day, 200 mg was the first dose beyond the sigmoid and complete plasma saturation came at 1,000 mg; the reanalysis gives 68.1 µmol/L at 200 mg and 85.2 at 2,500 mg and "no pharmacokinetic justification for the use of megadoses"; in women plasma and cells saturated at 400 mg. The plateau is as much tissue saturation as renal loss, and a healthy mixed diet already puts never-smokers near it, 65 µmol/L in NHANES II.

    **2. The cells the "immune support" claim is about are full at 100 to 400 mg a day: neutrophils, monocytes and lymphocytes saturated at 100 mg in men at 14 times plasma, and at 400 mg in women.**

    Leukocyte saturation at 100 mg a day and plasma-and-cell saturation at 400 mg in women bracket the range; two gold kiwifruit a day took young men above 70 µmol/L in a week and raised neutrophil chemotaxis 20 %; the Bayer-linked immune review itself puts prevention at 100 to 200 mg a day. Pauling's lower "optimum" of 2.3 g is 6 to 23 times the cellular saturation dose; a 1980 ex vivo assay that found neutrophil motility enhanced only at 2 to 3 g a day is a small study without clinical endpoints and conflicts with the saturation data.

    **3. Above a 500 mg dose absorption falls and the surplus leaves in urine; the renal threshold sits at about 86 µmol/L in men and 72 in women, and at pharmacological levels clearance approaches the glomerular filtration rate.**

    Bioavailability was complete at 200 mg and "at single doses of 500 mg and higher, bioavailability declined and the absorbed amount was excreted"; the tubular threshold was 1.51 mg/dL in men and 1.26 in women, unaffected by age; a 60 g infusion cleared at 100 mL/min with a 1.87-hour half-life, and the kinetics shift "from zero to first order" between nutritional and pharmacological doses. At 1 g, urinary recovery was 30 % from tablet or solution and 14 % from a timed-release capsule.

    **4. Oral and intravenous vitamin C are different drugs: 1.25 g by mouth peaks at 135 µmol/L and the same dose by vein at 885; the maximum tolerable oral regimen is modelled to top out near 220 µmol/L against 13,400 for 50 g intravenously.**

    Measured peaks were 134.8 ± 20.6 versus 885 ± 201.2 µmol/L and the 220 and 13,400 figures are model predictions for 3 g every 4 hours and 50 g by vein; infusions reach 20 to 49 mmol/L and clear in about two hours; the case series that launched the oncology programme restates the same ceiling. Nothing a pill does happens at the concentrations the intravenous literature depends on.

    **5. Synthetic and food-derived vitamin C are bioequivalent in every steady-state human study; the "bioflavonoids add 35 %" claim is one eight-person single-dose study from 1988.**

    Half a gold kiwifruit and a 50 mg tablet gave identical plasma, urine, leukocyte and muscle ascorbate over six weeks; 200 mg as tablet or kiwifruit gave identical 8-hour curves with 40 and 50 % of the dose in urine; "all steady state comparative bioavailability studies in humans have shown no differences"; a jejunal-perfusion study found orange juice and synthetic solution absorbed alike; and the citrus-extract study had n = 8 and no steady-state arm. A 1994 trial of three commercial forms has no PubMed abstract; ascorbate from food and from crystals promoted iron absorption equally.

    **6. Daily vitamin C does not prevent colds in ordinary people and shortens them by about 8 % in adults and 14 % in children; started at onset it has no consistent effect.**

    Cochrane pooled 10,708 community participants to incidence RR 0.97 (0.94 to 1.00), duration −8 % (3 to 12) in adults and −14 % (7 to 21) in children with daily use, and no consistent effect in seven therapeutic comparisons covering 3,249 episodes, confirming the 2004 version's RR 0.96; an independent Harvard meta-analysis gives RR 0.96 and −9 % duration; severity falls about 15 %, concentrated on the worst days. The best modern therapeutic trial, 3 g at onset in 400 volunteers, found no difference on any measure. Two in three care-seeking adults still believed vitamin C reduces cold symptoms in 1997; most supplement use is self-directed, with 23 % of products clinician-recommended, in a population where 52 % take something.

    **7. Brief heavy physical stress is the one setting with a large effect: colds were halved in 598 marathon runners, skiers and sub-arctic soldiers, from small old trials, and a 674-recruit trial at 2 g was null.**

    The Cochrane subgroup gives RR 0.48 (0.35 to 0.64); the Comrades trial at 600 mg a day found post-race respiratory symptoms in 33 % against 68 %; pooled heavy-stress trials give RR 0.50 and a wider military review 45 to 91 % reductions in five small trials; 674 marine recruits on 2 g for eight weeks showed no difference in incidence or duration, and 39 adolescent swimmers an incidence ratio of 1.01.

    **8. Pauling's megadose case was inference, not trial: a 2.3 g "optimum" from an evolutionary argument, a pooled re-analysis of four small trials, and a 250 to 4,000 mg band; the 2,349-person Toronto trial found no dose-response and put the optimum below 250 mg.**

    The 1970 estimate was evolutionary, the 1971 confidence levels came from pooling four pre-1970 trials, and the 1974 band spanned 16-fold; the first Toronto trial found non-significant incidence and 30 % fewer disability days and the second, across eight regimens, "no evidence of a dose-related effect"; the 1975 reviews found the benefits "minor and insignificant" and "no clear, reproducible pattern". Hemilä's audits later recalculated a 21 % shorter cold from the same 1 to 6 g trials.

    **9. Five hundred milligrams a day for eight to nine years in 22,812 people changed no hard outcome: cardiovascular events HR 0.99 and RR 1.02, total cancer 1.01, prostate cancer 1.02, cataract 1.02, total mortality 1.07.**

    PHS II in 14,641 men gave major events 0.99 (0.89 to 1.11), stroke 0.89, total mortality 1.07 (0.97 to 1.18), total cancer 1.01 and prostate 1.02, cataract 1.02; WACS in 8,171 high-risk women gave RR 1.02 (0.92 to 1.13) and cancer 1.11; the Heart Protection Study raised plasma vitamin C a third in 20,536 people for five years with a rate ratio of 1.00; SU.VI.MAX's 120 mg cocktail was null for ischaemic disease; Cochrane rates the primary-prevention evidence as one trial with no benefit.

    **10. Vitamin C alone carries no mortality signal in either direction: RR 1.02 (0.98 to 1.07) across 29 low-bias trials and 66,000 participants, unlike β-carotene, vitamin A and vitamin E.**

    The 2007 JAMA analysis found "vitamin C and selenium had no significant effect on mortality"; the 2012 Cochrane update gives 9.9 % against 9.3 % dead, RR 1.02; 50 RCTs of vitamin and antioxidant supplements gave major cardiovascular events RR 1.00 with no benefit in any subgroup.

    **11. Gram doses raise urinary oxalate 20 to 60 %: 1.2 to 1.8 % of the millimoles become oxalate, 6 to 13 mg a day per 1,000 mg, in stone formers and non-formers alike, and the in-vitro assay artefact does not explain the controlled studies.**

    Preservative-collected urine gave 6 to 13 mg more oxalate per gram; a placebo-controlled crossover at 2 g raised oxalate 20 % in normals and 33 % in stone formers with pH unchanged; 1 g for three days raised healthy subjects from 25 to 39 mg with an EDTA control excluding in-vitro conversion; isotope studies showed raised endogenous synthesis, and 1,000 mg twice daily raised the Tiselius index in 40 % of 48 people. The artefact paper's null at 5 to 10 g predates these controls; about 60 mg of ascorbate turns over daily and could make up to 30 mg of oxalate against 10 to 25 endogenous, ascorbate being the main dietary precursor of the endogenous half to three-quarters of urinary oxalate.

    **12. Men taking 1,000 mg or more a day have about 40 to 90 % more symptomatic kidney stones; women show no association at any dose, and food-only intake none in either sex.**

    HPFS at 14 years gave RR 1.41 (1.11 to 1.80) for 1,000 mg or more against under 90 mg, overturning its six-year null of 0.78; the pooled Harvard cohorts gave men HR 1.29 at 500 to 999 mg and 1.43 at 1,000 or more, women null, dietary null; Swedish supplement users had HR 1.92 (1.33 to 2.77), a research letter quoted through the Ferraro full text; the Nurses' Health Study women gave RR 1.06; a four-study meta-analysis found men more affected than women, OR 1.62. On HPFS rates that is about one extra stone per 80 men a decade.

    **13. The famous "antioxidants blunt training" results are vitamin C plus vitamin E results, and pooled across 18 trials the combination did not change VO2max, endurance, lean mass or strength.**

    Ristow used 1,000 mg of C with 400 IU of E; the Norwegian endurance and strength trials and the elderly trial used 1,000 mg of C with 235 mg of E; Yfanti used 500 mg with 400 IU. The meta-analysis gives VO2max SMD −0.14 (−0.43 to 0.15), endurance −0.01, lean mass −0.07 and strength P = 0.35; eleven weeks of C plus E around eccentric training showed "the complete lack of any effect"; the blunting camp's own review calls the effect "not a universal finding".

    **14. Antioxidant supplements do not meaningfully reduce muscle soreness, 0.2 to 0.5 cm on a 10 cm scale against a 1.4 cm threshold, and vitamin C alone protected neither force nor range of motion in any trial.**

    The Cochrane pool of 50 trials gives soreness SMDs of −0.30 to −0.05, rescaled below the minimal important difference, with 47 trials at high risk of bias; 3 g reduced soreness for the first 24 hours only or not at all; 1 g left soreness unchanged with "a delayed recovery" of function; 400 mg gave modest benefit; the oldest positive crossover had no untreated control.

    **15. The only randomised blinded trials of oral megadoses were null: 10 g a day in 250 cancer patients and 8 g a day in 214 COVID-19 outpatients.**

    The Mayo trials of 10 g a day found overlapping survival curves in 150 patients and no advantage in 100 chemotherapy-naive colorectal patients; COVID A to Z found days to 50 % symptom reduction of 5.5 on 8 g against 6.7 on usual care, P =.45, stopped for futility; the "70 % faster recovery" reanalysis has no abstract and re-reads a null trial; a 500 mg trial in critically ill Iranian patients had unequal, apparently unblinded arms.

    **16. Intravenous gram-scale vitamin C harmed septic and COVID-19 patients in the largest trials: LOVIT found more death or persistent organ dysfunction, RR 1.21, and the 2,590-patient COVID trials were stopped for harm and futility.**

    LOVIT randomised 872 ICU adults to 50 mg/kg every six hours: 44.5 % against 38.5 %, RR 1.21 (1.04 to 1.40), P = 0.01; LOVIT-COVID and REMAP-CAP found posterior probabilities of harm of 91 and 97 %; CITRIS-ALI was null on all three primaries, VITAMINS, ACTS, ORANGES and ATESS null on their clinical endpoints, and the Marik before-and-after study that launched the protocol was never reproduced. The small positive trials with 64 % placebo mortality drive the favourable pooled subgroups while the RCT-only pools are null; septic patients start deficient at 18 µmol/L.


??? note "Tier 2 — Well Supported (Moderate Confidence) (16 findings)"

    **17. Acute vitamin C improves endothelial dilation in sick arteries for a couple of hours; chronic 500 to 1,000 mg a day does not, and the chronic-only pooled effect is small with a lower bound of 0.02.**

    A 2 g oral dose lifted dilation from 2.0 to 9.7 % in coronary patients with abnormal baseline; intra-arterial infusions restored responses in hypertensives, smokers and heart failure, through a biopterin-dependent nitric oxide mechanism shown in cells and mice. In smokers, 1 g a day for eight weeks held plasma at 92 µmol/L and left dilation at 3.9 %, P = 0.26; in hypertensives, 500 mg a day changed nothing. Pooling any route gives SMD 0.50, beyond two weeks 0.25 (0.02 to 0.49), and the umbrella review finds "limited evidence"; the arterial-stiffness pool is all antioxidants together.

    **18. Blood pressure falls about 4/1.5 mmHg in eight-week trials at about 500 mg, more in hypertensives and the old; the one five-year trial found nothing, and no trial links the change to events.**

    Twenty-nine RCTs give −3.84/−1.48 mmHg, hypertensives −4.85 systolic with diastolic not significant; eight hypertensive trials give −4.09/−2.30; 60- to 80-year-olds showed a 2 mmHg ambulatory fall; 500 against 50 mg for five years gave identical rises of about 5.8 mmHg; hypertensives carry serum vitamin C 15 µmol/L lower. PHS II stroke was 0.89 (0.74 to 1.07).

    **19. Lipid effects are inconsistent and the HDL-blunting scare comes from a cocktail: LDL −7.9 mg/dL in hypercholesterolaemic patients against an overall null, and the statin-niacin HDL2 rise was blocked by vitamins E and C with β-carotene and selenium together.**

    The chiropractic-journal meta-analysis gives LDL −7.9 and triglycerides −20.1 mg/dL at 500 mg or more; the larger synthesis found no overall change with baseline-dependent subgroups; no LDL change at 500 mg in older adults; HATS attenuated HDL2 and blunted regression with the four-agent cocktail.

    **20. The observational benefit belongs to being the kind of person with high plasma ascorbate: about 20 % lower mortality per 20 µmol/L, stronger when measured in blood than as intake, null in genetic instruments, and read by its own authors as a fruit-and-vegetable signal.**

    EPIC-Norfolk gave half the mortality in the top quintile and 20 % per 20 µmol/L, "equivalent to about 50 g per day" of produce, and stroke RR 0.58 with the authors calling plasma vitamin C a lifestyle marker; per 50 µmol/L of blood, mortality RR 0.72 against 0.89 per 100 mg of intake, supporting produce "but not antioxidant supplement use"; NHANES I intake SMRs were 0.65 in men while NHANES II serum showed a threshold and no cardiovascular association; pooled cohorts disagree on diet versus supplement; Mendelian randomisation gives OR 0.90 (0.75 to 1.08) and nothing after correction in two larger studies.

    **21. Low plasma ascorbate is the population a pill moves: men in the bottom tenth gained 5.4 mL/kg/min of VO2max and 18.7 Nm of torque from 1 g for 30 days, men below 45 µmol/L had fewer colds, and low-baseline office workers lost fatigue; all of it is repletion.**

    The 200-man screen found the low group 25 µmol/L and 8.2 mL/kg/min below controls, restored by 1 g, after a falsification design in which only the targeted deficiency responded and a 100-man screen with marginal VO2max gains; an n-of-1 series found no true responder structure; 28 men below 45 µmol/L had cold RR 0.55; 10 g intravenously cut fatigue only in low-baseline workers and two kiwifruit lifted mood only in those with high baseline disturbance. The group's position is supplementation tailored to measured deficiency.

    **22. Marker-level blunting is real for 1 g of vitamin C with vitamin E: PGC-1α, COX4, SOD, TFAM and p38, ERK and p70S6K signalling were attenuated, Ristow's insulin-sensitivity result was not reproduced at 12 weeks, and the one outcome signal is a 1.4 % against 3.9 % lean-mass gain in older men.**

    COX4 +59 ± 97 % against −13 ± 54 % and PGC-1α +19 against −13 % with VO2max +8 % in both arms; SOD and TFAM attenuated with citrate synthase and VO2peak intact; signalling blunted with no effect on mass or synthesis; muscle IL-6 release blocked; insulin sensitivity +17.2 against +18.9 % with higher protein carbonyls on vitamins; lean mass 1.4 % (0 to 5.4) against 3.9 % (3.0 to 5.2) in 60- to 81-year-olds with identical strength gains, and hip bone density up only on placebo; no long-term effect on heat-shock or antioxidant enzymes; women's torque rose equally. The textbook mechanism, ascorbate scavenging the ROS signal, is chemically contested and an N-acetylcysteine infusion left PGC-1α intact.

    **23. The vitamin-C-only training evidence is thin in both directions: five supplemented men behind the founding paper, a null interval-training trial at 1 g, a 1.3 ± 1.3 % training-speed effect in women, abolished HSP70 induction at 500 mg, and no trial at 200 mg.**

    Gomez-Cabrera's human arm had 14 men, five supplemented, and its molecular data were rat; Roberts found equal gains with between-group P of.24 to.76; Braakhuis found "likely harmful" 1.3 % on training speed and no harm from a 15 mg food dose, and her review's 200 mg food threshold is a judgement; Khassaf found HSP70 induction abolished with baseline already raised. Rats, which make their own ascorbate, showed no blunting at 35 to 50 times the human dose, poorly mimic the human response, and the often-cited mitochondrial paper used no vitamin C; reviews on both sides concede the conflict, and the hormesis idea's best human support is a training study with no pill at all. The "worse than useless" letter is opinion.

    **24. The ultramarathon cortisol and cytokine attenuation sits in three small South African studies at 1,000 to 1,500 mg and did not replicate in the largest, carbohydrate-fed, double-blind trial at the same dose.**

    Post-race cortisol was lower only at 1,500 mg, a "transient" attenuation, with pre-race plasma of 128, 153 and 83 µmol/L; cytokines showed no interaction until groups were pooled; 1,000 mg in 16 runners gave 30 % lower cortisol and higher CRP; 28 runners on 1,500 mg showed no effect on any oxidative, immune or cytokine measure. Three tiny trials in exercise-induced bronchoconstriction gave a 48 % smaller FEV1 fall.

    **25. About 7 % of US adults are biochemically deficient, unchanged since 2003; a quarter of low-income British men were; fruit, vegetables or juice three or more times a day keep people above the line, and deficiency tracks smoking, deprivation, male sex, weight and inpatient status.**

    NHANES gave 7.1 % in 2003-2004 and 6.8 % in 2017-2018, with supplement users 27 µmol/L higher; the UK low-income survey 25 % of men and 16 % of women; Glasgow 26 % of men with produce three times a day lifting status; EPIC-Norfolk's lowest quartile carried odds of 1.43 for poor physical function; a German clinic sample 17 % insufficient; inpatients 27.7 %; deficiency "not uncommon in high income settings". Scurvy persists in restrictive diets, neurodevelopmental disorders, homelessness and isolation; low intake carried odds of 1.19 for periodontal disease.

    **26. Smokers and heavy people need about double the intake to reach the same plasma level: roughly 165 mg a day for smokers and 155 for people of 100 kg or more against 110 for everyone else.**

    The NHANES dose-concentration curves give the two-fold factor; radiotracer turnover shows smokers need at least 140 mg against about 100; heavy smokers' serum was 46.6 against 65.3 µmol/L with odds of 3.0 for deficiency; smoking depleted ascorbate specifically and 272 mg repleted it; the Nordic review adds 40 mg for smokers and 140 mg for people of 100 kg; body weight is a determinant no recommendation has used.

    **27. The requirement debate runs from 75 to 200 mg a day on the same data, and every camp's number is reachable from food: leukocyte saturation gave the IOM 75 to 90 mg, a 50 µmol/L mass balance gave the German societies 110 and 95 mg, and the plasma curve gave the NIH group 200 mg.**

    The IOM report is a book whose only abstract-verifiable value is 75 mg for women; the German derivation is 50 mg losses, 80 % absorption, 25 % urinary loss, 91 mg average and 110/95 mg reference; the NIH criteria give 100 mg average and 120 mg RDA or 200 mg from five servings; the Linus Pauling Institute proposed 120 and then 200 while cataloguing the field's artefacts; 100 mg a day reaches 50 µmol/L in free-living adults; the 20 mg/kg body pool fills at about 100 mg. A 2026 commentary attacks the UK's 40 mg, and the saturation argument explains why most supplement trials enrol people who cannot be raised.

    **28. Status has defined bands and one valid biomarker: deficiency below 11.4 µmol/L, marginal at or below 23, adequate at or above 50, saturating above 65; fasting plasma in a promptly stabilised EDTA tube is the measure, and baseline status and body weight shift the whole dose curve.**

    The cut-offs come from NHANES and the metabolic-unit studies; plasma tracked intake with less variability than cells and urine and saliva were useless; which leukocyte fraction to use was never settled; unstabilised samples under-read; men with hypovitaminosis reached only 30 µmol/L on an extra 50 mg while 200 mg saturated the replete in a week and the depleted in two; an SVCT1 variant shifts serum 5 µmol/L. Two transporters move the vitamin, SVCT1 in gut and kidney and SVCT2 in brain and tissues, the second indispensable, the first the renal reabsorber, with a glucose-transporter route for the oxidised form and the brain holding the highest and most protected stores.

    **29. No formulation beats plain ascorbic acid where it matters: Ester-C gives the same plasma curve and a gentler stomach, its leukocyte edge is manufacturer-run and single-dose, liposomal forms raise a single dose's exposure without anyone measuring whether it is kept, and slow release flattens the curve to no clinical importance.**

    Calcium ascorbate matched plasma with higher 24-hour leukocyte values in 15 men and in the manufacturer's 36-person crossover, showed no difference at 250 mg in an independent trial, and produced 33 against 55 epigastric complaints at 1 g in acid-sensitive people; the alternative-forms review, by authors with Bayer, Haleon, dsm-firmenich and Bountiful ties, found "no study reported retention in tissues". Liposomal gave 1.77-fold and +21 % AUC, 1.2 to 7.2-fold across ten studies with elimination never assessed, and the founding 11-person trial has a maker co-author; slow release was "small and unlikely to be of significant clinical importance" though it lowered oxidised DNA bases in smokers; 60-minute tablets absorbed best in Roche's hands. Eight men on 2 × 500 mg raised muscle ascorbate 30 % with no redox change.

    **30. Vitamin C in the same meal helps non-haem iron absorption, about 50 mg per meal is enough and food equals crystals; across a whole diet the effect is small, added to iron tablets it adds nothing, and diagnosed iron overload is a reason to avoid supplements.**

    Enhancement was largest in phytate- and tannin-rich meals with 50 mg per meal optimal; 1,500 mg with meals raised absorption from 27 to 38 % in iron-depleted women without changing ferritin; across a complete diet from 51 to 247 mg a day, absorption did not differ; iron with or without 200 mg of vitamin C raised haemoglobin equally in 440 anaemic adults; homozygous haemochromatosis "has not been studied", per a Roche-authored review that nonetheless advises avoidance, one autopsy case and a 1981 editorial carrying the concern; the UL defence calls iron harm "not confirmed in clinical trials".

    **31. Pharmacological ascorbate is a legitimate research drug, not a treatment: the peroxide pro-drug mechanism is reproducible at millimolar concentration, every phase I trial found zero single-agent responses, and the evidence for benefit is one 38-patient single-arm phase II and one open-label 34-patient randomised trial awaiting replication.**

    Cancer lines die below 4 mmol/L while normal cells survive 20, through extracellular peroxide generated in tumour interstitium in mice, with a GLUT1 and KRAS route in colorectal cells and labile iron in lung and brain tumours; phase I trials found minimal toxicity and no responses; the mechanism papers with clinical arms report no numbers or historical comparators; the lung phase II gave a 34 % response rate against a 20 % benchmark with one treatment death; the pancreatic trial gave median survival 16 against 8.3 months, HR 0.46 with a 90 % interval of 0.23 to 0.92. Four reviews split on tone and agree on no high-quality evidence; quality-of-life claims rest on a single-arm week and a manufacturer-authored cohort; the Pauling-era survival data were retrospective with asymmetric clock-starts; practitioners were treating 11,000 patients a year against 322 randomised.

    **32. Oxalate nephropathy and oxalosis require intravenous grams, kidneys that cannot clear oxalate, or a gut that over-absorbs it, and usually two of the three; oral cases carry the second factor, at doses as low as 680 mg with chronic diarrhoea.**

    A 45 g infusion in nephrotic amyloidosis was fatal; a massive intravenous dose, infusion in lupus, continuous burn-resuscitation infusions and 100 g weekly from an alternative provider ending in permanent dialysis make up the intravenous cases; the oral cases are undisclosed high-dose use ending fatally, 680 mg with chronic diarrhoea and furosemide reversing after cessation, remote gastric bypass with CKD, pancreatic insufficiency and 4 g a day on peritoneal dialysis with plasma oxalate 187 µmol/L; haemolysis in G6PD deficiency spans 1 to 200 g a day, 43 % oral, most undiagnosed; under 0.5 % of a 100 g infusion appears as oxalate in people with normal kidneys, and a Cape Town high converter raised his oxalate 350 % in eight days.


??? note "Tier 3 — Contested or Emerging (Low Confidence) (6 findings)"

    **33. Vitamin C is mildly uricosuric: 500 mg a day lowers serum urate by about 0.35 mg/dL in healthy people and did nothing useful in gout, where allopurinol lowered it eight times as much; the gout-prevention association is observational.**

    Thirteen RCTs give −0.35 mg/dL with 77 % heterogeneity; 40 gout patients fell 0.23 against 1.9 mg/dL on allopurinol, "no clinically significant urate-lowering"; 46,994 men showed gout RR 0.55 at 1,500 mg or more; urate excretion rose at 1,000 mg in the NIH study.

    **34. In type 2 diabetes, 1,000 mg a day shows a glucose and blood-pressure signal in small short trials, HbA1c with very low certainty, and one cohort of diabetic women shows higher cardiovascular mortality with supplemental vitamin C; in non-diabetics glucose, HbA1c, insulin and isoprostanes do not move.**

    Twenty-two RCTs were null overall with a 0.44 mmol/L glucose fall in the diabetic subgroup; 31 patients cut postprandial glucose area 36 % and systolic pressure 7 mmHg over four months; 28 trials give systolic −6.27 mmHg with moderate and HbA1c −0.54 % with very low certainty, "cannot currently be recommended"; diabetic women's top quintile carried CVD mortality RR 1.84 and stroke 2.57; isoprostanes were unchanged at every dose in healthy women.

    **35. Pneumonia prevention rests on three small trials with 37 events in 1940s to 1970s recruits and schoolboys, and the one well-run treatment trial used 200 mg in depleted elderly inpatients; the evidence is "too weak" for general use and reads as repletion.**

    The Cochrane review found 37 cases in 2,335 people with one adequately blinded trial and 197 treatment patients; 57 elderly inpatients on 200 mg "fared significantly better", most of all the depleted; a short piece on intake and susceptibility has no abstract; Hemilä's 2017 review tallies the same trials. For COVID-19 the meta-analyses conflict, RR 0.73 not significant, OR 0.59 with a longer ICU stay, RR 0.87 not confirmed by sequential analysis, and the pilot of 12 g twice daily was null on its primary.

    **36. The 6 to 8 g dose-response hypothesis is a post hoc reading of two 1970s trials whose own authors concluded the opposite, has never had a modern blinded test, and lost two of its supporting meta-analyses to retraction.**

    The claim rests on Karlowski, who attributed the 17 % effect to a broken blind, and the second Toronto trial, which found no dose-response, as reread by Hemilä; the 3 g onset trial was null; Ran 2018 (PMID 30069463) and Vorilhon 2019 (PMID 30465062) are flagged Retracted Publication, the paediatric critique being a letter; the bias narrative is one side's statement; the pharmacokinetics cap what oral grams can do. A five-year 500-against-50 mg trial in gastritis patients showed fewer repeat colds and no duration effect; a product trial without a stated dose reported fewer colds.

    **37. Cocktail trials that contained vitamin C cannot isolate it: a six-year carotid trial with C plus E slowed intima-media thickening 26 % in hypercholesterolaemic Finns with low baseline, an angiographic trial of C plus E in coronary women counted 16 deaths against 6, and HOPE and HOPE-TOO, often cited, contained no vitamin C at all.**

    ASAP's slow-release 250 mg twice daily with vitamin E slowed progression most in men and in low-vitamin-C participants; WAVE's 1 g of C with E gave HR 2.8 (1.1 to 7.2) on 22 deaths; HOPE was vitamin E alone with RR 1.05 and HOPE-TOO added heart failure RR 1.13; SU.VI.MAX's male-only benefit vanished after the capsules stopped; 4 g a day in heart failure improved an endothelial surrogate and worsened muscle metabolism.

    **38. Pro-oxidant chemistry of ascorbate is real in the test tube and transient at most in people: iron co-supplementation raised some oxidised DNA bases at six weeks and normalised by twelve, lipid peroxidation did not change at any oral dose, and no clinical harm has been tied to it.**

    The 1998 Nature letter and its rebuttal are both letters; the Linus Pauling Institute review found lipid protection "compelling", DNA data "conflicting" and in-vivo pro-oxidant effects "uncertain"; 14 mg of iron with 60 or 260 mg of vitamin C gave a transient, baseline-dependent DNA signal; ascorbate decomposes lipid hydroperoxides to genotoxins in vitro without metals; F2-isoprostanes were unchanged from 30 to 2,500 mg in women held at steady state; dehydroascorbate blunted five chemotherapy drugs in cells and mice while a small clinical study reported the reverse.


??? note "Tier 4 — Claims That Fail the Evidence (4 findings)"

    **39. "Rebound scurvy", "vitamin C destroys B12" and "vitamin C interferes with warfarin" do not survive their own literature: two subjects' urine dipped for ten days, the B12 assay was wrong, and the warfarin case was followed by a negative controlled study.**

    Urinary ascorbate fell below baseline for 10 to 12 days in two people who stopped 10 g a day, in a hypotheses journal, with a 1973 paper lacking an abstract as the other source; two laboratories repeating Herbert's meals found no B12 destruction; the 1971 case report was answered by "lack of a clinically important interaction". Real laboratory effects exist: false-negative guaiac stool tests and glucose-meter interference that is universal in vitro but not clinically meaningful at 1.5 g intravenously.

    **40. "Vitamin C prevents cataract, macular degeneration and dementia" fails: cataract HR 1.02 over eight years, no antioxidant effect across 117,272 people, AMD RR 0.96 with high certainty, no protective effect of vitamin C alone on Alzheimer's, and 25 % more cataract surgery in Swedish women taking about a gram.**

    PHS II gave cataract HR 1.02; AREDS found no lens effect from vitamin C with E and β-carotene and an AMD benefit only in established disease with zinc added; the Cochrane cataract review recommended no further trials; the AMD prevention review gives RR 0.96 (0.79 to 1.18); 24,593 women on roughly 1,000 mg had HR 1.25 for cataract extraction; cognitively intact groups have higher vitamin C with no within-group correlation and industry-funded authors; Cache County's protection was for E plus C with vitamin C alone null.

    **41. "Vitamin C pills build collagen and heal skin" fails for replete adults: the eightfold collagen rise is in cultured fibroblasts that had no ascorbate, the cofactor role saturates at tissue levels food reaches, and no oral trial links extra vitamin C to skin or wound outcomes in well-nourished people.**

    Fibroblasts raised collagen synthesis about eightfold with 3-fold lysyl hydroxylase when given ascorbate from zero; the skin review, part-funded by a kiwifruit marketer, calls the efficacy of topical versus dietary optimisation "poorly understood"; the fifteen-enzyme cofactor biology is real and concentration-bound.

    **42. "The 2,000 mg upper level means two grams is safe" fails for men: the level was set on diarrhoea in 2000, its main defence is first-authored from the supplement-industry trade association, and the stone signal in men appears below it.**

    The year before the level was set, a review said the evidence to define one "is not available"; the defence names osmotic diarrhoea as the only consistent adverse effect and comes from the Council for Responsible Nutrition; the men's stone cohorts of 2004 to 2016 postdate it; the NIH group set its own safe line below 1,000 mg on oxalate and urate; the AUA stone guideline's abstract carries no vitamin C statement.


??? note "Practical — what to actually do (3 findings)"

    **43. The Avoid-list rule holds: a non-smoking adult eating produce several times a day sits at or near plasma saturation, a pill on top buys about 10 µmol/L and a few hours of higher urine, and 200 mg is two gold kiwifruit or a red pepper with an orange, less 20 to 60 % if the vegetables are boiled.**

    The plateau runs 68 to 85 µmol/L from 200 to 2,500 mg; free-living adults at 100 mg reach 50 µmol/L; half a gold kiwifruit matched a 50 mg tablet and two a day saturated below-average young men; produce three times a day prevented deficiency in Glasgow; pepper cultivars hold 223 to 1,025 mg per 100 g dry weight and lose ascorbate when roasted; boiling frozen vegetables costs 18 to 60 %. Per-100 g values for orange, pepper and broccoli are database entries, not PubMed records. The produce pattern tracks RR 0.90 per 200 g a day for mortality and circulating vitamin C RR 0.60 for cardiovascular death, associations the pill has never reproduced.

    **44. When the rule should bend: smokers, people over about 100 kg, anyone eating almost no produce and anyone below 50 µmol/L are repletion cases at 100 to 200 mg a day, a short course of 0.5 to 1 g around an ultramarathon or ski camp has real effect sizes, and any pill belongs in the 200 to 400 mg range where no renal signal appeared.**

    Smokers and the heavy need about double; 200 mg saturates the depleted in two weeks and 272 mg repleted smokers; low-status men gained VO2max and strength from 1 g; heavy-exertion colds halved at 0.6 to 1 g; "doses above 400 mg have no evident value", the men's stone trend begins at 500 to 999 mg, and the oxalate increment at that range is within noise. Any pill paired with vitamin E at 1 g carries the marker and lean-mass cost.

    **45. Who should never take gram doses, and how to know rather than guess: anyone with a prior calcium-oxalate stone, kidney disease or dialysis, gastric bypass or other malabsorption, chronic diarrhoea, G6PD deficiency for infusions, iron overload, or chemotherapy without the oncologist's agreement; a fasting plasma ascorbate in a stabilised EDTA tube at or above 50 µmol/L is adequate and above 65 saturating.**

    The nephropathy case literature is these people plus intravenous dosing; stone formers respond to ascorbate like everyone else and were told to stay under 2 g; G6PD haemolysis spans oral and intravenous grams; iron overload has no trial and a precautionary consensus; chemotherapy antagonism is preclinical and unresolved; LOVIT ended the "it is just a vitamin" assumption at gram-scale infusion; the sample must be stabilised promptly and the adequacy and saturation cut-offs are 50 and 65 µmol/L.
