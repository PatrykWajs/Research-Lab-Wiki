    Ashwagandha: What It Actually Does vs What People Think¶

    The rare supplement whose popular use — a mild cortisol, stress and sleep lever — is genuinely supported, just modestly and at low certainty, while nearly everything above that core is oversold, and the flagship belief that "natural means safe" is false.

    Independent research, 4 September 2026. 64 cited sources (62 PubMed-verified — every PMID title-matched against PubMed, 0 wrong). Not medical advice.

    Start here¶

    Bottom line

    Ashwagandha is the rare supplement whose popular use is genuinely supported, but only modestly: it reliably lowers the cortisol biomarker and gives a small short-term sleep and stress benefit, on a short, small, manufacturer-funded evidence base with no long-term data. It is not a testosterone booster for a healthy man, "adaptogen" is a marketing word, and the flagship belief that "natural means safe" is false: it causes real, confirmed liver injury, with deaths and a transplant on record, plus a thyroid and transplant-rejection risk, and it is banned in Denmark.

    Myths vs. reality

    The claim you've heard What the evidence actually shows
    "Ashwagandha crushes your stress and cortisol" It reliably lowers the cortisol biomarker, but the best independent meta-analysis found cortisol falls while perceived stress does not, and the stress-scale effect is low-certainty, inflated by small manufacturer-funded trials
    "It's a natural testosterone booster" A modest men-only ~+57 ng/dL, carried by stressed, overweight and subfertile men and tracking the cortisol drop; the newest clean trial in healthy men was null, and no trial isolates a rise in a healthy non-stressed man
    "It fixes sleep better than melatonin" A real but small sleep benefit (best in diagnosed insomnia); in the one head-to-head, melatonin 3 mg slightly out-performed it, and there is zero polysomnography evidence
    "It's a safe adaptogen you can take forever" "Adaptogen" is a marketing term (EMA-rejected); no trial runs beyond ~12 weeks, and it causes confirmed liver injury (deaths, a transplant), raises thyroid hormones, and is banned in Denmark
    "It boosts your immune system" Only tiny marker studies — and the same immunostimulation caused a documented organ-transplant rejection
    "Ayurvedic means it's pure and natural" About 1 in 5 Ayurvedic products carry lead, mercury or arsenic above limits; safety depends on third-party testing, not the word "natural"
    "It protects your brain / prevents dementia" Zero human trials — the brain-protection data is entirely rodent and cell-line

    What you actually learn

    • The popular use is real, but modest and low-certainty. Cortisol drops reliably, sleep improves a little (best in insomnia), stress scores improve short-term — but the effect sizes are inflated by small, 8-week, manufacturer-funded trials, and one independent meta found the feeling of stress didn't move even as cortisol did.
    • The testosterone story is stress-correction, not androgen-boosting. The +57 ng/dL rides on stressed, overweight and subfertile men; in healthy men the newest clean trial is null.
    • "Safe because natural" is the flagship myth. Ashwagandha causes confirmed drug-induced liver injury — with deaths and a transplant — plus a thyroid-hormone and transplant-rejection risk, and Denmark has banned it in supplements.
    • The whole evidence base is short, small, and sponsor-funded, with no Cochrane review; the most independent appraisals are the most cautious.
    • "Adaptogen" is a marketing word, not a validated drug class.

    What this means for you

    • If you take it for stress or sleep, that's the one defensible reason — but expect a mild, short-term effect, not a cure, and know it doesn't beat melatonin for sleep.
    • Don't take it as a testosterone booster if you're a healthy, lean, low-stress man — the evidence there is null.
    • Treat "natural" as no safety guarantee. Avoid it, or use it only with monitoring, if you have liver, thyroid, or autoimmune disease, or take thyroid medication; never if pregnant, trying to conceive, or breastfeeding; and never as a transplant recipient or on immunosuppressants. Stop immediately and check your liver if you get jaundice or dark urine.
    • If you use it: a standardized root extract (KSM-66-type), 300-600 mg a day, third-party-tested for heavy metals, in a defined block (8-12 weeks) matched to a stress or sleep goal — not a permanent daily tonic.
    • Expect additive effects with alcohol and sedatives, and with blood-pressure or diabetes medication.

    TL;DR

    Ashwagandha is the unusual case where the popular use is not a fabrication — but it is modest, short-term, and low-certainty. Its one reproducible human effect is lowering cortisol15, and it probably produces small short-term reductions in perceived stress, anxiety scores and, best-evidenced, sleep (the sleep meta pools SMD -0.59, strongest in diagnosed insomnia at 600+ mg for 8+ weeks)14. The honest ceiling: the large "SMD ~-1.5" stress headline is inflated by small, 8-week, manufacturer-funded Indian trials at LOW-certainty evidence with heterogeneity up to 94%, and the one independent meta found cortisol falls while perceived stress does not86. Testosterone is a modest, men-only ~+57 ng/dL meta effect5 carried by stressed, overweight and subfertile men and tracking the cortisol drop — and the newest clean trial in healthy men is null36. "Adaptogen" is a Soviet-era marketing term, EMA-rejected57. And "safe because natural" is the flagship myth: ashwagandha causes RUCAM-confirmed drug-induced liver injury with deaths and a transplant on record19202122, raises thyroid hormones28, triggered a documented transplant rejection47, and has been banned in Denmark26. The popular use is real but oversold; the harmless-natural-tonic framing is not.

    What this review covered¶

    Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) is the best-selling "adaptogen" on the market, sold for stress, sleep, testosterone, immunity and general vitality. A seven-cluster evidence sweep — pharmacology and forms, stress/cortisol/anxiety, testosterone and fertility, sleep, cognition/performance/thyroid, safety and hepatotoxicity, and the marketing myths — tested each claim against the peer-reviewed literature: the randomized trials, the meta-analyses, the case-report and pharmacovigilance record, and the regulatory positions. Every PubMed citation was title-matched against PubMed before use (0 wrong), and three citation conflicts were resolved by hand. Unlike most supplements in this series, its popular use turns out to have a genuine signal — the honest work is separating that modest, low-certainty core from everything the marketing piles on top, including the false idea that "natural" means "safe."

    Tier 1 — The strongest, settled evidence¶

    Cortisol-lowering is the one reproducible human biomarker — but cortisol is a surrogate. Multiple randomized trials show a ~20-28% serum-cortisol drop versus placebo1234, and the hormone meta pools a large cortisol effect5. This HPA-axis lowering is the best human-anchored mechanism ashwagandha has. But cortisol is diurnally noisy, assay-dependent, and a surrogate — a lower number is not the same as a better outcome, and the decisive independent finding is that cortisol can fall while perceived stress does not (one independent meta found no significant pooled effect on perceived stress, and the independent US trial was null between groups even as cortisol dropped)67. The biomarker moves more reliably than the feeling — the reverse of the marketing.

    Perceived-stress and anxiety scores improve short-term — but at low certainty. The meta-analyses report large pooled reductions (anxiety and stress standardized effects around -1.5 to -1.8)89, but on heterogeneity up to 94%, GRADE certainty rated LOW, trials mostly 8 weeks or less, samples of 40-130, overwhelmingly Indian and manufacturer-linked108111213. The direction is probably real; the size and certainty are overstated. Depression is the weakest endpoint — no adequately powered standalone trial, very-low certainty, with publication bias detected13.

    Sleep is the best-evidenced benefit, with a clear ceiling. The dedicated sleep meta pools a significant SMD -0.59, larger in diagnosed insomnia and at higher dose and longer duration14; real-world magnitude is modest (sleep onset shaved ~12-16 minutes, efficiency up ~7-8 points)1516. But there is zero polysomnography — the objective evidence is wrist-actigraphy in only two trials, and everything else is self-report1718; "objectively fixes sleep architecture" is unproven.

    "Safe because natural" is refuted — this is the most important finding. NIH LiverTox scores ashwagandha "B" — a likely cause of clinically apparent liver injury — cataloguing ~23 RUCAM-assessed cases of cholestatic or mixed hepatocellular injury with jaundice19. The DILIN case series established the signal20, an Indian series reported three deaths in patients with pre-existing liver disease21, and a US case required a liver transplant2223. It is idiosyncratic, and short efficacy trials are structurally too small to detect a roughly one-in-thousands harm — so "no serious events in the trials" is not evidence against it.

    The whole evidence base is short, small, single-country and manufacturer-funded. Almost every pivotal trial is 12 weeks or less, n 40-130, Indian single-center, and supplied or funded by the extract's maker (KSM-66/Ixoreal, Sensoril/Natreon, Shoden/Arjuna); publication bias has been detected, there is no Cochrane review, and the most independent appraisals are the most cautious81324252627. Ashwagandha also measurably raises thyroid hormones (T3, T4 up; TSH down)28 — a narrow benefit in subclinical hypothyroidism, and a hazard for everyone else. Physical performance is the best-meta'd benefit (large pooled effects on VO2max and strength)29303132, but the inputs are small, industry-linked, and paired with training programs that drive much of the gain.

    Tier 2 — Real but modest, and population-specific¶

    Testosterone is the modest, population-dependent exception. The hormone meta pools a men-only ~+57 ng/dL (women's change is non-significant; no estradiol effect either sex)5, but the average is carried by stressed, overweight and subfertile men, and the coherent through-line is the parallel cortisol drop — this reads as stress- and deficiency-correction, not a direct androgenic action. The most-cited trial is confounded by a resistance-training program plus fat loss3334; the Sensoril strength trial was even testosterone-neutral35; and the newest clean trial in healthy men found no significant testosterone rise36. No trial isolates a testosterone increase in a healthy, non-stressed, non-training man3736. Male fertility gains (sperm count, motility) are real but only in oligospermic or infertile men — correction of a suppressed axis38394041. In women, ashwagandha does not raise testosterone, and the female sexual-function signal is subjective54243.

    Cognition and the immune claim. Cognition rests on single small trials each — MCI memory, bipolar cognition, a reaction-time crossover — none replicated444546. The immunostimulant action is real enough to be a hazard: a documented kidney-transplant rejection followed ashwagandha use, so it warrants caution in transplant, autoimmune and immunosuppressed users47.

    Tier 3 — Weak or preclinical: mechanism outruns the data¶

    The calming mechanisms are rodent-only. The GABAergic and serotonergic effects invoked to explain the anxiolysis are shown in mouse neurons, not humans, and are attributed to withanolide glycosides, not the marquee compounds4849; the human evidence stops at the cortisol biomarker. Withaferin A — the most-assayed withanolide — is mainly a cytotoxicity story (a reactive electrophile), not the reason a stress capsule works, and higher-withaferin leaf extracts are arguably a liability for daily use5051. Even the sleep mechanism does not line up: the proposed sleep molecule (triethylene glycol) is from mouse leaf extract, while the tested products are withanolide-standardized root52. Human pharmacokinetics is sparse (about four studies, low ng/mL, no dose-response link)5354, so in-vitro withaferin-A pharmacology does not translate to a swallowed capsule. "Ashwagandha 600 mg" is nearly meaningless — KSM-66, Sensoril, Shoden and generic powder differ by plant part and standardization and are not bioequivalent55. And for dementia or Alzheimer's there are zero human trials — only rodent and cell-line data56; in humans this is a myth.

    Tier 4 — Myths and refuted claims¶

    "Adaptogen" is a Soviet-era (1947) marketing construct with unfalsifiable criteria; the EMA rejects it for marketing authorization and no regulator recognizes it as a drug class5726. "A natural testosterone booster for anyone" is a myth for the general man — the signal is extract-, population- and industry-specific, and the newest healthy-men trial is null53658. "Boosts immunity" rests on tiny marker studies and is double-edged (the same immunostimulation caused a transplant rejection)47. "Beats melatonin" is a myth — in the one head-to-head, melatonin 3 mg slightly out-performed ashwagandha14. "A safe daily tonic you can take forever" is a myth — no trial runs beyond ~12 weeks, and regulators (Denmark's ban, an EU procedure, Dutch liver warnings) do not treat the file as settled265960, and it is a precautionary contraindication in pregnancy6162. And "Ayurvedic means pure" is false — about one in five Ayurvedic products carry lead, mercury or arsenic above limits6364, so real-world safety depends on third-party testing, not on the word "natural."

    Bottom line¶

    Ashwagandha is a rare case where the popular use — a mild, short-term cortisol, stress and sleep lever — is genuinely supported, just modestly and at low certainty, on a manufacturer-funded evidence base with no long-term data. Everything above that core — a general testosterone boost, immune boosting, a validated "adaptogen" identity, a consequence-free daily tonic — is oversold, and the flagship consumer belief that "natural" means "safe" is contradicted by a real, RUCAM-confirmed liver-injury signal with deaths and a transplant. Used sensibly — a standardized root extract, 300-600 mg a day, third-party-tested for heavy metals, in a time-limited block matched to a stress or sleep goal — it is a reasonable tool. Taken as a permanent daily tonic, as a testosterone booster, or trusted because it is "natural," it is not what it is sold as.

    The full findings — all 35¶

    Every conclusion this review reached, grouped by how strongly the evidence backs it. Each finding is distilled from the fully-cited evidence above.

    Tier 1 — Settled: strongest, cleanest, multi-study evidence (9 findings)

    1. Ashwagandha's one reproducible human biomarker effect is lowering cortisol — but cortisol is a noisy surrogate, not a clinical outcome.

    Multiple RCTs show a ~20-28% serum-cortisol drop vs placebo (Chandrasekhar −27.9%), and the hormone meta pools a large cortisol effect (SMD −1.18). This HPA-axis lowering is the single best human-anchored mechanism the herb has. But cortisol is diurnally noisy, assay-dependent, and a surrogate — a lower number is not the same as a better outcome.

    2. Perceived-stress and anxiety SCORES improve short-term vs placebo — but at LOW-certainty evidence with extreme heterogeneity and inflated effect sizes.

    Meta-analyses report large pooled reductions (anxiety SMD −1.55, stress −1.75), but on I² ≈ 83-94% heterogeneity, GRADE certainty rated LOW, trials mostly ≤8 weeks, n 40-130, overwhelmingly Indian and manufacturer-linked. The direction is probably real; the "SMD ≈ −1.5" headline is inflated by small, short, sponsored trials.

    3. The decisive independent nuance: cortisol can fall while perceived stress does NOT — the biomarker moves more reliably than the feeling.

    The one independent meta focused on this found a real cortisol drop but no significant pooled effect on perceived stress (SMD −0.36, p=0.40), and the independent US trial (Remenapp) was null between groups on mood/stress even as cortisol fell. This is the reverse of the marketing, and it is the honest ceiling on the stress claim.

    4. Sleep is the best-evidenced benefit — a small-to-moderate real effect, concentrated in diagnosed insomnia at higher dose and longer duration.

    The dedicated sleep meta pooled SMD −0.59 (5 RCTs, n=400), larger in the insomnia subgroup (−0.84), at ≥600 mg/day (−0.69), and ≥8 weeks (−0.68); it is weak-to-null in healthy sleepers. Real-world magnitude is modest — sleep onset shaved ~12-16 min, efficiency +7-8 points.

    5. There is ZERO polysomnography evidence — every sleep trial is subjective questionnaires plus actigraphy in only two, so "objectively fixes sleep architecture" is unproven.

    No human RCT has used the gold-standard PSG; objective data is wrist-actigraphy (a movement proxy) in just Langade 2021 and Deshpande 2020, and everything else is self-report (PSQI, diaries) — the most placebo-sensitive instrument. There is no data on sleep stages, REM, or slow-wave sleep.

    6. "Safe because it's natural" is false — ashwagandha causes RUCAM-confirmed drug-induced liver injury, with deaths and a transplant.

    NIH LiverTox scores it "B" (a likely cause of clinically apparent liver injury) with ~23 published cases; the DILIN case series (Björnsson) and RUCAM-"probable" reports document cholestatic/mixed hepatocellular injury with jaundice, one series reported 3 deaths in patients with pre-existing liver disease, and another an acute liver failure requiring transplant. It is idiosyncratic (variable latency, not tight dose-response), and short RCTs are structurally too small to detect it.

    7. The whole evidence base is short, small, single-country and manufacturer-funded, with no Cochrane review — so confidence should be downgraded, not upgraded.

    Almost every pivotal RCT is ≤12 weeks, n 40-130, Indian single-center, and supplied/funded by the extract maker (KSM-66/Ixoreal, Sensoril/Natreon, Shoden/Arjuna); publication bias has been detected, no Cochrane systematic review exists, and the most independent appraisals are the most cautious. Certainty drops as independence rises — itself a tell.

    8. Ashwagandha measurably raises thyroid hormones — a narrow benefit in subclinical hypothyroidism and a real hazard for everyone else.

    In the subclinical-hypothyroid RCT it raised T3 +41.5% and T4 +19.6% and lowered TSH −17.4% over 8 weeks. The same pharmacodynamic push can move a euthyroid or hyperthyroid person out of range (documented thyrotoxicosis) and can potentiate levothyroxine — so it is beneficial only in that narrow group and a monitored-caution elsewhere.

    9. Physical performance is the best-meta'd benefit — but the large effects come from small, industry-linked, training-confounded trials.

    The Bayesian meta reports large pooled effects on VO₂max (d≈1.85), strength/power (d≈0.68) and recovery, but the inputs are small, mostly Indian, several manufacturer-funded, and paired with a supervised training program that itself drives much of the gain. Read it as "large signal, low-quality inputs."

    Tier 2 — Probable / population-dependent (9 findings)

    10. The modest testosterone rise is real on average but population-dependent — it tracks cortisol-drop, fat-loss and sleep, not a direct androgenic action.

    The hormone meta pools a men-only +57.43 ng/dL (women +5.09, non-significant; no estradiol effect either sex), but the average is carried by stressed, overweight and subfertile men, and the coherent through-line is the parallel cortisol drop (SMD −1.18). This reads as stress- and deficiency-correction, not testicular stimulation.

    11. The newest clean trial in HEALTHY men found no significant testosterone rise — undercutting the "T booster for anyone" claim.

    Khanna 2026 (KSM-66, 76 healthy men, 8 weeks) reported no significant between-group difference in testosterone; sexual-desire and erectile scores improved, but the hormone did not. The strongest recent, cleanest-population data point moves against the general-male booster story.

    12. No trial isolates a testosterone increase in a healthy, non-stressed, eugonadal, weight-stable, non-training man.

    Every positive testosterone trial is confounded by a stressed/overweight population, a resistance-training program, low-desire selection, or subfertility. The specific claim that ashwagandha raises T in an already-healthy man, by itself, has never been demonstrated.

    13. Male fertility improvements are real but only in subfertile/infertile/stressed men — deficiency-axis correction, not a booster effect.

    In oligospermic men, ashwagandha raised sperm count ~167% and improved motility/volume, and in infertile men it normalized a suppressed hormone axis (T↑, LH↑, FSH↓). The effect is correction of a dysregulated baseline, and does not generalize to a fertile, healthy man.

    14. In women, ashwagandha does not raise testosterone and changes no estradiol — the female sexual-function signal is subjective, not hormone-mediated.

    The hormone meta found no significant testosterone change in women and no estradiol effect in either sex; the one female-sexual-function pilot improved subjective FSFI scores without demonstrating a hormonal mechanism.

    15. Strength and body-composition gains are real WITH a training program — but the STAR trial was testosterone-neutral, so the muscle effect does not run through a T rise.

    Wankhede showed greater strength, muscle size and fat loss under resistance training, and the Sensoril STAR trial showed strength/recovery gains with no significant testosterone difference. The performance benefit is real in trained contexts but is not a testosterone story.

    16. Depression is the weakest endpoint — secondary subscales only, very-low certainty, with publication bias detected.

    There is no adequately powered standalone depression RCT; the signal appears only as DASS/secondary subscales, the mental-disorders meta rated depression very-low GRADE (SMD −1.28, wide CI) and detected publication bias for anxiety. The anti-anxiety signal does not transfer to a clean depression claim.

    17. Cognition and memory rest on single small RCTs each, with no independent replication.

    The MCI-memory trial (Choudhary), the bipolar-cognition trial (3 of ~12 tasks positive), and the healthy-men reaction-time crossover (n=20) are each one small, mostly KSM-66/Sensoril study. Direction is favorable but the evidence is thin and unreplicated.

    18. Immunostimulant activity is real enough to be a hazard — a documented kidney-transplant rejection followed ashwagandha use.

    A kidney-transplant recipient developed acute T-cell-mediated rejection and lost the graft after taking ashwagandha, consistent with its Th1-activating/immunostimulant action. It therefore warrants active caution in transplant recipients, autoimmune disease, and anyone on immunosuppressants.

    Tier 3 — Weak / preclinical / mechanism (6 findings)

    19. The calming mechanisms people cite (GABAergic, serotonergic) are rodent/in-vitro only — the sole human-anchored mechanism is cortisol lowering.

    Direct GABA_A/ρ-receptor activity and serotonergic effects are shown in mouse neurons and oocytes, not humans, and are attributed to withanolide glycosides, not the marquee compounds. The human evidence stops at the cortisol biomarker.

    20. Withaferin A — the most-studied, most-assayed withanolide — is mainly a cytotoxicity story, not the reason a stress/sleep capsule works.

    Withaferin A is a reactive electrophile that covalently modifies many proteins, making it potently cytotoxic to normal as well as cancer cells; its pharmacology is an anticancer/in-vitro story, and higher-withaferin leaf extracts are arguably a liability for daily use. The compounds plausibly behind the calming effect are the harder-to-measure glycosides.

    21. The proposed sleep molecule (triethylene glycol) is from mouse LEAF extract, but the tested products are withanolide-standardized ROOT — the mechanism and the product don't line up.

    Triethylene glycol induced NREM sleep in mice while the withanolide-rich alcoholic extract did not, yet the human sleep trials use withanolide-standardized root products. The clinical benefit and the proposed molecule come from different plant fractions.

    22. Human pharmacokinetics is real but sparse — low ng/mL plasma, only ~4 studies, and no PK/PD link.

    Withanolides are absorbed to only single-digit-to-tens-of-ng/mL levels, from just four human PK studies, with no data linking a plasma concentration to a clinical effect. In-vitro withaferin-A pharmacology runs at micromolar concentrations far above what an oral capsule achieves — so straight-line extrapolation from the bench is unjustified.

    23. "Ashwagandha 600 mg" is nearly meaningless — the brand, plant part and standardization determine everything.

    KSM-66 (root, low-withaferin) ≠ Sensoril (root+leaf) ≠ Shoden (≥35% glycosides) ≠ generic powder, and at equal total withanolide dose two products are not bioequivalent. Pooling different brands, or extrapolating a branded trial to generic powder, is not valid.

    24. Dementia and Alzheimer's: zero human RCTs — the "brain-protective" claim is rodent and cell-line only.

    There are no human trials for dementia or slowing cognitive decline; the Aβ-plaque and neurite-regeneration findings are from the 5xFAD mouse and cell lines (withanolide A). In humans this is a myth, not a proven neuroprotectant.

    Tier 4 — Myths (6 findings)

    25. "Adaptogen" is a Soviet-era marketing/heritage construct, not a validated pharmacological or regulatory class.

    The term was coined by Lazarev in 1947 for a synthetic compound and its criteria ("non-specific resistance," "normalizing") are essentially unfalsifiable; the EMA rejects it for marketing authorization and it has never been adopted as a class by the FDA or EMA. Ashwagandha's specific effects stand or fall on trial data, not on the adaptogen label.

    26. "Natural testosterone booster for anyone" is a myth for the general man — the signal is confined to specific extracts, stressed/overweight/subfertile populations, and industry trials.

    Positive testosterone data is extract- and population-specific and manufacturer-linked, and the newest clean trial in healthy men is null between groups. For a healthy, non-stressed man it is not a demonstrated booster.

    27. "Boosts immunity" is overstated — only tiny mechanistic RCTs exist, and the immunostimulation is itself a transplant/autoimmune hazard.

    Immune claims rest on small marker studies (NK-cell/WBC counts) with no hard clinical immune outcomes, and the very same Th1 activation caused a documented transplant rejection. "Boosts immunity" is both under-evidenced and double-edged.

    28. "Beats melatonin for sleep" is a myth — in the one head-to-head, melatonin 3 mg slightly out-performed it on every metric.

    In a 4-arm insomnia trial, melatonin 3 mg edged ashwagandha 600 mg on PSQI, sleep-onset latency and efficiency, and the combination was best. At best a tie, at worst a slight loss — not the superior natural hypnotic it is sold as.

    29. "A safe daily tonic you can take forever" is a myth — there is no RCT beyond ~12 weeks, so long-term efficacy and safety are unknown.

    Every trial is ≤12 weeks, reviewers explicitly flag the absence of >180-day data, and regulators (Denmark's outright restriction, an EU Article 8 procedure, RIVM/Lareb liver warnings) do not treat the file as settled. Indefinite daily use is unstudied and shadowed by the liver and thyroid signals.

    30. "Ayurvedic means pure and clean" is a myth — about 1 in 5 Ayurvedic products carry lead, mercury or arsenic above limits.

    JAMA surveys found ~20% of Ayurvedic products (US stores and online) exceeded regulatory metal limits at recommended dosing. Real-world safety depends on third-party testing, not on the "natural" label.

    Practical — what to actually do (5 findings)

    31. Contraindicated in pregnancy and lactation — on precaution, though the strength of the abortifacient evidence is itself disputed.

    Traditional abortifacient use plus the cytotoxic withaferin A and the absence of adequate reproductive-toxicity data support a precautionary contraindication; recent ethnobotanical reviews argue the abortifacient literature is inflated and inconclusive, but the precaution stands. Do not use if pregnant, trying to conceive, or breastfeeding.

    32. Avoid or use only with monitoring if you have liver, thyroid, autoimmune disease, or are a transplant recipient — and stop immediately if jaundice or dark urine appear.

    The liver-injury, thyroid-hormone and immunostimulant signals converge on a clear caution list: pre-existing liver disease, hyper/hypothyroidism or thyroid meds, autoimmune disease, transplant/immunosuppression. Any jaundice, dark urine, or right-upper-quadrant pain warrants stopping and checking liver enzymes.

    33. If you use it, the sensible form is a standardized ROOT extract (KSM-66-type), 300-600 mg/day, third-party-tested, for a defined block matched to a goal — not indefinitely.

    The trials that work use standardized root extracts at 300-600 mg/day for 8-12 weeks; low-dose MK-4-style claims aside, root beats leaf for daily-use safety (less withaferin A), and heavy-metal testing matters more than the "natural" label. Match a defined course to a goal (stress or sleep) rather than taking it as a permanent tonic.

    34. Expect additive drug effects — sedation with alcohol/benzodiazepines, and possible additive glucose- and blood-pressure-lowering.

    Its GABA-mimetic action can add to CNS depressants (alcohol, benzodiazepines), and its modest glucose- and BP-lowering can add to antidiabetic and antihypertensive drugs. These are mostly theoretical/mechanistic but worth flagging for anyone on those medications.

    35. For Patryk's stack (ashwagandha before sleep, for cortisol/sleep support), it is the one item with a defensible mechanism for the goal — but it is not a T booster for him, not risk-free, and should be time-limited and third-party-tested.

    This is the single supplement in the stack with real (if modest, low-certainty) evidence for what it is taken for — a cortisol/sleep lever — unlike vitamin D, zinc or magnesium whose "T-boost" rationale is deficiency-only. But it does not raise testosterone in a healthy man, it carries a real liver + thyroid risk, and it should be cycled and heavy-metal-tested, not taken as a permanent daily tonic.
