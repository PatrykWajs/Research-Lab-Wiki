    Melatonin: What It Actually Does vs What People Think¶

    A clock-shifting darkness signal that has been mis-sold as a sleeping pill. Almost every consumer error about melatonin, the dose, the timing and the disappointment, follows from that single category mistake.

    Independent research, 9 September 2026. 139 cited sources; 223 PMIDs resolved and 193 title-matched claim/citation pairs verified against PubMed, 0 wrong. Not medical advice.

    Start here¶

    Bottom line

    *Melatonin is a clock-shifting darkness signal, not a sleeping pill. As a sleep aid it buys you about 4 to 7 minutes, which is why sleep doctors formally recommend against it for insomnia; as a tool for a timing problem such as jet lag or a delayed sleep phase it genuinely works. Timing matters far more than milligrams, mistimed melatonin shifts your clock the wrong way, and the usual 3 to 10 mg dose is 10 to 100 times more than your body ever makes at night.*

    Myths vs. reality

    The claim you've heard What the evidence actually shows
    "Melatonin is a natural sleeping pill" It is a timing signal. The measured hypnotic effect is 4 to 7 minutes off sleep latency, and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends clinicians NOT use it for chronic insomnia
    "Take more for a stronger effect" 0.5 mg equals 3.0 mg when each is given at its own optimal time; the clock responds to timing, not dose. One blind patient entrained on 0.5 mg and failed on 10 and 20 mg
    "Take it 30 minutes before bed" Bedtime is the phase-shifting dead zone. For a real clock shift the dose belongs hours earlier, and morning melatonin actively shifts your clock the wrong way
    "It stops your body making its own" Refuted by every study that measured it. Half a milligram for a week, and even 50 mg a day for 37 days, left natural production unchanged. It moves your rhythm, it does not switch off your pineal gland
    "Your melatonin collapses as you age, so replace it" In healthy older adults, melatonin was indistinguishable from young adults on every measure. What shifts with age is the timing of the peak, not the amount
    "I'm low in melatonin, so I should supplement" In the largest trial to test it, low melatonin did not predict who responded. Age did
    "It's natural, so it's harmless" The FDA's own survey found products at 0 to 667 percent of their label, and melatonin measurably impairs glucose tolerance in carriers of a common gene variant
    "Melatonin is an antioxidant that fights cancer and ageing" No human longevity trial exists. That literature is preclinical and unusually retraction-prone, and the famous cancer-survival result came from unblinded trials at a single hospital
    "Blue-blocking glasses fix your sleep" Evening light really does suppress melatonin, but the blue-blocker trials are null, and Cochrane found no trial evidence at all linking these lenses to melatonin levels
    "It's banned across Europe" Prescription-only in the UK, Australia and Switzerland, but legally sold as a low-dose supplement in Spain, Italy, France, Poland, Portugal and Belgium

    What you actually learn

    • The category error is the whole story. Melatonin was studied as a clock-shifter and sold as a sedative. Once you see that, the dose confusion, the timing confusion and the disappointment all follow.
    • Timing beats dose, and it is not close. The size of the clock shift tracks when you take it at r-squared 0.94, while doubling or sextupling the dose changes almost nothing.
    • Mistimed melatonin is worse than none. Morning dosing pushes your clock later, and the Cochrane jet-lag review warns it delays adaptation.
    • The best uses are narrow and real: jet lag, a genuinely delayed sleep phase, and non-24-hour rhythm in blind people, where it is the single strongest treatment available.
    • The scary claims and the real risk are different things. "It stops your own production" and "it delays puberty" do not hold up in humans. What does hold up is a measurable hit to glucose tolerance, and a product where half the bottles miss their label, usually by containing too much.
    • The strongest sleep lever in this whole topic is free: ordinary room light before bed delayed melatonin onset in 99 percent of people tested.

    What this means for you

    • If you sleep badly in general, melatonin is the wrong tool. The guideline-backed answer for chronic insomnia is cognitive behavioural therapy, and melatonin is specifically recommended against.
    • If you are crossing time zones or your body clock genuinely runs late, it is the right tool - and it works at a small dose.
    • Use 0.3 to 1 mg, not 5 or 10. Higher doses mostly extend the signal into your morning, which is the opposite of what you want.
    • Take it hours before your target bedtime for a clock shift, not minutes before, and never in the morning.
    • Buy only a USP Verified or NSF-certified product. Half of surveyed products miss their label and the usual error is too much, not too little.
    • Do not take it right before eating. Melatonin impairs glucose handling, sharply so if you carry the common MTNR1B variant.
    • Get the light right first. Dimming the room in the evening moves your own melatonin more reliably than a tablet does.

    TL;DR

    Melatonin is a timing signal, not a sedative12. As a sleeping pill its effect is real but trivial: 4 to 7 minutes off sleep-onset latency78, which is why the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends clinicians not use it for chronic insomnia11 and why three independent network meta-analyses rank it below the prescription hypnotics121314. As a chronobiotic it genuinely works: jet lag with a number needed to treat of 222, delayed sleep-wake phase disorder34, and non-24-hour rhythm in totally blind people36. The mechanism is timing - the melatonin phase-response curve runs about 12 hours out of phase with the light curve17, the phase advance tracks administration time at r-squared 0.9419, and mistimed melatonin shifts the clock the wrong way1822. Three popular claims are refuted: it does not suppress your own production7879, melatonin does not decline with healthy ageing80, and being "low in melatonin" did not predict who responds48. The real risks are not where people look: the FDA's own survey found products at 0% to 667% of label27, and melatonin measurably impairs glucose tolerance, concentrated in carriers of a common genetic variant5354.

    The picture, ranked by how strongly the evidence agrees

    • Settled: weak hypnotic (4-7 min), real chronobiotic, timing dominates dose, retail doses are supraphysiological, product labels are unreliable.
    • Well supported: jet lag, delayed sleep phase, blind non-24, larger effects in children but at very low certainty.
    • Contested: the glucose effect's long-term meaning, and every "no tolerance, no withdrawal" claim, which rests almost entirely on manufacturer-authored studies.
    • Refuted or preclinical: endogenous suppression, age-related decline, "low melatonin" predicting response, antioxidant and anti-cancer and longevity claims, and blue-blocking glasses fixing sleep.

    What this review covered¶

    Seven parallel evidence sweeps: foundations and pharmacology; sleep efficacy in insomnia and healthy sleepers; circadian uses (jet lag, shift work, delayed sleep phase, blindness); dose, timing, tolerance and dependence; safety, hormones and children; product quality and regulation; and the beyond-sleep claims. Every PubMed-indexed citation was then independently title-matched against the PubMed summary API before publication.

    Tier 1 - The strongest, settled evidence¶

    Melatonin is a darkness signal with two separable actions. A chronobiotic action that shifts and entrains the body clock, which is overwhelmingly timing-dependent, and a much weaker hypnotic action producing mild sleepiness and a small drop in core temperature12. Its production is gated by darkness under control of the body clock34, and its onset in dim light is the accepted marker of circadian phase5. Both actions run through the same two high-affinity receptors, MT1 and MT26. Marketing sells the second action while borrowing the credibility of the first.

    As a sleeping pill, the number is 4 to 7 minutes. Two independent meta-analyses agree: -4.0 minutes and -7.06 minutes of sleep-onset latency78. Total sleep time gains 8 to 13 minutes, with lower confidence bounds under 3 minutes87. Sleep efficiency moves about 2%, and one umbrella review found it not significant at all9. One independently funded meta-analysis shows the whole story inside a single dataset: -7.2 minutes for insomnia, but -38.8 minutes for delayed sleep phase syndrome10. The authors of the most-cited meta-analysis describe their own result as modest.

    The professional guideline recommends against it, and the head-to-head evidence agrees. The AASM suggests clinicians not use melatonin for sleep-onset or sleep-maintenance insomnia in adults, placing it alongside valerian and diphenhydramine11. The largest network meta-analysis, covering 154 trials and 44,089 participants and publicly funded, found melatonin "did not show overall material benefits"12, and two further independent network meta-analyses reach the same ranking1314. An age-stratified meta-analysis found it not significantly effective in adults at all15. Even the closest comparator drug, ramelteon, moves latency only 4.3 minutes16.

    Timing is the mechanism; dose is the wrong lever. The melatonin phase-response curve runs roughly 12 hours out of phase with the light curve17. For 3 mg, maximum clock advance comes about 5 hours before dim-light melatonin onset and maximum delay about 11 hours after it, with a dead zone across the first half of habitual sleep18. The size of the phase advance correlates with administration time at r-squared 0.94 while 0.3 mg and 3.0 mg do not separate19; in children, circadian timing predicted the effect and dose did not20; and 0.5 mg equals 3.0 mg when each is given at its own optimal time21.

    Mistimed melatonin makes things worse, so the worst case is not "nothing happens". Morning dosing falls in the delay region of the curve18, and the Cochrane review warns explicitly that early-day dosing causes sleepiness and delays adaptation22.

    Retail doses are supraphysiological by one to two orders of magnitude. Only 0.1-0.3 mg produces peaks inside the normal nocturnal range23, oral bioavailability is about 15% with a 45-minute half-life2425, and pharmacological doses measurably degrade vigilance while circulating26.

    The product is unreliable, and the dominant error is overdosing. The FDA's own laboratory survey of 110 products marketed to children found content spanning 0% to 667% of label, half outside an acceptable range, and doses reaching 50 mg per serving27. In US gummies, 88% were inaccurately labelled at 74-347% of label28. The original Canadian survey found -83% to +478% of label with up to 465% lot-to-lot variation inside a single product29.

    Evening light suppresses melatonin more easily than most people assume. Roughly half the suppressing effect of 9,000 lux bright light is reached at about 100 lux30, and ordinary room light before bed delayed melatonin onset in 99.0% of 116 people31.

    Tier 2 - Real, with limits¶

    Jet lag is the best-replicated use, with a number needed to treat of 222, confirmed by a newer meta-analysis showing similar benefit eastbound and westbound32 - so the folk rule that it only helps flying east is wrong, even though jet lag itself is worse eastward because the human clock runs at 24.18 hours33.

    Delayed sleep-wake phase disorder moves the clock about an hour while total sleep time does not change34 - the chronobiotic signature in one sentence. The largest single trial found a smaller effect and no between-group circadian shift35.

    Non-24-hour rhythm in totally blind people is the strongest indication of all: six of seven free-running subjects entrained36, at doses as low as 300 micrograms37. The prescription receptor agonist in this indication works but entrains only a minority, and its evidence is entirely manufacturer-funded38.

    Shift work is the weakest circadian use: about 24 extra minutes of daytime sleep, graded low quality, with no meaningful reduction in sleepiness39.

    Guidelines encode timing, not dose4041, and the same body that recommends against melatonin for insomnia endorses it, weakly, for circadian disorders1140.

    Children show larger effects at much lower certainty. Total sleep time +30 minutes and latency -18 minutes42 - three to four times the adult effect43 - but the independently funded panel graded certainty very low, found non-serious adverse events tripled, and concluded melatonin "should never be the first choice"42. An independent trial found children gained little additional sleep because they woke earlier too44. In autism the pooled effects are large but heterogeneity reaches 94%45, and in ADHD melatonin shifts sleep timing without improving behaviour, cognition or quality of life46.

    The regulator that approved it recorded a small effect: prolonged-release melatonin is licensed only for the over-55s, short-term, up to 13 weeks, and the manufacturer's own trials show a net benefit of about 11 minutes4748, with the independent meta finding even less49. Dose-response for sleep peaks near 4 mg and roughly 3 hours before bed may beat 30 minutes505152.

    Tier 3 - Contested, or resting on the manufacturer¶

    The glucose effect is the under-appreciated real risk. 5 mg taken before a glucose load raised the incremental glucose AUC by 186% in the morning53; the cleanest double-blind test localises this to carriers of the common MTNR1B diabetes-risk variant, in whom first-phase insulin secretion fell 40%54, and the variant magnifies the effect roughly sixfold55. The same physiology explains why late dinners harm carriers56. Two honest limits: every study is an acute challenge in about 20 people, and no trial has followed chronic users to a diabetes endpoint. The actionable variable is proximity to food.

    "No tolerance, no rebound, no withdrawal" is probably true and almost entirely manufacturer-authored4857. What does happen is relapse: stopping delayed sleep onset again in 92% of children and only 9% could stop entirely58 - the underlying problem returning, not a rebound above baseline59. No independent purpose-designed discontinuation trial exists60.

    Two drug interactions matter. Fluvoxamine raised melatonin exposure roughly 17-fold61, and in patients on nifedipine melatonin raised 24-hour blood pressure62.

    Adverse events are mild but poorly measured. Daytime sleepiness runs 1.66% across 37 RCTs63; at 10 mg or more minor events rise, though 37% of those trials reported no adverse-event data at all64, and roughly half of controlled studies report at least one significant adverse event65.

    Blood-pressure lowering does not survive independent re-analysis: the positive meta was manufacturer-co-authored66 and the independent version crosses zero, with no trial ever reporting a cardiovascular outcome67.

    Migraine prophylaxis is the best-supported non-sleep use, beating placebo and matching amitriptyline with better tolerability6869.

    Delirium splits by setting, and the split is a bias story. Post-operatively the receptor agonists help70, but in the ICU the definitive 847-patient blinded trial was flatly null71 and a bias-restricted meta gives a null result72.

    Paediatric ingestions rose 530% in a decade - but across 260,435 cases there were 5 ventilations and 2 deaths73, tracking availability and gummy formatting rather than a change in toxicity. Adult use roughly quintupled over two decades74. Prolonged-release tablets must not be crushed or quartered75.

    A 2026 cohort reporting more dementia among melatonin users should not be read as causal - its own authors say so, and melatonin is preferentially prescribed for REM-sleep behaviour disorder and circadian disruption, both prodromes of dementia and Parkinson's76. Classic reverse causation.

    Pregnancy data are thin rather than reassuring: no RCT has tested melatonin for sleep in pregnancy at all77.

    Tier 4 - Refuted, or preclinical only¶

    "It stops your body making its own" is refuted by every study that measured it. 0.5 mg for 7 days, and even 50 mg/day for 37 days, left endogenous amplitude unchanged78; 2 mg for 3-4 weeks phase-advanced secretion without suppressing it, and changed no measured hormone including testosterone79; and after 6-12 months of nightly use there was no suppression of endogenous production57. It moves your rhythm; it does not switch off your pineal gland.

    "Melatonin collapses with age" is refuted. In healthy drug-free 65-81-year-olds versus young adults, there was no difference on any measure80. What changes is the timing of the peak, not the amount. Relatedly, being "low in melatonin" did not predict who responds - age did48, and older normal sleepers with low melatonin gained nothing at any dose81.

    "More is better" fails for the clock, though the sleep picture is genuinely messier. A blind subject entrained on 0.5 mg and failed on 10 and 20 mg82, and 0.3 mg outperformed 3.0 mg on carryover8183. But the largest meta-regression found higher doses associated with larger sleep effects8, so there is no clean inverted-U for falling asleep - the real high-dose problems are carryover, hypothermia and failed entrainment.

    "It delays puberty" is not supported in humans at supplement doses. Up to 104 weeks left Tanner staging and growth within normal ranges59. The human LH-suppression study everyone cites used 300 mg/day, roughly 100 times a supplement dose84. One retrospective self-report survey hints at delay85 - a reason to keep watching, not a demonstrated harm.

    "Blue-blocking glasses fix your sleep" does not follow from the real physiology. Evening light genuinely suppresses melatonin3031, but a meta-analysis of double-blind crossover trials found latency and total sleep time both non-significant86, and Cochrane found no RCT evidence at all relating these lenses to serum melatonin87.

    The antioxidant, anti-cancer and longevity claims are preclinical, and that literature is unusually retraction-prone. No human longevity trial was found; the mechanism corpus is rodent and cell-line88, and PubMed flags 89 retracted melatonin-titled papers, including one retracted human RCT89. In humans melatonin moves inflammatory biomarkers and no clinical outcome90. The famous cancer-survival result comes from ten unblinded trials at a single hospital network91, and the one independent double-blind oncology trial was stopped for futility92. The IARC classification applies to shift work, not supplements93, and the prospective evidence across 1.4 million women is null94, though a premenopausal signal survives in retrospective data95. Melatonin did not reduce COVID-19 mortality96. The honest summary of the whole field is an umbrella review: 195 reviews, mostly low quality, only 7 meta-analyses significant at P<0.00197.

    Tart cherry is inconsistent: it raised urinary melatonin and helped in one 20-person crossover98, but the largest trial found no effect on sleep at all99.

    The regulatory picture is more nuanced than "banned in Europe". Melatonin is prescription-only in the UK, Australia and Switzerland, but legally sold as a low-dose supplement in Spain, Italy, France, Poland, Portugal and Belgium, and not permitted in Denmark, Germany, Sweden, Slovenia or Czechia100.

    Bottom line¶

    Weak hypnotic, real chronobiotic, badly labelled product. If melatonin is used, the evidence-aligned pattern is narrow: a low dose (0.3-1 mg, not 5-10), correctly timed (hours ahead for a phase shift, not minutes before bed), for a timing problem (jet lag, a genuinely delayed sleep phase) rather than ordinary insomnia, from a USP Verified or NSF product, and not immediately before eating. For ordinary adult insomnia the guideline-backed answer is cognitive behavioural therapy, and melatonin is specifically recommended against11.

    The full findings — all 44¶

    Every conclusion this review reached, grouped by how strongly the evidence backs it. Each finding is distilled from the fully-cited evidence above.

    Tier 1 - Settled, multi-study consensus (11 findings)

    1. Melatonin is a darkness signal, not a sedative, and that single distinction explains almost everything else.

    It has two separable actions: a chronobiotic action that shifts and entrains the body clock, which is timing-dependent, and a much weaker hypnotic action that produces mild sleepiness and a temperature drop. Nearly every consumer disappointment, dosing error and mistimed dose follows from treating the second as if it were the first.

    2. As a sleeping pill the measured effect is 4 to 7 minutes of sleep-onset latency.

    Two independent meta-analyses agree: -4.0 min (95% CI 2.5-5.4) and -7.06 min (95% CI 4.37-9.75). Total sleep time gains 8 to 13 minutes, with lower confidence bounds of 1.7 and 2.9 minutes. The effect is statistically real and clinically marginal, well below the ~20-minute threshold usually treated as meaningful in insomnia trials.

    3. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends AGAINST melatonin for chronic insomnia in adults.

    The guideline suggests clinicians not use it for sleep-onset or sleep-maintenance insomnia, grouping it with valerian, tryptophan and diphenhydramine, while ramelteon (a melatonin receptor agonist) received a weak recommendation for.

    4. Three independent network meta-analyses rank melatonin below the prescription hypnotics.

    The largest, covering 154 trials and 44,089 participants and publicly funded, concluded that "melatonin, ramelteon, and non-licensed drugs did not show overall material benefits", with benzodiazepines, eszopiclone, zolpidem and zopiclone more efficacious (SMD 0.27-0.71); two further independent network meta-analyses reach the same ranking. Three independent syntheses agreeing is as close to settled as this literature gets.

    5. Melatonin's genuine strength is circadian, not insomnia, and the same professional body says so in both directions.

    The AASM recommends against it for chronic insomnia while positively (if weakly) endorsing strategically timed melatonin for delayed sleep-wake phase disorder, non-24-hour rhythm in blind adults, and irregular rhythm in children with neurological disorders. This is not a contradiction - it is the chronobiotic/hypnotic distinction expressed as clinical policy.

    6. Timing is the mechanism, and the melatonin phase-response curve runs roughly 12 hours out of phase with the light phase-response curve.

    For 3 mg, maximum clock advance occurs about 5 hours before dim-light melatonin onset and maximum delay about 11 hours after it, just after habitual wake, with a dead zone across the first half of habitual sleep.

    7. Mistimed melatonin shifts the clock the wrong way - the worst case is not "nothing happens".

    Morning administration falls squarely in the delay region of the phase-response curve, and the Cochrane jet-lag review warns explicitly that melatonin taken early in the day causes sleepiness and delays adaptation. For someone whose sleep is already delayed, morning melatonin should be expected to make it worse.

    8. Oral melatonin has low and highly variable bioavailability - about 15%, ranging 9-33% - with a half-life near 45 minutes.

    A 100 mg oral dose produced a peak of 101,163 pg/mL against 72.1 pg/mL from 0.02 mg intravenously. This is why a large tablet does not reproduce the nocturnal signal: it overshoots it enormously and then clears.

    9. Retail doses of 3 to 10 mg are supraphysiological by roughly one to two orders of magnitude.

    Only 0.1-0.3 mg oral produces peak serum levels inside the normal nocturnal range; even 0.3 mg produced peaks around 220 pg/mL.

    10. Ordinary evening light suppresses melatonin far more easily than most people assume.

    Roughly half the melatonin-suppressing effect of ~9,000 lux bright light is reached at about 100 lux, and ordinary room light before bed delayed melatonin onset in 99.0% of 116 people and shortened melatonin duration by about 90 minutes. The behavioural lever here is real and free.

    11. Product content does not reliably match the label, and the dominant error is overdosing.

    The FDA's own laboratory survey of 110 products marketed to children found content spanning 0% to 667% of label, with half outside an acceptable range - 35 above label versus 20 below - and doses reaching 50 mg per serving. In US gummies, 22 of 25 (88%) were inaccurately labelled at 74-347% of label, with one product containing no detectable melatonin at all. The original Canadian survey found -83% to +478% of label and up to 465% lot-to-lot variation within a single product.

    Tier 2 - Well supported, with real limits (10 findings)

    12. Jet lag is melatonin's best-replicated indication, with a number needed to treat of 2.

    Nine of ten trials found benefit crossing five or more time zones, and the newest quantitative synthesis confirms it eastbound (SMD -0.76, 95% CI -1.06 to -0.45) and westbound (-0.66, -1.07 to -0.26). The honest caveat: the Cochrane review dates from 2002 and a 2008 updating search found no new trials.

    13. The popular "it only works flying east" rule is not supported.

    Jet lag is worse eastward, because the human clock runs at an intrinsic 24.18 hours and therefore drifts naturally later, but the meta-analysed melatonin benefit is similar in both directions.

    14. In delayed sleep-wake phase disorder melatonin moves the clock without adding sleep.

    It advanced endogenous melatonin onset by 1.18 h (95% CI 0.89-1.48) and sleep-onset clock time by 0.67 h, cutting sleep-onset latency by 23.27 min - while wake time and total sleep time did not change significantly. That pattern is the chronobiotic signature.

    15. Non-24-hour rhythm disorder in totally blind people is the single strongest indication.

    Six of seven free-running blind subjects entrained to 24.0 h (P<0.001) from a baseline mean period of 24.5 h, and entrainment works at genuinely physiological doses as low as 300 micrograms.

    16. Timing dominates dose, and this is measured, not asserted.

    In delayed sleep phase syndrome the size of the phase advance correlated with time of administration at r-squared 0.94 (P<0.0001) while 0.3 mg and 3.0 mg did not separate; in children, circadian time of administration predicted the effect and dose did not; and in matched phase-response curves, 0.5 mg and 3.0 mg produced similarly sized shifts when each was given at its own optimal time.

    17. For the sleep effect, dose-response peaks around 4 mg per day and taking it earlier probably beats taking it later.

    A dose-response meta-analysis of 26 RCTs found the effect peaking near 4 mg and timing significantly predicting sleep-onset latency, suggesting roughly 3 hours before desired bedtime rather than the conventional 30 minutes; for jet lag, doses above 5 mg were no more effective than 0.5-5 mg.

    18. The effect is larger in children than adults, but the certainty is graded very low and the panel that produced the numbers said not to reach for it first.

    Total sleep time +30.33 min and sleep-onset latency -18.03 min, both with zero heterogeneity - three to four times the adult effect. The same independently funded review graded certainty very low, noted the outcomes were sleep-diary rather than objective, found non-serious adverse events tripled (RR 3.44, 95% CI 1.25-9.42), and concluded melatonin "should never be the first choice". Two further reviews independently reproduce the children-over-adults pattern.

    19. In adults with chronic insomnia specifically, one good age-stratified meta-analysis found melatonin not significantly effective at all

    for sleep-onset latency, total sleep time or sleep efficiency.

    20. The regulator that approved melatonin recorded that its effect is small.

    Prolonged-release melatonin is authorised in the EU only for primary insomnia in patients aged 55 and over, short-term, up to 13 weeks - and the CHMP stated it "has only been shown to have a small effect in a relatively small number of patients": 32% responders versus 19% on placebo [R1]. Approval documented the small effect rather than contradicting it.

    21. Objective and subjective measurements agree, so this is not a case of "feels better, measures worse".

    Prolonged-release melatonin gave subjective sleep-onset latency -6.30 min alongside objective -5.05 min. The effect is small on both.

    Tier 3 - Contested, single-source, or industry-dependent (9 findings)

    22. Melatonin measurably impairs glucose tolerance, and this is the most under-appreciated real risk.

    A 5 mg dose taken 15 minutes before a glucose load raised the incremental glucose AUC by 186% in the morning and 54% in the evening. The cleanest test - a double-blind placebo-controlled crossover - localises the effect to carriers of the common MTNR1B diabetes risk variant, in whom glucose tolerance worsened 11.7% and first-phase beta-cell responsivity fell 40%; the variant magnifies the effect roughly sixfold and explains about 26% of between-person variance. The same physiology explains why late dinners harm carriers. Honest limits: every human study is an acute challenge in about 20 people, and no trial has followed chronic nightly users to a diabetes endpoint.

    23. The actionable variable is melatonin's proximity to food, not the dose alone

    • the trial authors' own conclusion. No trial has yet tested whether separating melatonin from eating prevents the effect.

    24. At 10 mg and above, minor adverse events rise measurably while serious ones do not.

    RR 1.40 (p<0.001) for drowsiness, headache and dizziness across 79 studies and 3,861 participants - but only 4 studies were at low risk of bias and 37% reported no adverse-event data at all. Roughly half of controlled studies report at least one significant adverse event, most often fatigue, mood change and psychomotor impairment.

    25. The entire "no tolerance, no rebound, no withdrawal" evidence base is manufacturer-authored.

    Six-month, 6-12 month, 52-week and 104-week studies all report no efficacy loss and no withdrawal syndrome, and every one was authored by staff of the company making the prolonged-release product, with three of four open-label and lacking a placebo arm. Independent corroboration exists but is indirect: a meta-regression found longer trials showed larger effects, and academic follow-up cohorts agree in direction. No independent purpose-designed placebo-controlled discontinuation trial exists.

    26. Relapse on stopping is real and is not the same thing as dependence.

    In children followed a mean 3.7 years, 65% were still using daily, temporary discontinuation delayed sleep onset again in 92%, and only 9% could stop entirely. But after two years of treatment, sleep during a blinded two-week withdrawal was still better than pre-treatment baseline - the underlying problem returning, not a rebound above baseline.

    27. There is one genuinely serious drug interaction, and one counterintuitive cardiovascular one.

    Fluvoxamine raised oral melatonin AUC roughly 17-fold via CYP1A2 inhibition, an effect specific enough that citalopram does not share it. Separately, in hypertensive patients well controlled on nifedipine, melatonin raised 24-hour blood pressure by 6.5/4.9 mmHg - the opposite of its usual direction.

    28. Blood-pressure lowering does not survive independent re-analysis.

    The positive meta-analysis was co-authored by manufacturer staff and found the all-trials pool non-significant, with only a controlled-release subgroup positive. The independent re-analysis found asleep systolic -3.57 mmHg with a confidence interval crossing zero, from four trials of which one was at low risk of bias, at low-to-very-low certainty, and no trial has ever reported a cardiovascular outcome.

    29. Migraine prophylaxis is melatonin's best-supported non-sleep use.

    A 196-patient three-arm RCT found melatonin 3 mg cut headache days by 2.7 versus 2.2 for amitriptyline and 1.1 for placebo (P=0.009 versus placebo), with better tolerability than amitriptyline; a nine-RCT meta-analysis agrees (-1.54 headache days, responder RR 1.38).

    30. Delirium prevention splits cleanly by setting, and the split is a bias story.

    Post-operatively, melatonin receptor agonists gave OR 0.54 across 158 trials and 41,084 participants, though rated moderate-to-very-low quality and behind dexmedetomidine. In the ICU, the definitive 847-patient blinded trial was flatly null on delirium, mortality and sleep, and a meta restricted to low-risk-of-bias trials gives RR 0.89 (0.73-1.09). Benefit appears when high-risk-of-bias trials are included and disappears when they are excluded.

    Tier 4 - Refuted, or preclinical-only (9 findings)

    31. "Taking melatonin stops your body making its own" is refuted by every study that has actually measured it.

    0.5 mg nightly for 7 days left the amplitude of endogenous secretion unchanged, and 50 mg/day for 37 days produced no change in the endogenous profile; 2 mg for 3-4 weeks phase-advanced endogenous secretion by 1-3 hours without suppressing it; and urinary 6-sulfatoxymelatonin after 6-12 months of nightly use showed no suppression. It moves your rhythm; it does not switch off your pineal gland. Honest limit, stated plainly: the direct evidence is roughly 34 subjects across two studies, and nobody has tested nightly 10 mg for years.

    32. "Melatonin collapses with age, so older people should replace it" is refuted.

    In healthy drug-free adults aged 65-81 versus healthy young adults under constant-routine conditions, there was no difference on any measure: 24-hour mean 70 versus 73 pmol/L (P=0.97), nocturnal peak 162 versus 161 pmol/L (P=0.63), integrated area 85,800 versus 86,700 (P=0.66). What changes with age is the timing of the peak, not the amount.

    33. "I'm low in melatonin so I should supplement" is refuted by the one large trial that tested it.

    In 791 patients, those with low endogenous melatonin did not respond better than placebo; age predicted response, melatonin level did not. Older normal sleepers with low melatonin gained nothing at any dose.

    34. "More is better" is refuted for the clock, but the picture for sleep is genuinely messier and the honest version matters.

    For entrainment, a blind subject entrained on 0.5 mg and failed on 10 mg and 20 mg, the authors' interpretation being that excess melatonin spills onto the wrong zone of the phase-response curve. For sleep, 0.3 mg restored sleep efficiency while 3.0 mg caused hypothermia and left melatonin elevated into daylight - but the largest meta-regression found higher doses associated with larger sleep effects. So there is no clean inverted-U for falling asleep; the real high-dose problems are carryover, thermoregulation and failed entrainment.

    35. "It delays puberty in children" is not supported in humans at supplement doses.

    Up to 104 weeks of prolonged-release melatonin in 80 children aged 2-17.5 left Tanner staging, height, weight and BMI within normal ranges. The fear is extrapolated from seasonal-breeding animals where melatonin gates reproduction. The one human study showing LH suppression used 300 mg/day, roughly 100 times a supplement dose, in a contraceptive-development programme - citing it as a risk of a 3 mg gummy is a dose-level category error. The honest counterweight: one retrospective self-report survey after a mean 7.1 years suggested a tendency toward delayed puberty, which is a reason to keep watching, not a demonstrated harm.

    36. "Blue-blocking glasses fix your sleep" does not follow from the real physiology.

    Evening light genuinely suppresses melatonin, but a meta-analysis of the double-blind crossover trials found sleep-onset latency -4.86 min (95% CI -20.23 to 10.52, p=0.54) and total sleep time +8.75 min (-35.31 to 52.82, p=0.70), both non-significant. Cochrane found sleep-quality effects indeterminate and no RCT evidence at all relating these lenses to serum melatonin; the famous positive trial had 14 participants.

    37. The antioxidant, anti-cancer and longevity claim families are preclinical - and that preclinical corpus carries an unusual integrity burden.

    No human trial of melatonin for longevity or healthspan was found. PubMed flags 89 papers with "melatonin" in the title as Retracted Publication and 21 with an Expression of Concern, with J Pineal Res alone carrying 22 retractions and 16 EoCs, almost entirely preclinical antioxidant, mitochondrial and anti-cancer mechanism papers. One human melatonin oxidative-stress RCT was retracted in 2025, and the meta-analysis pooling that literature was co-authored by the same advocacy group and found no TNF-alpha effect while concluding favourably. In humans melatonin moves inflammatory biomarkers and no clinical outcome.

    38. The cancer-survival claim does not survive scrutiny.

    The famous 34% one-year mortality reduction comes from ten unblinded trials conducted at a single hospital network - the authors say so verbatim - with implausibly perfect homogeneity. The one independent double-blind placebo-controlled melatonin oncology trial was stopped for futility. Separately, IARC's Group 2A classification applies to shift work, not supplements, and the prospective evidence on night work and breast cancer is null across 1.4 million women (RR 0.99), though a premenopausal signal survives in retrospective data.

    39. Melatonin did not reduce COVID-19 mortality in the most rigorous synthesis

    (RR 0.72, 95% CI 0.47-1.11, I-squared 82%, low certainty), with recovery and every inflammatory marker non-significant; the dramatic positive result came from an advocacy-co-authored meta-analysis.

    Practical — what to actually do (5 findings)

    40. If melatonin is used, use a low dose: roughly 0.3-1 mg, not 5-10 mg.

    Only 0.1-0.3 mg produces peaks inside the normal nocturnal range, and in the cleanest head-to-head 0.3 mg restored sleep efficiency while 3.0 mg caused hypothermia and left melatonin elevated into the daylight hours. Guidance for older adults is 0.3-2 mg immediate-release. Higher doses do not buy a bigger clock shift.

    41. Take it hours before target bedtime for a phase shift, not minutes before.

    Bedtime dosing sits in the phase-shifting dead zone, the dose-response analysis suggests roughly 3 hours before desired bedtime rather than the conventional 30 minutes, and mistimed melatonin actively shifts the clock the wrong way. Never take it in the morning.

    42. Use it for a timing problem, not for ordinary insomnia.

    Jet lag, a genuinely delayed sleep phase and non-24 in blind people are where melatonin earns its place; for chronic adult insomnia the AASM recommends against it and the guideline-backed alternative is cognitive behavioural therapy.

    43. Buy only a USP Verified or NSF-certified product.

    Content variability did not correlate with manufacturer or product type, the FDA's own survey found half of 110 children's products out of specification with doses to 50 mg, and 88% of gummies were mislabelled. Third-party certification is the only buyer-side lever. Prolonged-release tablets must not be crushed or quartered.

    44. Do not take it immediately before eating, and fix the light first.

    Melatonin measurably impairs glucose tolerance, concentrated in MTNR1B carriers, so proximity to food is the actionable variable. And the largest free lever in this whole topic is light: ordinary room light before bed delayed melatonin onset in 99.0% of 116 people and shortened its duration by ~90 minutes.
