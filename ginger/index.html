    Ginger: What It Actually Does vs What People Think¶

    A food-dose gut agent with a modest, timing-dependent antiemetic and analgesic record and no demonstrated systemic mechanism: free gingerols are undetectable in blood after 2 g of extract, 100- to 1,000-fold below the enzyme concentrations the "natural ibuprofen" story needs. About 1 g/day eases pregnancy nausea (not vomiting) and pre-loaded chemotherapy vomiting; pain and soreness effects are small; testosterone, fat-loss and blood-thinning claims have no controlled human evidence; heartburn is the one consistent cost.

    Literature reviewed to 18 October 2026. 228 sources, every identifier resolved against PubMed.

    Start here¶

    Bottom line

    Ginger is a food-dose gut agent with a modest, timing-dependent record against nausea and a small analgesic effect, and no demonstrated systemic mechanism: free gingerols are undetectable in blood after even 2 g of extract, 100- to 1,000-fold below the concentrations the "natural ibuprofen" story needs, so the marketed anti-inflammatory, hormonal, metabolic and blood-thinning claims rest on test tubes, rodents and uncontrolled studies rather than on people.

    Myths vs. reality

    The claim you've heard What the evidence actually shows
    "Ginger is a natural ibuprofen that blocks COX in your blood" After 2 g of a 5 % extract, free 6-gingerol is undetectable and free 10-gingerol and 6-shogaol peak near 10 ng/mL (about 0.05 µM); COX-2 IC50s are 7.5 to 32 µM. In patients, gastric mucosal PGE2 rose on ginger extract where it fell on diclofenac. The mechanism has not been shown in a person.
    "Ginger is as effective as sumatriptan for migraine" One 100-patient trial with no placebo arm, no non-inferiority margin and no effect sizes in its abstract; the 2026 GRADE synthesis rates it "very uncertain". The only placebo-controlled prophylaxis RCT (600 mg/day, 3 months) was negative.
    "Ginger raises testosterone by 17 %" No randomized placebo-controlled trial of ginger on testosterone exists in men. The 17.7 % figure is an open-label pre/post change in 75 men with upper-normal baseline testosterone, no placebo and no stated dose, in a journal PubMed does not index.
    "Ginger thins the blood like aspirin" At 1 to 4 g/day of dried powder, no controlled study changed platelet aggregation, bleeding time or INR, including a warfarin pharmacokinetic trial. The signal comes from single 10 g doses, litres of tea, self-reported bleeding and case reports without rechallenge.
    "Ginger stops vomiting in pregnancy" About 1 g/day lowers nausea intensity (MD 1.20 VAS points, I² = 0 %) but has no reliable effect on vomiting episodes, and it is equivalent to vitamin B6. A 68,522-woman cohort shows no malformation or perinatal signal.
    "The big chemo trials proved ginger does nothing" The largest trial (576 analysed) was positive for acute nausea when 0.5 to 1 g/day was started three days before chemotherapy; two well-run trials starting 1 to 2 g on the chemo day were null. Pooled, the reproducible effect is less acute vomiting at ≤1 g/day for more than 3 days.
    "Take ginger before your workout and it will hurt less" Every single-dose trial is null for pain, effort, VO2 and endurance. The positive data require 2 g/day for 5 to 11 days before the damaging session, cut next-day soreness by about a quarter in one laboratory's trials, and never lowered muscle-damage enzymes.
    "Ginger lowers blood sugar like a drug and melts fat" In type 2 diabetes the pooled Iranian trials give HbA1c −0.4 to −1.0 points; the one risk-of-bias-filtered pool and the largest single trial found nothing. Weight falls 1 to 1.5 kg in one pool and not at all in the larger one; "thermogenesis" is 43 kcal/day in a ten-man meal test.
    "Fresh ginger is stronger than dried, and heating makes it stronger" Dried powder holds 4.4 to 11.3 mg/g of actives against 1.1 to 1.5 mg/g in fresh rhizome. In the only human comparison, heated ginger gave 23 % and raw 25 % soreness reduction.
    "4 g/day is the safe upper limit set by the WHO" The figure is the top of the German Commission E's 1990 therapeutic range, not a toxicological limit. The 2025 EU monograph sets 1 to 2 g before travel and a maximum of 2.5 g/day; no human NOAEL exists.

    What you actually learn

    • Pharmacokinetics settle most of the argument: gingerols and shogaols are glucuronidated on first pass, circulate as conjugates with 1 to 3 h half-lives, never accumulate, and reach free concentrations 100- to 1,000-fold below every enzyme IC50 in the mechanism story.
    • Nausea is the one bounded, reproducible use: about 1 g/day for pregnancy nausea (not vomiting), 0.5 to 1 g/day pre-loaded before chemotherapy for acute vomiting, 1 g or more before surgery for nausea scores; more than 1 g did less, and timing mattered more than dose.
    • The gut mechanism is real and small: 1.2 g halves liquid gastric half-emptying on ultrasound without relieving dyspepsia, the constituents inhibit the 5-HT3 receptor non-competitively at a site distinct from ondansetron's, and the one human intermediate tied to nausea is blunted gastric tachygastria and vasopressin release.
    • Pain effects are small and the comparators are weak: dysmenorrhea improves about 1.5 cm on a 10 cm scale against placebo in small Iranian trials; osteoarthritis pain by SMD −0.30 with twice the GI withdrawals; "equal to NSAIDs" and "equal to sumatriptan" come from trials with no placebo arm and no non-inferiority design.
    • Exercise soreness is analgesia, not less damage: eleven days of 2 g/day cut 24 h soreness by 23 to 25 % in one laboratory with borderline p-values; creatine kinase never fell and once rose longer with ginger; acute doses, heating and endurance performance are null.
    • Cardiometabolic effects are real in direction and weak in certainty: CRP −0.4 to −1.0 mg/L in sick cohorts with no healthy-adult trial, triglycerides −9 to −25 mg/dL, blood pressure −5/−2 mmHg only where it was elevated, HbA1c positive in Iranian pools and null in the risk-of-bias-filtered pool.
    • Hormones have no controlled human evidence: the rodent testosterone literature is toxicant rescue at human-equivalent doses of 0.5 to 11 g/day of extract; the one RCT in men found lower sperm DNA fragmentation, no change in count, and motility trending worse.
    • The one consistent harm is heartburn: GI adverse effects are common (1 to 10 %), dose-related, grade 1 at 2 g, and doubled withdrawals in osteoarthritis trials; a daily shot holds at most 35 to 90 mg of actives, a food-range dose with no measurable bleeding, metabolic or hormonal effect.

    What this means for you

    • Use ginger for what it does: nausea, sluggish gastric emptying, period pain and post-exercise soreness, at 0.5 to 1 g/day of dried powder for nausea and 1.5 to 2 g/day for the pain and soreness protocols; expect small effects.
    • Time it, don't dose it: take it one hour before travel, three days before chemotherapy, and for 5 to 11 days before a hard training block; a single dose on the day does nothing measurable.
    • Do not expect anti-inflammatory, hormonal or metabolic effects: no controlled trial supports testosterone, fat-loss or drug-level blood-sugar claims, and the biomarker effects seen in sick cohorts have no healthy-adult trial behind them.
    • Stop worrying about bleeding at normal doses: 1 to 4 g/day of powder had no platelet or INR effect in any controlled study; pause supplements before surgery as a cost-free precaution, and avoid litres of ginger tea if you take a direct oral anticoagulant.
    • Buy dried powder in capsules, not promises: capsule powder carried every positive trial, ginger in tea did not change glycemia, supplements vary 10-fold between brands, and no trial exists for ginger ale, candied ginger or lozenges.
    • Treat a morning shot as a food: it holds at most tens of milligrams of actives, comparable to 1 to 2 g of powder; keep it if you like it and drop it if it gives you heartburn, the one cost the trials consistently record.
    • Pregnancy and drugs: about 1 g/day is reassuring for malformations in 68,522 women while early-loss precision is poor and the EU monograph still says "preferable to avoid"; expect additive effects with antidiabetic and antihypertensive drugs at 2 g/day, and do not combine with aprepitant without noting the one trial where nausea worsened.

    1. Executive Summary and Chemistry¶

    Ginger rhizome is a food, a spice, a traditional medicine and, in the last fifteen years, a supplement sold on seven overlapping promises: that it is a "natural ibuprofen", a cure for nausea of every origin, a migraine drug as good as sumatriptan, a period-pain remedy as good as NSAIDs, a muscle-recovery aid, a metabolic agent that lowers blood sugar and melts fat, and a testosterone booster. A daily ginger shot sits in Patryk's own morning routine on the strength of a diffuse version of these claims.

    The human literature behind them is large in count and narrow in shape. PubMed holds more than a hundred randomized trials of ginger, about 36 meta-analyses of its cardiometabolic effects alone, and three umbrella reviews. Most trials enrolled 20 to 90 people for 4 to 12 weeks, dosed 0.5 to 3 g/day of dried rhizome powder in capsules, and were run in a small number of Iranian university centres 1 2 3. Two exceptions anchor the evidence: a multicentre US chemotherapy trial of 576 analysed patients 4, and a Norwegian pregnancy cohort of 68,522 women 5. Everything else needs to be read with the knowledge that the positive signal comes from one research culture, one supplement form and one set of laboratories.

    What ginger is, chemically. The pungent principles are the gingerols, a homologous series of phenolic β-hydroxy-ketones named by side-chain length (6-, 8-, 10-gingerol), with 6-gingerol the most abundant in fresh rhizome. On drying, heating or acid exposure the gingerols dehydrate reversibly to the corresponding shogaols; retro-aldol cleavage yields zingerone 6 7. Fresh rhizome holds about 1.1 to 1.5 mg/g of total gingerols plus shogaols; dried powder 4.4 to 11.3 mg/g; dietary supplements anywhere from 2.9 to 30 mg/g 8. A 1 g capsule of powder, the typical trial dose, therefore delivers single-digit milligrams of actives; a 2 g dose of a 5 % standardized extract, the dose used in the pharmacokinetic studies, delivers about 100 mg.

    What the body does with it. Gingerols and 6-shogaol are absorbed within an hour and immediately glucuronidated and sulfated. After the maximal 2 g extract dose, free 6-gingerol and 8-gingerol are undetectable at every time point; free 10-gingerol peaks at 9.5 ng/mL and free 6-shogaol at 13.6 ng/mL; the glucuronides circulate at 0.17 to 0.85 µg/mL; half-lives are 1 to 3 h and nothing accumulates with daily dosing 9 10. The purified compounds inhibit COX-2 in a dish at 7.5 to 32 µM 11. The free aglycone in a human vein peaks near 0.05 µM. That 100- to 1,000-fold gap is the single most important fact in this dossier, because it means the mechanism that the marketing describes has never been shown to operate in a person, and whatever ginger does in trials, it does by a route not yet identified.

    What the trials show, in one paragraph. Nausea of pregnancy improves modestly versus placebo at about 1 g/day (nausea, not vomiting), equivalent to vitamin B6 12 13. Chemotherapy vomiting falls when 0.5 to 1 g/day is started before the infusion, and not otherwise 4 14. Postoperative nausea scores fall; vomiting is inconsistent 15 16. Period pain falls by about 1.5 cm on a 10 cm scale against placebo in small Iranian trials 17. Osteoarthritis pain falls by a third of a standard deviation, with twice the dropouts 18. Soreness after unaccustomed exercise falls by about a quarter after 11 days of pre-loading in one laboratory's trials, without any change in muscle-damage enzymes 19 20. Fasting glucose and HbA1c fall in pooled Iranian diabetes trials and do not fall in the one risk-of-bias-filtered pool or in the largest single trial 21 22 23. Triglycerides fall; cholesterol barely moves; blood pressure falls 5/2 mmHg only in people whose pressure was high 24 25. CRP falls by less than 1 mg/L in sick cohorts and has never been tested in healthy adults 26. Migraine prophylaxis failed; "as good as sumatriptan" rests on a trial with no placebo 27 28. No controlled trial of ginger on testosterone in men exists at all 29. At 1 to 4 g/day it does not thin the blood in any controlled study 30 31. Its one consistent harm is heartburn and belching 9.

    The rest of this report walks through each cluster, states the numbers as the abstracts give them, and marks where the evidence is thin, contested or absent.

    2. Composition, Pharmacokinetics and Dose Conventions¶

    2.1 Raw, dried, heated and extracted ginger are different products¶

    The gingerol-to-shogaol dehydration is reversible, pH-dependent (most stable at pH 4) and reaches equilibrium within about 2 h at 100 °C and pH 1 6. GC-MS of commercially processed dry ginger found gingerol concentrations "slightly" lower and shogaols higher than in fresh rhizome 7. Because dried powder has lost 80 to 90 % of its water, it is 3 to 8 times richer per gram in actives than fresh root; per original rhizome mass the two are roughly a wash 8. The popular claim that "fresh is stronger" is therefore false per gram and meaningless per root.

    The supplement market is not standardized. In ten US ginger-root products, 6-gingerol ranged from 0.0 to 9.43 mg/g (mean 2.56 ± 2.95), 6-shogaol from 0.16 to 2.18 mg/g, and suggested daily servings from 250 mg to 4.77 g; two products contained no detectable 6-gingerol 32. Coefficients of variation across nine products were 115 % for 6-gingerol and 142 % for 10-gingerol 33. The 6-shogaol to 6-gingerol ratio is a usable marker of heat exposure and age. The one cancer-supportive-care trial that dosed by analysed content used a standardized product delivering 84 mg/day of gingerols plus shogaols from 1.2 g of powder, about 7 % 34.

    The ginger shot. No peer-reviewed assay of a commercial ginger shot was found. If a 30 to 60 mL shot carried the entire gingerol content of 30 to 60 g of fresh rhizome it would hold at most about 35 to 90 mg of actives; actual juice transfer and shelf conversion to shogaols are unmeasured. Two volunteers drinking ginger tea reached plasma 6-gingerol of 42 nmol/L, about 12 ng/mL, at 30 to 60 min 35. The practical reading is that a shot is a food-range dose, in the same order as a 1 to 2 g capsule, with unlabeled and variable content.

    Gingerols are metabolically unstable with high intrinsic clearance in human liver microsomes; in mice, β-glucuronidase treatment of samples raised measured exposure 10- to 700-fold, which identifies first-pass glucuronidation as the reason for the low free levels 36. The responsible enzymes are UGT1A9 and UGT2B7 in liver and UGT1A8 in gut, with 4'-O-glucuronidation favoured 3- to 20-fold over 5-O 37. The apparent disagreement between the two human studies on whether any free compound circulates is an assay-sensitivity artefact: a 100 ng/mL detection limit versus 2 to 5 ng/mL.

    Two consequences follow. First, dose-exposure linearity is not established: the dose-escalation study reported Cmax and AUC only for the 2 g level, and sulfate conjugates appeared only above 1 g. Second, most RCTs used 0.5 to 2 g/day of plain powder, which delivers 5 to 20 times less active compound than the 2 g standardized extract of the pharmacokinetic studies; plasma levels at those food-type doses have never been measured except in the two-volunteer tea pilot.

    2.4 Dose conventions and the regulatory record¶

    The figure "4 g/day" is repeated across consumer sites and reviews as a safe upper limit, sometimes attributed to the WHO or the NIH. Its traceable origin is the German Commission E monograph of 1990, which described 2 to 4 g/day for dyspepsia and motion sickness; the EMA assessment report relays this (EMA/HMPC/765220/2022, p. 9). It is the top of a therapeutic range, not a toxicological ceiling. The NIH has no Office of Dietary Supplements fact sheet on ginger; the NCCIH page lists abdominal discomfort, heartburn, diarrhoea and mouth irritation and gives no dose limit (https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ginger). The WHO 1999 monograph could not be accessed for this report and no number is attributed to it.

    The current reference is the EU herbal monograph, Revision 1, adopted 7 May 2025 (https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/medicines/herbal/zingiberis-rhizoma):

    Use Preparation Posology
    Well-established use: prevention of nausea and vomiting in motion sickness powdered rhizome Adults 1 to 2 g, one hour before travel, single use; not recommended under 18
    Traditional: motion sickness powder Adolescents and adults 500 to 750 mg 30 min before travel; children 6 to 12 years 250 to 500 mg; repeat every 4 h if travel exceeds 4 h; maximum 2.5 g/day (1.5 g/day for 6 to 12 years)
    Traditional: mild spasmodic GI complaints, bloating, flatulence powder, tinctures 0.18 to 1 g three times daily; adults only; see a doctor if symptoms persist beyond 2 weeks
    Traditional: loss of appetite, minor articular pain, common cold powder, tinctures 0.25 to 1 g three times daily (0.75 to 3 g/day); adults only

    Its safety sections: contraindication is hypersensitivity only; no gallstone or anticoagulant warning; interactions "none known"; pregnancy "a moderate amount of data on pregnant women (between 300 and 1,000 pregnancy outcomes) indicates no malformative or feto/neonatal toxicity, as a precautionary measure it is preferable to avoid the use during pregnancy"; lactation "not recommended" for lack of data; GI disorders (stomach upset, eructation, dyspepsia, heartburn, nausea) common (1 to 10 %); no overdose case reported; rodent studies showed increased embryo resorption at doses slightly above to a few times the human dose. The German and Dutch national labels, by contrast, retain a gallstone consultation line, a bleeding-tendency warning, a stop-before-surgery instruction and an anticoagulant-monitoring note; the harmonized EU text adopted none of them after the assessor judged the bleeding data "not sufficient to include a warning".

    Where tolerability was actually tested: single doses to 2 g of extract 9; 2 g/day of powder for 12 weeks 42; 4 g/day for 3 months and a single 10 g dose 43; 15 g of raw root or 40 g of cooked stem ginger daily for 2 weeks 44. None reported serious toxicity. No human study defines a no-observed-adverse-effect level.

    3. Anti-Inflammatory Mechanisms versus Human Biomarkers¶

    3.1 The enzyme biochemistry is real, at micromolar concentrations¶

    Seventeen ginger pungent principles and analogues inhibited COX-2 in intact cells with IC50 values of 1 to 25 µM, with [8]-paradol and [8]-shogaol the strongest 45. Ultrafiltration mass spectrometry pulled ten COX-2 ligands out of a chloroform fraction (10- and 12-gingerol, 8- and 10-shogaol, 6-, 8- and 10-gingerdione, 6-dehydro-10-gingerol, 6- and 8-paradol); purified 10-gingerol, 8-shogaol and 10-shogaol inhibited COX-2 with IC50 32, 17.5 and 7.5 µM, and none inhibited COX-1 11. In LPS-stimulated U937 cells, gingerol-rich fractions suppressed COX-2 expression while shogaol-rich fractions blocked PGE2 without touching expression, implying direct enzyme inhibition, and were cytotoxic above 20 µg/mL 41. Fractions rich in gingerols from fresh rhizome inhibited LPS-induced PGE2 production 46.

    COX-1 inhibition is compound-specific. In human whole blood, [8]-gingerol, [8]-shogaol, [8]-paradol and two analogues inhibited arachidonic-acid-induced platelet aggregation with IC50 3 to 7 µM (aspirin 20 µM in the same assay), and [8]-paradol inhibited ovine COX-1 at 4 µM 47. "Dual COX-1/COX-2 inhibition" is therefore defensible for the paradol and 8-series and not as a blanket statement for ginger; the major constituents in the van Breemen assay did not inhibit COX-1 at all.

    5-lipoxygenase: gingerdione inhibited 5-HETE formation in intact human leukocytes at IC50 15 µM and PGE2 at 18 µM, the origin of the "dual inhibitor of arachidonic acid metabolism" phrase 48; gingerols and diarylheptanoids inhibited prostaglandin synthetase and RBL-1 5-LOX 49. The recent "dual COX/LOX" spice papers are docking studies and are not relied on.

    NF-κB: the mechanism is documented for 6-shogaol. In RAW 264.7 macrophages it blocked LPS-induced iNOS and COX-2 by preventing IκBα phosphorylation and p65 nuclear translocation and inhibited PI3K/Akt and ERK1/2 50; in microglia it reduced NO, PGE2, IL-1β and TNF-α and down-regulated COX-2, p38 and NF-κB, outperforming 6-gingerol 51. Zingerone, often listed beside the gingerols, has essentially no evidence class of its own: PubMed holds only rat chemical-toxicity models (arsenite, vancomycin, methotrexate, cisplatin) in which zingerone pre-treatment lowered NF-κB and TNF-α 52; no cell IC50 and no human data exist, and it is a minor thermal-degradation product in raw rhizome.

    One 2026 mouse study closes the loop in an interesting way: germ-free mice do not metabolize 6-shogaol, the most potent microbial-metabolite fecal fraction cut NO by 75 %, COX-2 by 64 % and NF-κB p65 activation by 67 % in macrophages, and intrarectal metabolites lowered TNF-α and IL-6 in LPS-treated mice as much as or more than 6-shogaol itself 40. If ginger works in people, this is the kind of route by which it could, but it is one mouse study.

    3.2 What people reach in plasma¶

    Section 2.2 gives the numbers. Free aglycones after a maximal supplement dose sit 150- to 600-fold below the lowest COX-2 IC50 measured for any purified ginger compound. The "6-gingerol blocks COX-2 in your blood" story fails on two counts at once: free 6-gingerol is undetectable, and purified 6-gingerol was not among the COX-2 inhibitors the ultrafiltration assay identified 11 10.

These are the only human tissue-level tests of the COX/LOX hypothesis, and neither passed cleanly.

    3.4 Head-to-head against NSAIDs¶

    Every head-to-head trial measured pain, not biomarkers. Dysmenorrhea: 250 mg of ginger four times daily was indistinguishable from 250 mg mefenamic acid and 400 mg ibuprofen on the same schedule in 150 students, but allocation was "alternate" and there was no placebo arm, so "all work" cannot be separated from "all placebo-level in this design" 64; against mefenamic acid in 122 students, ginger ran numerically 4 to 5 VAS points worse in both cycles and lengthened menstruation 65; against the combination analgesic Novafen in a 168-student crossover, no difference 66. Pooled: ginger versus placebo 1.55 cm on a 10 cm VAS, ginger versus mefenamic acid SMD 0 (95 % CI −0.40 to 0.41) from two trials 67.

    Osteoarthritis: in 43 patients, 340 mg/day of a standardized ginger extract and 100 mg/day diclofenac, both with glucosamine, relieved pain equally over 4 weeks; gastric mucosal PGE1, PGE2 and PGF2α rose on ginger and fell on diclofenac, with endoscopic mucosal degeneration only on diclofenac 68. An open-label 60-patient trial of diclofenac, ginger 750 mg or both found all three arms improved, the combination numerically best 69.

    The gastric-prostaglandin divergence is the best human evidence against the "ginger is an oral NSAID" model: whatever ginger does, it does not do it by suppressing constitutive gastric COX-1 the way diclofenac does. Combined with the undetectable free aglycone, the honest position is that a modest biomarker effect exists in sick cohorts, its mechanism in humans is not established, and the head-to-head equivalence claims rest on small trials without placebo arms.

    4. Gastrointestinal Motility, Nausea and Reflux¶

    4.1 Gastric emptying and antral motility¶

    The method and the dose decide the answer. In 24 healthy volunteers, 1,200 mg of ginger taken 1 h before 500 mL of low-nutrient soup halved the gastric half-emptying time on antral ultrasound (13.1 ± 1.1 versus 26.7 ± 3.1 min, p < 0.01), with faster antral-area decline and more frequent antral contractions; fundus dimensions and symptoms were unchanged 70. The same design in 11 functional dyspepsia patients gave 12.3 versus 16.1 min (p ≤ 0.05), a trend to more antral contractions, and no change in symptoms, GLP-1, motilin or ghrelin 71. Stationary manometry after 2 × 100 mg of extract showed increased interdigestive antral motility during phase III of the migrating motor complex and a larger corpus response to a meal 72. Against this, 1 g of powdered ginger did not change gastric emptying by paracetamol absorption in 16 volunteers, and the same group found antiemetic activity after surgery without any emptying change, concluding that the antiemetic effect "is not associated with an effect on gastric emptying" 73 74; 500 mg had no effect on scintigraphic liquid emptying 75.

    Three positive motor studies and two null emptying studies, separated by dose (1.2 g versus 0.5 to 1 g), meal (liquid low-nutrient) and method (serial antral ultrasound is more sensitive to early antral dynamics than drug-appearance curves). The fair summary: about 1.2 g accelerates liquid gastric emptying and raises antral contractions in small single-dose crossovers; solid-meal emptying is unmeasured; the motor effect did not relieve dyspepsia symptoms in the one test; and emptying is not the mechanism of the nausea benefit.

    An open-label 8-week study of 1,080 mg/day in 47 functional dyspepsia patients reported bloating in 14.9 %, heartburn in 12.8 % and diarrhoea in 10.6 %, with 87.2 % rating tolerability good or excellent, no placebo arm and no efficacy outcome 76. No placebo-controlled trial of ginger alone with a validated dyspepsia symptom endpoint beyond a single dose was found.

    4.2 Antiemetic mechanism: allosteric 5-HT3 inhibition¶

    The shorthand "5-HT3 antagonism" is close but not exact. [6]-, [8]- and [10]-gingerol and [6]-shogaol inhibited guanidinium influx through 5-HT3 channels in N1E-115 cells and serotonin-evoked guinea-pig ileum contraction, in the order [6]-shogaol ≥ [8]-gingerol > [10]-gingerol ≥ [6]-gingerol, and none displaced the labelled antagonist [3H]GR65630; weak anticholinergic and antineurokinin activity was also seen, and the vanilloid receptor was not involved 77. In HEK293 cells expressing human 5-HT3A and 5-HT3AB receptors and in whole-mounts of human submucous plexus, ginger extracts and 6-gingerol/6-shogaol inhibited receptor activation at low micromolar potency, reduced the maximal serotonin response without changing serotonin potency, and again displaced no radioligand: non-competitive, allosteric inhibition at a site distinct from the ondansetron site 78. Rat visceral afferent patch-clamp recordings reproduce the non-competitive block, shogaol stronger than gingerol stronger than zingerone 79.

    Two other human-relevant mechanisms exist. 6-shogaol directly activates gastro-oesophageal vagal C-fibres through TRPA1 and then desensitizes them to repeat 6-shogaol but not to distension 80, which may bear on why doses above 1 g do not work better. And in 13 motion-sickness-prone volunteers, 1,000 and 2,000 mg reduced vection-induced nausea, tachygastria and plasma vasopressin, but did not alter nausea produced by intravenous vasopressin, placing ginger's action upstream of the vasopressin surge 81; 1 g blunted the tachygastria evoked by a hyperglycemic clamp but not misoprostol-evoked dysrhythmia, which the authors read as blunted prostaglandin production rather than action 82. These are the only human studies that tie a measurable intermediate to a nausea endpoint. Substance P and muscarinic receptor stories remain weak animal-tissue observations; the mechanism review that lists them is cited for the list only 83. No human receptor-occupancy or pharmacological-blockade study of ginger exists.

    4.3 Nausea and vomiting of pregnancy¶

    The oldest trial, a crossover in 30 women with hyperemesis gravidarum, found 70.4 % preferred the ginger period (p = 0.003) at 1 g/day for 4 days 84. In 70 women at or before 17 weeks, 1 g/day for 4 days reduced nausea VAS by 2.1 ± 1.9 versus 0.9 ± 2.2 (p = 0.014) and vomiting episodes by 1.4 versus 0.3 (p < 0.001) 85. The pooled estimate from 12 RCTs in 1,278 women: nausea MD 1.20 (95 % CI 0.56 to 1.84, p = 0.0002, I² = 0 %); vomiting episodes MD 0.72 (95 % CI −0.03 to 1.46, p = 0.06, I² = 71 %), not significant; subgroups favoured doses under 1,500 mg/day; spontaneous abortion RR 3.14 (95 % CI 0.65 to 15.11) versus placebo and 0.49 (0.17 to 1.42) versus B6, neither significant; no excess heartburn or drowsiness 12. A 2022 pool found improvement OR 7.48 versus placebo, nausea SMD 0.82, vomiting SMD 0.55 (CI crossing zero, I² 91 %), and no difference versus vitamin B6 on any outcome 86. The Cochrane review of all NVP interventions says ginger "may be helpful" but the evidence "was limited and not consistent" 87. The umbrella review of seven NVP meta-analyses, covering 22 independent studies with 49 % overlap, found most positive for nausea and rated the meta-analyses themselves "critically low to low" on AMSTAR-2 88.

    The equivalence trial against pyridoxine (291 women under 16 weeks, 1.05 g ginger versus 75 mg B6 for 3 weeks) found nausea MD 0.2 (90 % CI −0.3 to 0.8), retching 0.3 and vomiting 0.5 (0.0 to 0.9): equivalent 13. Smaller trials point the same way, one finding ginger better than 40 mg B6 on nausea with equal vomiting 89, one three-arm trial finding both beat placebo with no ginger-B6 difference 90.

    Fetal safety. The Norwegian Mother and Child Cohort, 68,522 women with 1,020 (1.5 %) ginger users, found no increased risk of congenital malformations, stillbirth or perinatal death, preterm birth, low birth weight or low Apgar score; the abstract gives no point estimates, exposure is self-reported and doses are unknown 5. The Motherisk prospective comparison of 187 exposed and 187 control pregnancies recorded 181 live births, 2 stillbirths, 3 spontaneous abortions and 3 major malformations, no different from controls and within the 1 to 3 % baseline 91. Against these, a Korean naturopathic dried-ginger cohort of 159 exposed and 306 controls reported major malformations OR 4.9 (95 % CI 0.9 to 25.5, p = 0.051) and stillbirth OR 7.8 (0.9 to 70.3) versus controls, confounded by variable dose and concomitant herbs and "requiring confirmation" 92; the Norwegian cohort found more vaginal bleeding after week 17 among ginger users (7.8 % versus 5.8 %, adjusted OR 1.4, 95 % CI 1.0 to 1.7) but no excess of bleeding beyond spotting. The EU monograph keeps "preferable to avoid" as a precaution while acknowledging 300 to 1,000 reassuring outcomes. Verdict: about 1 g/day reduces nausea intensity, not vomiting frequency, is equivalent to pyridoxine, and is reassuring for malformations and perinatal outcomes without being able to rule out a small effect on early loss.

    4.4 Chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting¶

    Both the claim and the counter-claim have evidence. The largest trial, a URCC multicentre double-blind RCT (744 randomized, 576 analysed, all on a 5-HT3 antagonist on day 1), gave placebo or 0.5, 1.0 or 1.5 g/day for 6 days starting 3 days before chemotherapy: all doses reduced acute nausea severity on day 1 (p = 0.003), with the largest reductions at 0.5 g (p = 0.017) and 1.0 g (p = 0.036); the abstract gives no absolute score differences; NIH-funded with no declared conflicts 4. Two well-run trials were null on their primary endpoints: 162 patients with prior CINV on a 5-HT3 antagonist and/or aprepitant, given 1.0 or 2.0 g/day for 3 days starting on the chemotherapy day, showed no difference in acute or delayed nausea or vomiting, blinding failed, and patients on ginger plus aprepitant had more severe acute nausea than aprepitant alone 93; 244 patients on high-dose cisplatin given 160 mg/day of standardized extract starting the day after cisplatin showed no difference in delayed nausea, with subgroup signals in women and head-and-neck cancer 94. A crossover in 48 gynaecologic oncology patients found 1 g/day for 5 days no better than metoclopramide 95. The most recent positive trial, 103 chemotherapy-naïve adults at two Australian hospitals given a product standardized to 84 mg/day of gingerols plus shogaols from the day of chemotherapy for 5 days over 3 cycles, favoured ginger on nausea-related quality of life (p = 0.003), delayed nausea severity (p = 0.003), delayed vomiting in cycle 2 (4 % versus 27 %, p = 0.001) and fatigue, with no effect on acute or anticipatory CINV and less malnutrition at cycle 3 34.

    Meta-analyses converge on one reproducible finding. Acute vomiting fell with ginger at or below 1 g/day for more than 3 days: OR 0.4 (95 % CI 0.17 to 0.81, 3 studies, n = 301, GRADE moderate), with no significant association with overall or delayed vomiting or with nausea likelihood or severity 14; a 23-RCT pool found acute nausea, delayed nausea, acute vomiting and delayed vomiting all non-significant overall and acute vomiting OR 0.30 (0.12 to 0.79) in the subgroup at or below 1 g/day for more than 4 days 96; a 35-RCT pool that mixed capsules with ginger-partitioned moxibustion reported grade 3 acute nausea RR 0.19 and high-grade vomiting RR 0.47 97. The signal is dose- and timing-dependent: pre-loading at 0.5 to 1 g works, same-day 1 to 2 g does not, and more is not better.

    4.5 Postoperative nausea and vomiting¶

    Early trials were encouraging: fewer nausea episodes than placebo after major gynaecological surgery, similar to metoclopramide 98; day-case laparoscopy PONV 21 % with ginger, 27 % with metoclopramide, 41 % with placebo 74. The 180-patient 2003 laparoscopy trial of two oral doses "failed to reduce the incidence" of PONV 99, and the 2000 systematic review found a pooled absolute risk reduction of 0.052 (95 % CI −0.082 to 0.186), not significant 100. The 2006 meta-analysis that most reviews cite pooled 5 RCTs of at least 1 g in 363 patients: PONV RR 0.69 (95 % CI 0.54 to 0.89), vomiting RR 0.61 (0.45 to 0.84), abdominal discomfort the only side effect 15. Later and larger syntheses agree on nausea and disagree on vomiting: 14 studies in 1,417 participants gave nausea severity MD −0.71 (p = 0.03), rescue antiemetic RR 0.71, nausea over 6 h RR 0.68, and vomiting RR 0.78 (0.42 to 1.44, p = 0.43) 16; 14 trials in 1,506 patients gave lower nausea scores at 2, 6 and 12 h and no significant effect on vomiting, PONV incidence or antiemetic use outside the recovery room 101; a Bayesian network of 18 publications ranked ginger essential-oil aromatherapy best for vomiting (RR 0.39) while no ginger preparation beat placebo on nausea with moderate-to-low certainty 102. Ginger is plausible as a low-cost adjunct at 1 g or more preoperatively and does not justify replacing standard prophylaxis.

    4.6 Motion sickness¶

    One 36-subject 1982 Lancet report, ginger 940 mg versus dimenhydrinate 100 mg versus placebo before a rotating chair, is the origin of "ginger beats Dramamine"; it has no PubMed abstract, and its numbers (335.8, 216.2 and 90.0 s tolerated) are quoted from a secondary summary 103. The next rotating-chair study found no protection from 500 or 1,000 mg of powdered or 1,000 mg of fresh ginger while 0.6 mg scopolamine allowed 147.5 more head movements (p < 0.01), concluding that ginger "does not possess antimotion sickness activity" 75. Ginger had no effect on caloric, rotatory or optokinetic nystagmus, so it has no vestibular or oculomotor action 104. The vection study gives the positive laboratory counterpart with a physiological readout 81. At sea, 1 g reduced vomiting and cold sweating in 80 naval cadets (protection index for vomiting 72 %) 105, and in 1,741 whale-safari tourists randomized among seven agents with no placebo, vomiting (4.1 to 10.2 %) and malaise did not differ, ginger indistinguishable from cinnarizine or dimenhydrinate with caffeine 106. No meta-analysis exists. If ginger helps motion sickness, it does so by damping the gastric dysrhythmia and vasopressin surge, not through the vestibular system.

    4.7 Reflux and heartburn¶

    In 14 healthy men, 1 g of dried ginger left resting lower-esophageal sphincter pressure unchanged over 180 min of manometry, increased swallow-induced relaxation and reduced contraction velocity, an effect the authors framed as antiflatulent; no reflux endpoint was measured 107. Heartburn as an adverse effect: not in excess of placebo in pregnancy RCTs 12; 12.8 % in the open-label dyspepsia series 76; abdominal discomfort the only side effect in PONV trials 15. The GERD benefit claim rests on a dietary-intervention review that pooled two "ginger-containing supplement" trials, both multi-ingredient, to OR 7.50 (95 % CI 3.62 to 15.54), an implausible estimate for two trials that cannot be attributed to ginger 108. Faster gastric emptying would reduce reflux substrate; more LES relaxation would increase it; no pH-impedance or endoscopic study in GERD patients exists. Neither "causes reflux" nor "cures reflux" is supported.

    4.8 Dose and form¶

    The positive results carried 1 to 1.2 g/day in pregnancy, 0.5 to 1.0 g/day started three days before chemotherapy, at or below 1 g/day for more than 3 days for acute vomiting, at least 1 g preoperatively, 1 to 2 g before vection and 1.2 g for the ultrasound emptying effect. Higher doses were not better: the 1.5 g chemotherapy arm did less than 0.5 to 1.0 g, the 2 g arm was null, the pregnancy subgroup favoured under 1,500 mg/day. Powdered rhizome in capsules dominates the positive trials; the one standardized-extract cisplatin trial was null and the one standardized-by-actives trial was positive. Fresh ginger at 1,000 mg did no better than dried in the rotating chair. PubMed searches for ginger ale, candied ginger, lozenges and tea for nausea returned no randomized trials; commercial ginger ales commonly contain little or no ginger.

    5. Pain: Migraine, Dysmenorrhea, Osteoarthritis and Other Pain¶

    5.1 Migraine¶

    The sumatriptan comparison. The 100-patient Zanjan trial randomized migraine-without-aura patients to ginger powder or sumatriptan across five attacks and reported that "two hours after using either drug, mean headache severity decreased significantly", that efficacy "was similar", and that ginger had fewer adverse effects; the abstract gives no doses, group sizes, effect sizes, confidence intervals or p-values, and the 250 mg and 50 mg doses and the 4.6 versus 4.7 severity reductions circulate from secondary sources 28. There is no placebo arm, so "similar to sumatriptan" cannot be separated from regression to the mean (placebo 2 h pain-free rates in triptan trials are commonly 10 to 30 %); there is no non-inferiority margin, no confidence interval on the difference and about 50 per arm, so "statistically comparable" means only that a difference test was non-significant. The 2026 BMJ Evidence-Based Medicine meta-analysis (8 RCTs, 594 patients, ROB-2, GRADE) rates every outcome of this comparison "very uncertain" 109.

    The clean placebo-controlled trial. In a Brazilian emergency room, 60 adults with episodic migraine received 400 mg of ginger extract (5 % actives) or cellulose placebo, both added to intravenous ketoprofen 100 mg: better clinical response with ginger at 1 h (p = 0.04), 1.5 h (p = 0.01) and 2 h (p = 0.04), with reduced pain and better functional status at all times assessed; academic sponsor, registered, double-blind, single centre, add-on design that says nothing about monotherapy 110. The pooled pain-score estimate (MD −1.27 at 2 h, 95 % CI −1.46 to −1.07) from the 3-RCT meta-analysis is this single study's estimate 111; the 2026 synthesis rates it low certainty and, in its own words, "may have little to no difference", a phrase that sits oddly beside the confidence interval and should be read in the full text before quoting either 109.

    The feverfew-ginger combination. The 2005 study was open-label and single-arm (30 subjects, 48 % pain-free at 2 h), so its "effective as a first line abortive" conclusion is not supported by its design 112. The 2011 pilot randomized 60 subjects 3:1, analysed 208 attacks, and found 2 h pain-free 32 % versus 16 % (p = 0.02) and 2 h relief 63 % versus 39 % (p = 0.002), with 15 placebo subjects supplying 57 attack diaries; the product is a combination, the ginger dose is not in the abstract, and press coverage reported funding by the product's maker with one author its chief executive 113. GRADE: very uncertain.

    Prophylaxis. The only placebo-controlled prophylaxis RCT of ginger alone enrolled 107 episodic migraineurs not on prophylaxis and gave 200 mg of dry extract (5 % actives) three times daily or placebo for 3 months after a 1-month baseline: the 50 % responder rate did not differ, and days with severe pain, acute analgesic use and attack duration fell in both arms with no between-group difference 27. The "important reduction" the 2026 synthesis found for prevention is ginger added to an active prophylactic drug (pooled MD −3.18 severity at 3 months, 2 studies, 183 participants, low certainty), not ginger alone 109.

    Migraine verdict: better than placebo in one small add-on trial and one small industry-linked combination trial, low to very low certainty; equivalence to a triptan not demonstrated; prophylaxis negative; monotherapy versus placebo in acute migraine never tested.

    5.2 Primary dysmenorrhea¶

    This is the indication with the most trials and the narrowest population: Iranian female students, 3 to 5 days of dosing, one or two cycles. The only trial an independent review rated adequately double-blind gave 500 mg of root powder three times daily or placebo to 120 students under two protocols; VAS change was 2.21 versus 0.93 cm (p = 0.015) starting two days before menses and 2.72 versus 1.51 cm (p = 0.029) starting on day 1, with 13 placebo dropouts, no intention-to-treat analysis and a possible carry-over 114. A three-arm trial found ginger and zinc sulfate both beat placebo and did not differ from each other 115. The head-to-head trials against NSAIDs are described in section 3.4 64 65 66.

    Syntheses: the 2015 meta-analysis reported its pooled effect as "risk ratio −1.85 (95 % CI −2.87 to −0.84)", a negative risk ratio being impossible and the figure a mislabelled VAS mean difference, and its first author was employed by a supplement manufacturer 116; the NIH-funded 2016 pool gave ginger versus placebo 1.55 cm (0.68 to 2.43) and versus mefenamic acid SMD 0 (−0.40 to 0.41), flagging small numbers, poor quality and high heterogeneity 67; the Cochrane review of 27 supplement trials (3,101 women, 22 of 27 Iranian) gave ginger MD −1.55 (−2.43 to −0.68, 3 RCTs, 266 women), all low or very low quality, with "no high quality evidence to support the effectiveness of any dietary supplement for dysmenorrhoea" 17; the 2024 PROSPERO-registered pool (24 studies, 12 pooled) gave SMD −1.13 versus placebo (I² 81 %), −0.29 for pain duration, 0.01 versus NSAIDs and 0.06 versus exercise, with safety data "infrequently reported" 117. The umbrella review rates the dysmenorrhea effect moderate to large with low to moderate GRADE 118.

    Verdict: better than placebo by about 1.5 cm on a 10 cm scale, low-quality evidence; "equal to NSAIDs" is a pooled SMD near zero from two or three small trials, none designed for non-inferiority, one alternate-allocated, one with ginger numerically worse; dose 750 to 2,000 mg/day of powder for 3 to 5 days.

    5.3 Osteoarthritis¶

    The double-dummy three-period crossover of ginger extract, ibuprofen and placebo in hip or knee osteoarthritis ranked ibuprofen above ginger above placebo on VAS pain and the Lequesne index, with ginger versus placebo not significant in the crossover analysis and both actives better than placebo only in an exploratory first-period analysis; it is routinely cited as positive and by its primary analysis is negative 119. The largest trial, 261 patients with moderate-to-severe knee pain given a standardized extract of Zingiber officinale plus Alpinia galanga (EV.EXT 77) twice daily for 6 weeks, found responders (at least 15 mm VAS reduction in pain on standing) in 63 % versus 50 % (p = 0.048), pain on standing −24.5 versus −16.4 mm (p = 0.005), WOMAC −12.9 versus −9.0 mm (p = 0.087), and GI adverse events in 59 versus 21 patients, mostly mild 120. A 29-patient crossover of 250 mg four times daily for 3 months per period found no difference at the crossover and p < 0.001 at 24 weeks favouring the arm then on ginger, with acknowledged carry-over and two dropouts for heartburn 121. A topical 4 % ginger-plus-plai gel matched 1 % diclofenac gel over 6 weeks with no placebo 122.

    The trialist-led meta-analysis of 5 placebo-controlled RCTs (593 patients) gave pain SMD −0.30 (95 % CI −0.50 to −0.09, p = 0.005, I² 27 %), disability SMD −0.22, and withdrawals for adverse events RR 2.33 (1.04 to 5.22), moderate-quality evidence downgraded for small numbers and inadequate intention-to-treat; two of its authors led two of the five trials, at least two of which tested extracts other than pure rhizome 18. The independent Chilean synthesis of overlapping trials gave capsules versus placebo pain MD −7.88 mm and concluded "insufficient evidence to support the use of oral ginger compared with placebo", "heterogeneous" and of "poor methodologic quality" 123. The umbrella review calls the osteoarthritis effect small with high GRADE, a judgement of the Bartels review that coexists with the Chilean verdict 118.

    Verdict: probably better than placebo, small (about 8 mm on 100), with doubled withdrawal for GI effects; not equivalent to a drug, since ibuprofen ranked above it in the one three-way trial; the best trial used a two-species extract.

    5.4 Other pain¶

    Tension-type headache: PubMed searches for ginger with tension-type headache and randomized designs returned zero records; any claim is extrapolated from low-certainty migraine data. Low back pain: the only RCT compared Swedish massage with aromatic ginger oil against traditional Thai massage in 140 older adults, so the massage technique is confounded with the oil and nothing bears on oral ginger 124. Postoperative pain: a 10-patient split-mouth crossover of ginger 400 mg versus ibuprofen 400 mg after periodontal flap surgery found no significant VAS difference and cannot establish equivalence 125; an unblinded tonsillectomy trial of ginger capsules added to routine care in 49 patients reported lower pain on days 1 to 10 (p < 0.001) and faster epithelialization, with no placebo 126; the systematic review of herbal medicines in surgery found ginger reduced vomiting (RR 0.57) and nausea (RR 0.69) but rescue analgesia (damask rose and ginger pooled) was non-significant (RR 0.52, 95 % CI 0.13 to 2.13), very low certainty 127. The Exeter review of ginger as sole oral treatment for any pain (8 trials, 481 participants, Jadad 2 to 5) concluded that the evidence "remains insufficient"; nothing since has been large enough to overturn it 128. A family-level meta-analysis of Zingiberaceae extracts for chronic pain (SMD −0.67) pools turmeric, ginger and galangal and cannot be attributed to ginger 129.

    5.5 Quality of the pain evidence¶

    Dysmenorrhea has exactly one adequately double-blind trial; one trial alternated allocation; the sumatriptan trial does not describe how a ginger capsule was matched to a sumatriptan tablet. The largest pain trial has 261 patients; everything else is 10 to 150; placebo-controlled migraine trials total about 227 patients. Non-inferiority against an NSAID or triptan needs several hundred per arm. Cochrane counts 22 of 27 dysmenorrhea supplement trials as Iranian; populations are overwhelmingly female students aged 18 to 25. This is not evidence of misconduct; it is a narrow, clustered base with unregistered protocols. Trialists meta-analysing their own trials, a supplement-company-authored meta-analysis with a mislabelled effect measure, and repeated pooling of the same two or three trials under new titles all appear; the independent syntheses (Cochrane, the Chilean group, the 2026 BMJ EBM group) are uniformly more cautious than the trialist- or industry-adjacent ones. The GI tolerability advantage over NSAIDs at matched efficacy has never been tested: ginger caused more GI events than placebo in the largest osteoarthritis trial, and no trial compared GI harm with an NSAID at equianalgesic doses.

    6. Exercise Recovery, Soreness and Muscle Damage¶

    6.1 The foundational series and its replications¶

    The University of Georgia series, funded by the McCormick Science Institute, began with null acute experiments. A single 2 g dose 30 min before 30 min of cycling at 60 % VO2peak in 25 people had no effect on quadriceps pain, RPE, work rate, heart rate or VO2 during or after exercise, which the authors read as evidence that prostaglandins are not the main driver of this kind of exercise pain 130. A single 2 g dose at 24 and 48 h after eccentric elbow-flexor exercise in 27 people had no effect on pain, dysfunction or metabolic rate 45 min after ingestion, with a 13 % lower next-day pain in the subset dosed at 24 h 131. The chronic design followed: two identical double-blind placebo-controlled experiments of 2 g/day of raw (n = 34) or heat-treated (n = 40) ginger for 11 days before 18 eccentric elbow-flexor actions, with pain at 24 h reduced by 25 % (−0.78 SD, p = 0.041) with raw and 23 % (−0.57 SD, p = 0.049) with heated ginger, "smaller effects" without numbers on effort, plasma PGE2, arm volume, range of motion and strength, and no enhancement from heating 19. The senior author's 2026 review confirms that the acute experiments "yielded null results" and led to the chronic design 132. A letter commenting on the 2010 trial was retracted in 2014; it carries no data and is excluded from this dossier.

    Independent work is mixed. In 20 non-weight-trained adults, 4 g/day for 5 days before a high-intensity eccentric protocol did not change pain; 1RM fell at 24 h in both groups and recovered at 48 h only with ginger; creatine kinase rose in both groups and kept rising only in the ginger group at 72 and 96 h; the authors concluded that 4 g may speed strength recovery but does not change indicators of muscle damage or soreness 20. In 32 experienced recreational runners, 1.425 g/day for 5 days around a 40-min downhill run gave soreness effects of d = −0.69 immediately post-run and −0.72 at day 5 with 90 % confidence limits crossing zero, a possible small vertical-jump benefit (d = 0.24), and "likely no meaningful impact" on 1.5-mile run time 133. A quasi-experimental study of 36 women found pre-exercise extract better than post-exercise and both better than placebo for pain at 24 to 48 h, with IL-6 lower in the pre-exercise group and creatine kinase, strength, circumference and range of motion unchanged 134. In 60 female taekwondo athletes, 3 g/day for 6 weeks lowered a soreness rating versus placebo (p < 0.01) without changing IL-6, malondialdehyde, performance or body composition 135 136. In 28 trained runners, 1.5 g/day during weeks 7 to 12 of training lowered post-test IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α (all p = 0.01), with no absolute values in the abstract 137. In 32 obese men, 1 g/day for 10 weeks lowered CRP in every group except placebo-only, a chronic-inflammation signal rather than a soreness result 138. Two triple-blind trials of a ginger plus Bixa orellana blend cannot isolate ginger and have declared manufacturer employment in the sibling paper 139 140.

    6.2 Dosing pattern, form and what moves¶

    Acute single doses are null in three datasets, including a 2025 crossover in which 1,000 mg before a time-to-exhaustion test changed neither endurance nor respiratory exchange ratio, while green tea extract did 141. Chronic loading of about 2 g/day for 5 to 11 days before the damaging bout is the only pattern with repeated positive soreness results, with the 4 g/day trial as the exception. Raw and heated ginger did not differ in the only direct test. Nearly all positive trials used dried powder in capsules; a 4-week trial of 53 g/day of juiced fresh ginger in 30 trained cyclists, funded by the maker of the shot used, found no difference in 75 km time-trial completion, VO2, power, heart rate or perceived soreness, a null result that argues against sponsor bias in that case 142. One 2026 trial of 2 g/day for 9 days in 32 CrossFit practitioners reported more repetitions in an AMRAP test (241.6 versus 225.9, p = 0.021), lower RPE and a smaller post-exercise decline in jump peak force; it is the first positive performance outlier and stands alone 143.

    The biomarker story is weaker than the soreness story and partly contradicts it. No ginger-only trial reports a lower creatine kinase; in the one trial where it moved, it moved the wrong way. Lactate dehydrogenase and myoglobin data specific to ginger effectively do not exist. Plasma PGE2 in the foundational trial is reported only as "smaller effects". Cytokines are mixed, with no absolute concentrations in any positive abstract. The only meta-analysis pools ginger with curcumin, ginseng and garlic across 25 studies and 353 participants, includes six ginger studies of which two are not PubMed-indexed, and reports no ginger-only confidence interval; its pooled damage-marker reduction is driven mostly by curcumin 144. The systematic review of ginger in sport concluded that about 2 g/day for at least 5 days "may modestly reduce muscle pain stemming from eccentric resistance exercise and prolonged running", with "no discernable effects" on body composition, metabolic rate, oxygen consumption, force or perceived exertion, and named the absence of any direct comparison with NSAID ingestion 145. The best-powered estimate of ginger's analgesic effect in any condition remains the osteoarthritis SMD of −0.30 18.

    The parsimonious reading: a modest analgesic effect on perceived soreness after chronic loading, in untrained or recreational participants, from one laboratory's trials with borderline p-values and partial independent replication; no reduction in muscle damage; no ergogenic effect on endurance; and no test of whether the analgesia blunts training adaptation, a question raised by the runners' trial and never measured. "Black ginger" supplement claims are Kaempferia parviflora mouse data, a different genus, and say nothing about Zingiber officinale 146 147.

    7. Glycemic Control, Lipids, Blood Pressure, Weight and Oxidative Markers¶

    7.1 Type 2 diabetes: the trials and what moves¶

    Every placebo-controlled type 2 diabetes trial in the tables below is Iranian and double-blind. At 2 g/day for 2 months in 64 patients, insulin (11.0 versus 12.1, p = 0.001), HOMA-IR (3.9 versus 4.5, p = 0.002) and QUICKI improved while fasting glucose and HbA1c did not 148. At 1,600 mg/day for 12 weeks in 70 patients, fasting glucose, HbA1c, insulin, HOMA, triglycerides and total cholesterol improved (p < 0.05), magnitudes not in the abstract 54. At 3 g/day for 8 weeks in 88 patients, fasting glucose fell 10.5 % in the ginger group while rising 21 % in the placebo group, with insulin, HOMA-IR and HOMA-derived β-cell and sensitivity indices improved; the deteriorating placebo arm is the main source of heterogeneity in later pools 149. At 2 g/day for 12 weeks in 41 patients, fasting glucose, HbA1c, apolipoprotein B and malondialdehyde fell 42. At 3 g/day for 3 months in 45 patients, glucose fell 19.41 versus rose 1.63 mg/dL, HbA1c −0.77 versus +0.02 %, insulin −1.46 versus +0.09 µIU/mL, hs-CRP −2.78 versus +0.2 mg/L, MDA −0.85 versus +0.06 µmol/L, paraoxonase-1 +22 versus +1.7 U/L (all p ≤ 0.01) 150. The largest trial, 204 patients across five arms given 3 g/day of ginger stirred into black tea for 8 weeks, found lipid changes and no effect on glycemic control, anthropometry, inflammation or oxidative stress 23. A 4 g/day trial in coronary artery disease patients found no effect on lipids or glucose 43.

    Other populations: obese women, 2 g/day for 12 weeks, BMI, insulin and HOMA-IR down versus placebo, glucose down in both arms, adiponectin and body composition unchanged 61; metabolic syndrome pilot, 37 patients, triglycerides, glucose and insulin resistance improved 151; NAFLD, 44 patients on diet advice, ALT, GGT, cytokines, insulin resistance and steatosis grade improved, fibrosis unchanged 152, pooled with three other trials to ALT SMD −0.43 and HOMA-IR SMD −1.14 with no effect on AST, cholesterol or BMI 153; peritoneal dialysis, 1,000 mg/day, fasting glucose down about 20 % 59; polycystic ovary syndrome, the one RCT, metformin and cinnamon lowered HOMA-IR versus placebo and versus ginger, ginger did not, and lowered FSH and LH only 154; healthy adults, 1,500 mg/day crossover for 6 weeks, no effect on lipids, weight or blood pressure 155. No RCT in prediabetes exists in PubMed.

    7.3 Lipids¶

    Triglycerides fall in nearly every pool and nothing else moves reliably. Twelve trials in 586 participants: triglycerides −17.59 mg/dL (−29.32 to −5.87), total cholesterol −5.13 (not significant), HDL +2.18 (not significant), with triglyceride and total cholesterol effects only at or below 2 g/day and a printed LDL confidence interval that does not bracket its point estimate 156. A 26-trial multivariate pool: triglycerides −12.54, total cholesterol −6.53, LDL −5.14, HDL +1.13 mg/dL, BMI −0.49, weight not significant 24. A 2023 pool: total cholesterol SMD −0.44 and triglycerides −0.61 significant, HDL and LDL not, total cholesterol dose-dependent in meta-regression 157. The type 2 diabetes pools disagree with each other: one found no lipid effect 158, the other triglycerides −17.10 and HDL +2.13 mg/dL with I² above 81 % 21. Primary trials outside diabetes: hyperlipidaemia, 3 g/day for 45 days, triglycerides and total cholesterol fell more than placebo, LDL and HDL not 159; peritoneal dialysis, 1,000 mg/day, triglycerides −15 %, cholesterol fractions and Lp(a) unchanged 160; healthy adults, no lipid change but higher cholesterol efflux capacity of apoB-depleted plasma 155. The GRADE umbrella rates every lipid outcome very low certainty with I² 94 to 98 % 118. The dose signal is contradictory across pools.

    7.4 Blood pressure¶

    Six RCTs in 345 participants: SBP −6.36 mmHg (−11.27 to −1.46, I² 90 %) and DBP −2.12, significant only in subsets with mean age at or below 50, duration at or below 8 weeks and dose at or above 3 g/day 161. The type 2 diabetes pools give SBP −4.20 158 and −1.62 mmHg 21. The 2026 GRADE dose-response meta-analysis of 12 RCTs: SBP −5.03 mmHg (−8.49 to −1.57, p = 0.004), DBP −2.03 (−3.90 to −0.15), larger with elevated baseline pressure, at or above 2,000 mg/day and in obesity, a non-linear inverse dose-response, duration without influence, no publication bias detected, GRADE low for inconsistency 25. Healthy normotensive adults showed no change 155. A 2 to 6 mmHg systolic fall in people whose pressure is already elevated is reproduced across four pools; in normotensive adults there is no signal.

    7.5 Weight, body composition and thermogenesis¶

    Fourteen RCTs in overweight or obese participants: weight SMD −0.66, waist-hip ratio −0.49, BMI not significant 162. Twenty-seven RCTs in 1,309 participants with GRADE: weight −1.52 kg (−2.37 to −0.66), BMI −0.58, waist −1.04 cm, body fat −0.87 %, non-linear dose-response with an effective dose of 2 g/day and effective duration beyond 8 weeks, certainty low for weight and fat and very low for BMI and waist 163. The newer 36-RCT GRADE pool: waist −0.65 cm, body fat −1.49 %, adiponectin +0.84 µg/mL, and weight and BMI not significantly improved, with a linear inverse association between duration and weight change 164. Adding nine trials erased the weight effect; which set is right depends on inclusion decisions not visible in abstracts.

    The thermogenesis claim rests on a single-meal crossover in 10 overweight men given 2 g of ginger in hot water with breakfast: thermic effect of food +42.7 ± 21.4 kcal/day (p = 0.049), resting energy expenditure unchanged (p = 0.43), respiratory quotient unchanged, lower hunger ratings and prospective intake, and "no effects of ginger on glucose, insulin, lipids, or inflammatory markers"; NIH-funded 165. In 80 obese women, 2 g/day for 12 weeks produced "slight but statistically significant" decreases in anthropometric measurements and appetite score, no change in body composition, and larger effects in UCP1 and β3-adrenoceptor genotypes 166. The systematic review of ginger and obesity (6 in-vitro, 17 animal, 4 human studies) concluded that animal data support weight lowering whereas "the available limited clinical studies showed no changes or slight changes" 167.

    7.6 Oxidative stress¶

    Twelve trials: GPx SMD 1.64, TAC 0.40, MDA −0.69, catalase not significant, I² 43 to 87 % 168. Eleven RCTs: GPx Hedges' g 1.93, MDA −1.45, TAC not significant, with the MDA reduction larger in trials of 1 g or less, under 12 weeks and 40 participants or fewer, the classic small-study pattern 169. The 29-RCT GRADE pool: MDA −0.76 µmol/L, TAC +0.22 mmol/L, SOD +48 U/L 26. The umbrella review rates MDA and TAC very low certainty with I² 83 to 95 % 118. The signal reproduces in direction in every pool and fails on heterogeneity, small-study bias and risk of bias.

    7.7 Mechanism in humans versus cells and rodents¶

    Shown in humans: lower fasting insulin and HOMA-IR in type 2 diabetes and obesity, an insulin-sensitizing rather than secretagogue pattern; reduced lipid peroxidation and higher paraoxonase-1 and total antioxidant capacity; about 43 kcal/day more postprandial thermogenesis with no change in resting energy expenditure; higher cholesterol efflux capacity in healthy adults; pooled adiponectin +0.84 µg/mL. Shown only in cells or rodents: gingerol fractions raise cell-surface GLUT4 in L6 myotubes with [8]-gingerol the most potent constituent, not [6]-gingerol 170; [6]-gingerol raises glucose uptake through Ca²⁺/CaMKK and AMPKα in L6 cells 171, through AMPKα2 and AS160/Rab5 in C2C12 cells while potentiating insulin-stimulated Akt 172, and lowers fasting glucose in db/db mice while protecting β-cells from AGE-induced oxidative stress 173; 200 mg/kg of [6]-gingerol for 28 days raised GLP-1 and glucose-stimulated insulin secretion in Lepr^db/db mice 174; an ethyl-acetate extract inhibits α-glucosidase and α-amylase in a tube 175. No human study has measured muscle AMPK, GLUT4 translocation, GLP-1 or a standardized postprandial glucose excursion after ginger, and 200 mg/kg of pure gingerol in a mouse has no counterpart in a 2 g/day powder containing a few percent gingerols.

    7.8 Reading the cardiometabolic evidence¶

    In type 2 diabetes on oral agents, 1.6 to 3 g/day of capsule powder for 8 to 12 weeks lowers fasting glucose by 16 to 21 mg/dL and HbA1c by 0.4 to 1.0 points in the pooled Iranian trials, with moderate GRADE for HbA1c and low for glucose, a null result in the one risk-of-bias-filtered pool and in the largest single trial, and no external replication. Triglycerides fall by about 9 to 25 mg/dL; cholesterol fractions barely move; all lipid certainty is very low. Blood pressure falls 5/2 mmHg at 2 g/day or more in people whose pressure is high and not at all in people whose pressure is normal. Weight falls 1 to 1.5 kg in one pool and not at all in the larger one; the thermogenesis effect is 43 kcal in 10 men. Nothing has been tested beyond about 3 months. Capsule powder carried the positive results; ginger stirred into tea or hot water did not change glycemia.

    8. Male Hormones, Testicular Function and Semen Quality¶

    8.1 The human record¶

    Every PubMed query combining ginger with testosterone and a human filter returns only reviews. There is no randomized placebo-controlled trial of ginger on testosterone in eugonadal, hypogonadal or diabetic men. The 2018 narrative review that collates the rodent literature states that the effect is "not yet confirmed in humans" 29; the 2019 systematic review of semen studies ends with "positive effects ... require additional approval in men" 176; the 2021 review spanning laboratory animals, poultry and humans calls for safety and clinical trials 177; the 2022 review of Iranian-studied herbs lists ginger without adding human data 178.

    The human hormone data are two sources. The first, published in the Tikrit Medical Journal in 2012 and not indexed in PubMed, is an open-label pre/post study of 75 infertile Iraqi men all treated with ginger for 3 months, with 25 fertile men as a reference group, no placebo, no randomization, no blinding and no stated dose in the readable text. It reports testosterone 9.24 to 11.03 ng/mL (+17.7 %), LH 3.68 to 6.48 mIU/mL (+43.2 %) and FSH +17.6 %, with a baseline testosterone in the results text (9.07) that does not match the table (9.24), a baseline near the upper limit of normal for any adult male population, and a baseline sperm count of 61.9 million/mL and 68.9 % normal forms, both inside the WHO reference range, so most of these "infertile men" were normozoospermic. The second is the only double-blind RCT: 100 infertile men at the Royan Institute, 250 mg of ginger powder twice daily (500 mg/day) for 3 months, primary outcome sperm DNA fragmentation. Hormones were measured only at baseline (with an LH imbalance despite randomization, p = 0.01), so the trial contributes nothing to the testosterone question; 8 to 10 % of participants were on testosterone and 82 to 88 % on infertility drugs, unadjusted 179. The 17.7 % figure that circulates is therefore a within-group change in an uncontrolled study of men who were not hypogonadal.

    For semen parameters, the RCT found sperm concentration unchanged (adjusted MD 1.08 million/mL, 95 % CI −9.13 to 11.3, p = 0.83), progressive motility trending against ginger (adjusted MD −7.79, CI −16.13 to 0.54, p = 0.06), normal morphology trending against ginger (−1.41, p = 0.06), and TUNEL DNA fragmentation lower (53.5 % to 17.8 % versus 56.8 % to 40.5 %, adjusted MD 3.21 percentage points, 95 % CI 0.78 to 5.63, p = 0.009) 179. No pregnancy or live-birth outcome exists; the result has not been replicated with a second assay. A four-herb mixture including ginger lowered total and free testosterone and raised LH in 60 women with polycystic ovary syndrome, the opposite direction from the male claim and not attributable to ginger 180.

    8.2 The rodent record¶

    In healthy animals the effect is small and inconsistent. An aqueous extract at 600 mg/kg/day for 8 days raised serum testosterone and testicular cholesterol in rats 181. Ginger at 100 mg/kg/day for 56 days raised testosterone, LH, FSH, sperm indices and antioxidant enzymes in diabetic and healthy rats 182. A 20-day study at 50 and 100 mg/kg (not PubMed-indexed) raised testosterone only at the higher dose with LH, FSH, sperm concentration and morphology unchanged. In healthy rats with or without endurance training, 100 mg/kg raised P450scc and P450c17 mRNA (p = 0.03 each) with LH and FSH receptor expression unchanged, and the abstract gives no serum testosterone for the ginger-only group 183.

    The large majority of the rodent literature is rescue of a damaged testis, and here the pattern is consistent: cisplatin (1 g/kg ethanol extract restored motility, normalized MDA and antioxidant enzymes, reduced apoptosis) 184 185; aluminium chloride (improvement only at day 60) 186; lead acetate at 0.5 to 1 g/kg (testosterone restored without any LH change) 187; lead acetate at 250 mg/kg with or without zinc (testosterone, LH, FSH, Nrf2 restored; inflammation and apoptosis markers down) 188; cadmium (testosterone, LH, FSH and semen restored, testicular GSH up, MDA and NO down) 189; alloxan diabetes (testosterone, fertility index, sperm count and motility up; LD50 above 5 g/kg) 190 191; streptozotocin diabetes, in which isolated zingerone, geraniol and 6-gingerol at 20, 200 and 75 mg/kg did not reverse the testosterone deficit 192; high-fructose diet (pituitary gonadotroph ultrastructure preserved) 193; L-NAME hypertension (testosterone fall prevented, testicular arginase down and NO up) 194; carbendazim (a 6-gingerol-rich fraction restored testosterone; LH unaffected by toxicant or fraction) 195; difenoconazole (testosterone, LH, FSH, StAR and 17β-HSD activity restored, Bax and caspase-3 down) 196; cyclophosphamide (testosterone and total antioxidant capacity up, MDA and ROS unchanged) 197; busulfan (testosterone and sperm count up at 100 to 150 mg/kg) 198; sodium metabisulfite (testosterone and spermatogenic cell counts restored) 199; high-cholesterol diet (testosterone, LH, FSH, 3β- and 17β-HSD activity and Leydig counts up, testicular cholesterol down, boiled better than raw, and estradiol also up) 200. In a mouse Leydig cell line, 25 µM zingerone reversed zearalenone-induced loss of testosterone output through PI3K-AKT-mTOR and Nrf2, again a rescue experiment 201.

    Human-equivalent doses by body-surface-area scaling run from about 0.5 to 11 g/day of extract for a 70 kg man; the human trials used 0.5 g/day of powder. Where LH is measured the models split: lead and carbendazim rescues occurred without LH change, diabetes, cadmium and fructose rescues with LH restoration, which suggests an action at the testis (antioxidant) with LH tracking the insult's own pituitary effect rather than a direct ginger action on the pituitary.

    9. Safety, Bleeding, Drug Metabolism and Special Populations¶

    9.1 Adverse effects¶

    In the only human dose-escalation study, any adverse event occurred in 0 of 3 at 100 mg, 2 of 3 at 250 mg, 1 of 3 at 500 mg, 3 of 6 at 1 g, 2 of 3 at 1.5 g and 6 of 9 at 2 g, nothing above NCI grade 1, the events being eructation, heartburn, indigestion, headache and fatigue 9. Pooled pregnancy RCTs found no significant excess of heartburn or drowsiness 12. The 162-patient oncology trial found no difference in all adverse events and less fatigue with ginger 93; the 103-patient trial reported no safety signal 34; 51 healthy adults on 1.2 g/day for 14 days had no moderate or severe adverse events 202. The 109-RCT review gives no pooled adverse-event rate in its abstract 1. Hypersensitivity and dermatitis are listed by the EU monograph with frequency "not known"; PubMed holds occupational contact dermatitis only for Zingiber mioga, a different species. GI withdrawals doubled in osteoarthritis trials 18; the largest osteoarthritis trial recorded GI events in 59 versus 21 patients 120.

The EU assessor's conclusion that the data are "not sufficient to include a warning" is defensible; the German and Dutch labels keep one; pausing supplements before surgery is a cost-free precaution; the dabigatran signal (P-glycoprotein substrate, rat data, one fatal case) is the one mechanistically plausible direct-oral-anticoagulant interaction and is untested in humans.

    9.3 Cytochrome P450 and transporters¶

    In vitro the signals are strong: 6-, 8- and 10-gingerol "potently inhibited CYP2C9", moderately CYP2C19 and CYP3A4, weakly CYP2D6, with 8-gingerol IC50 6.8, 12.5, 8.7 and 42.7 µmol/L, and 6-gingerol inducing while 8- and 10-gingerol suppressed CYP3A4 mRNA 209; 6-gingerol inhibited P-glycoprotein digoxin transport at 100 to 500 µM and CYP3A4 4-hydroxylation at 60 to 500 µM 210; whole extract had a much higher half-maximal inhibition value than individual gingerols, "indicating no possible herb-drug interactions" except additive CYP1A2 and CYP2C8 inhibition, and the phenolics were not P-gp substrates 211; a characterized extract and its constituents "dramatically inhibited" CYP3A4, 2C9, 1A2, 2B6, P-gp and BCRP and activated PXR and AhR, with the authors arguing that simulated intestinal-fluid concentrations "could conceivably exceed" CYP IC50s at recommended doses 212. The only human cytochrome phenotyping is the warfarin study: no CYP2C9 inhibition at label dose 30. No human midazolam, omeprazole or digoxin probe study exists.

    Exposure arithmetic settles the systemic question: free plasma 10-gingerol of about 0.03 µM and 6-shogaol of about 0.05 µM after 2 g of extract sit 100- to 1,000-fold below the lowest in-vitro IC50s, so systemic CYP inhibition is implausible at any realistic dose. The unresolved question is the gut wall, where luminal concentrations after a gram-scale dose can reach the micromolar range, so first-pass CYP3A4 and P-gp effects on narrow-index substrates (tacrolimus, dabigatran, some kinase inhibitors) remain possible and untested.

    9.4 Pregnancy, lactation and children¶

    Section 4.3 gives the malformation and perinatal data 5 91 92 12; the standard trial dose of 1 g/day for 4 days had "no adverse effect on pregnancy outcome" 85. LactMed states that "very limited data exist on the safety and efficacy of ginger in nursing mothers or infants", that it is generally recognized as safe as a flavouring, and that galactogogue evidence is weak 213; the EU monograph does not recommend use during lactation for lack of data. In children, the monograph permits 250 to 500 mg before travel from age 6 under traditional use with efficacy "not substantiated"; one double-blind RCT in 150 children aged 1 to 10 with gastroenteritis vomiting found at least one further vomiting episode in 67 % versus 87 %, absolute risk reduction 20 % (95 % CI 7 to 33), number needed to treat 5 214.

    9.5 Other interactions and cautions¶

    Antidiabetics: 2 g/day for 12 weeks lowered fasting glucose and HbA1c in 41 diabetics on oral agents 42; 4 g/day in coronary patients changed nothing 43; no hypoglycaemia case with sulfonylureas or insulin was found; an additive effect is plausible and unquantified. Antihypertensives: the 5/2 mmHg pooled fall at or above 2 g/day 25 plus the nifedipine platelet potentiation 215; no hypotension case reported. Aprepitant: more severe acute nausea with ginger plus aprepitant than aprepitant alone in one trial, mechanism unknown 93. Gallstones: the caution rests entirely on a 1985 rat study in which an acetone extract and 6- and 10-gingerol increased bile flow 216; no human gallstone adverse event or biliary-colic report was found; the German label keeps "consult your doctor", the EU monograph dropped it. Product quality: gingerol content varies about 10-fold across brands and some products contain no 6-gingerol 8 32; no published adulteration case was found; the documented risk is under-dosing and mislabelling, not spiking.

    11. Open Questions¶

    1. Which molecule acts in vivo, and does any ginger effect in humans go through COX at all? A pharmacokinetic sub-study inside a biomarker RCT, with deconjugation assays and metabolite profiling, has never been run.
    2. Does the glycemic effect survive a non-Iranian, pre-registered, RoB-2-clean trial of standardized powder with assayed gingerol content?
    3. Does ginger monotherapy beat placebo in acute migraine? A three-arm trial (ginger, placebo, triptan) of 300 or more patients would settle this and the sumatriptan claim.
    4. Can the dysmenorrhea effect survive a non-Iranian, adequately blinded trial of 300 or more women with a placebo and an NSAID arm?
    5. Is the 11-day soreness effect reproducible at n ≥ 100, and does the analgesia blunt training adaptation the way NSAIDs are argued to?
    6. Does ginger change testosterone in men at all? Morning total and free testosterone, SHBG, LH and FSH at baseline and 12 weeks in a placebo-controlled trial have never been measured.
    7. Is the sperm DNA-fragmentation effect real? It needs a second assay and a pregnancy endpoint.
    8. What does a commercial ginger shot contain, and what plasma levels does a food-range dose produce?
    9. Does ginger interact with dabigatran or other P-gp substrates in humans, and does heavy intake raise INR in real anticoagulated patients?
    10. Are the Korean stillbirth and Norwegian spotting signals in pregnancy dose-related, and does early-pregnancy loss change at trial doses?
    11. Why does the nausea dose-response flatten or reverse above 1 g? TRPA1-mediated vagal desensitization is a candidate and untested.
    12. Does ginger alter solid-meal gastric emptying with repeated dosing, and does it help or harm reflux on pH-impedance monitoring?

    The full findings — all 46¶

    Every conclusion this review reached, grouped by how strongly the evidence backs it. Each finding is distilled from the fully-cited evidence above.

    Tier 1 — Settled (High Confidence) (13 findings)

    1. Gingerols and 6-shogaol circulate almost entirely as glucuronide and sulfate conjugates; free 6-gingerol is undetectable even after 2 g of extract.

    In the two human pharmacokinetic studies, a single 2.0 g dose of a 5 % gingerol extract (about 100 mg of actives) gave conjugate peaks of 0.47 to 0.85 µg/mL for 6-gingerol, free 10-gingerol of 9.5 ng/mL and free 6-shogaol of 13.6 ng/mL at 1 h, with no free 6- or 8-gingerol detectable from 0.25 to 24 h; half-lives are 1 to 3 h and daily dosing does not accumulate. First-pass glucuronidation by UGT1A9/2B7 and intestinal UGT1A8 is the dominant clearance route.

    2. The free plasma levels humans reach sit 100- to 1,000-fold below the concentrations that inhibit COX-2, 5-LOX or CYP enzymes in a dish.

    Purified 10-shogaol, 8-shogaol and 10-gingerol inhibit COX-2 with IC50 7.5, 17.5 and 32 µM, gingerdione inhibits 5-LOX at 15 µM and gingerols inhibit CYP2C9 at about 7 µM; free 6-shogaol after a maximal supplement dose peaks near 0.05 µM. The species that does circulate at micromolar levels, the glucuronide, has no measured COX activity; the "how" of ginger in humans is not established.

    3. Dried ginger powder is 3 to 8 times richer in actives per gram than fresh rhizome, and supplements vary about 10-fold between brands.

    A validated UHPLC-MS/MS survey found 1.1 to 1.5 mg/g of total gingerols plus shogaols in fresh rhizome, 4.4 to 11.3 mg/g in dried powder and 2.9 to 30 mg/g in supplements; an earlier survey of ten US products found 6-gingerol from 0 to 9.43 mg/g, two products with none detectable, and suggested servings from 250 mg to 4.77 g/day. Heating and drying convert gingerols to shogaols reversibly rather than destroying activity.

    4. The "4 g/day limit" is the top of a 1990 therapeutic range, not a toxicological ceiling; the current EU monograph caps motion-sickness use at 2.5 g/day.

    The EMA assessment report traces the 2 to 4 g/day figure to the German Commission E monograph of 1990; the 2025 EU herbal monograph (EMA/HMPC/885789/2022, https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/medicines/herbal/zingiberis-rhizoma) sets 1 to 2 g once before travel for the well-established use, a maximum of 2.5 g/day in repeated motion-sickness dosing, 0.18 to 1 g three times daily for spasmodic GI complaints, lists no interactions and no gallstone warning, and rates GI adverse effects as common (1 to 10 %). No human NOAEL has been derived; tolerability has been tested to 2 g of extract in one dose, 4 g/day of powder for 3 months and 15 g of raw root daily for 2 weeks without serious toxicity.

    5. About 1 g/day reduces nausea in pregnancy versus placebo, has no reliable effect on vomiting, and is equivalent to vitamin B6.

    Twelve RCTs in 1,278 women give a nausea reduction of 1.20 VAS points (95 % CI 0.56 to 1.84, I² = 0 %) and a non-significant vomiting effect (MD 0.72, CI −0.03 to 1.46); the equivalence trial of 1.05 g ginger against 75 mg pyridoxine in 291 women found the two indistinguishable, and a 2022 pooled analysis found no difference from B6 on any outcome. Subgroups favoured doses below 1,500 mg/day.

    6. Pregnancy exposure at about 1 g/day shows no malformation or perinatal signal in a 68,522-woman cohort, but the trials are too small to exclude an effect on early loss.

    The Norwegian Mother and Child Cohort found no increase in congenital malformations, stillbirth or perinatal death, preterm birth, low birth weight or low Apgar among 1,020 ginger users; the Motherisk comparison of 187 exposed pregnancies found three major malformations, within the 1 to 3 % baseline. Pooled RCT spontaneous abortion was RR 3.14 (95 % CI 0.65 to 15.11), non-significant but imprecise.

    7. A 1.2 g dose accelerates liquid gastric emptying and antral contractions in small crossover studies; 0.5 to 1 g doses measured by other methods show nothing, and the motor effect does not relieve dyspepsia symptoms.

    Antral ultrasound after 1.2 g gave a gastric half-emptying time of 13.1 versus 26.7 min with placebo in 24 healthy volunteers and 12.3 versus 16.1 min in functional dyspepsia, with no change in symptoms, GLP-1, motilin or ghrelin; 1 g by paracetamol absorption and 500 mg by scintigraphy changed nothing. Antiemetic activity in surgery occurred without any emptying change, so emptying is not the mechanism of the nausea benefit.

    8. Gingerols and shogaols inhibit the 5-HT3 receptor non-competitively at low micromolar concentrations; they do not bind the ondansetron site, and human target engagement at oral doses is unmeasured.

    In human recombinant 5-HT3A/AB receptors and native receptors in human submucous plexus, ginger constituents reduce the maximal serotonin response without changing serotonin potency and without displacing the labelled antagonist; rat visceral afferent recordings show the same non-competitive block, shogaol stronger than gingerol. NK1 and muscarinic effects are weak animal-tissue signals only.

    9. At 1 to 4 g/day of dried powder, ginger does not alter platelet aggregation, bleeding time or INR in any controlled human study, including a warfarin pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic trial.

    A single 2 g dose left bleeding time, thromboelastography and whole-blood aggregometry unchanged; 15 g raw or 40 g cooked ginger daily for 2 weeks left thromboxane B2 unchanged; 4 g/day for 3 months had no effect while a single 10 g dose did; in 12 volunteers ginger did not change warfarin clearance, INR or CYP2C9 activity (S-warfarin clearance ratio 1.05, 90 % CI 0.97 to 1.13). A systematic review of eight trials called the platelet evidence equivocal, with effects only at 5 to 10 g, raw material or with a fat load.

    10. No randomized placebo-controlled trial of ginger on testosterone exists in men; the circulating "+17.7 %" comes from one uncontrolled study that PubMed does not index.

    Both systematic reviews of the topic state that the testosterone effect is "not yet confirmed in humans". The only double-blind RCT (n = 100, 500 mg/day for 3 months) measured LH, FSH and testosterone at baseline only. The +17.7 % testosterone, +43.2 % LH figure is an open-label pre/post change in 75 Iraqi men with upper-normal baseline testosterone (about 9 ng/mL), no placebo, and no stated dose.

    11. A single dose of ginger before or after exercise does nothing to pain, effort, oxygen uptake or endurance; four weeks of juiced ginger did not change cycling performance either.

    Acute 2 g before 30 min of cycling left quadriceps pain, RPE, work rate, heart rate and VO2 unchanged; 2 g at 24 or 48 h after eccentric exercise did nothing at 45 min; 1 g before a time-to-exhaustion test did not change endurance. Thirty trained cyclists on 53 g/day of juiced ginger for 4 weeks showed no change in 75 km time-trial time, VO2, power, heart rate or soreness. One 2026 CrossFit trial reporting more repetitions and lower RPE after 9 days at 2 g/day stands alone and needs replication.

    12. Ginger beats placebo for primary dysmenorrhea by about 1.5 cm on a 10 cm scale in small, mostly Iranian trials; equivalence to NSAIDs is asserted, not demonstrated.

    Cochrane pooled three RCTs (266 women): MD −1.55 (95 % CI −2.43 to −0.68), low quality, 22 of 27 supplement trials Iranian; a 2024 pool gave SMD −1.13 with I² 81 %; the only adequately double-blind trial used 1.5 g/day for 3 to 5 days. The NSAID comparisons are two or three small trials with pooled SMD 0.00 to 0.01, one alternate-allocated with no placebo arm and one in which ginger ran numerically 4 to 5 points worse than mefenamic acid.

    13. Gastrointestinal adverse effects are the one consistent harm: common at trial doses, dose-related, and doubling withdrawals in osteoarthritis trials.

    In the dose-escalation study any adverse event rose from 0 of 3 at 100 mg to 6 of 9 at 2 g, all grade 1 (belching, heartburn, indigestion); the largest osteoarthritis trial recorded GI events in 59 versus 21 patients; the osteoarthritis meta-analysis found withdrawals for adverse events RR 2.33 (95 % CI 1.04 to 5.22); an 8-week open-label dyspepsia series at 1,080 mg/day reported bloating in 14.9 %, heartburn in 12.8 % and diarrhoea in 10.6 %.

    Tier 2 — Well-supported (Moderate Confidence) (15 findings)

    14. Across 29 RCTs, ginger lowers CRP by roughly 0.4 to 1.0 mg/L and TNF-α by about 2 pg/mL, almost entirely in metabolically ill cohorts, with high heterogeneity and no trial in healthy non-obese adults.

    The GRADE-assessed dose-response meta-analysis gives CRP −0.86 mg/L (95 % CI −1.10 to −0.62), TNF-α −1.90 pg/mL and IL-6 −1.15 pg/mL; the type 2 diabetes pool gives hs-CRP −0.42 mg/L with all five trials at high risk of bias; the CRP effect is independent of dose; the umbrella review of 36 meta-analyses reports CRP ranges of −0.42 to −1.00 mg/L. No RCT of ginger alone on serum CRP, TNF-α or IL-6 in healthy non-obese adults was found; a 1 g/day dialysis trial was null for hs-CRP and adhesion molecules.

    15. In chemotherapy nausea the reproducible signal is less acute vomiting with 0.5 to 1 g/day taken for more than 3 days; pooled nausea effects are null, and the two best-run modern trials were negative on their primary endpoints.

    The 576-patient multicentre trial starting 0.5 to 1.5 g/day three days before chemotherapy reduced acute nausea severity (p = 0.003), best at 0.5 to 1.0 g; a 162-patient trial starting 1 to 2 g on the chemotherapy day was null and found worse acute nausea with ginger plus aprepitant; a 244-patient cisplatin trial of 160 mg standardized extract was null on delayed nausea. Meta-analyses converge only on acute vomiting, OR 0.3 to 0.4 at ≤1 g/day for more than 3 days; a 103-patient 2024 trial dosing by gingerol content (84 mg/day) improved delayed nausea, vomiting and quality of life over three cycles.

    16. For postoperative nausea, ginger at or above 1 g lowers nausea scores and rescue antiemetic use; the vomiting effect is inconsistent and the largest single trial was null.

    The often-cited 2006 meta-analysis gives PONV RR 0.69 (95 % CI 0.54 to 0.89) on 363 patients; the 180-patient laparoscopy trial found no reduction; a 14-study pool found nausea severity MD −0.71 and rescue antiemetic RR 0.71 but vomiting RR 0.78 (CI 0.42 to 1.44); a second 14-trial pool found lower nausea scores at 2, 6 and 12 h but no effect on vomiting or PONV incidence.

    17. Daily ginger for 5 to 11 days before a damaging exercise bout cuts next-day soreness by about a quarter in one laboratory's trials; heated ginger is no better than raw, and an independent trial at double the dose found no pain effect.

    Two parallel double-blind RCTs of 2 g/day for 11 days reduced 24 h eccentric-exercise pain by 25 % (raw, −0.78 SD, p = 0.041) and 23 % (heated, −0.57 SD, p = 0.049), a series funded by the McCormick Science Institute whose acute-dose experiments were null first. A 32-runner trial at 1.425 g/day for 5 days gave soreness d ≈ −0.7 with 90 % limits crossing zero; 4 g/day for 5 days did not change pain.

    18. Ginger does not lower objective muscle-damage markers; where creatine kinase moved, it kept rising longer with ginger, so the soreness effect reads as analgesia rather than less damage.

    No ginger-only trial reports a lower creatine kinase; in the 4 g/day trial creatine kinase kept rising at 72 and 96 h only in the ginger group; the quasi-experimental extract study found no meaningful change in creatine kinase, strength, circumference or range of motion. Post-exercise cytokine data are split (IL-6 lower in two small trials, unchanged in a third, none reporting concentrations). The only meta-analysis pools ginger with curcumin, ginseng and garlic and reports no ginger-only confidence interval.

    19. In knee osteoarthritis, ginger beats placebo by a small margin (SMD −0.30) with twice the withdrawal rate; ibuprofen ranked above it in the one three-way trial and an independent meta-analysis called the evidence insufficient.

    Five placebo-controlled RCTs (593 patients) give pain SMD −0.30 (95 % CI −0.50 to −0.09) and disability SMD −0.22; the double-dummy crossover ranked ibuprofen above ginger above placebo with ginger versus placebo non-significant in its primary analysis; the largest trial (n = 261) used a ginger plus galangal extract and reached a 13-point responder difference at p = 0.048; the Chilean synthesis of overlapping trials concluded "insufficient evidence".

    20. In the pooled Iranian type 2 diabetes trials, 1.6 to 3 g/day for 8 to 12 weeks lowers fasting glucose by about 16 to 21 mg/dL and HbA1c by 0.4 to 1.0 points, but the one risk-of-bias-filtered pool and the largest single trial found nothing.

    Pools give fasting glucose −21.24 mg/dL (95 % CI −33.21 to −9.26) and HbA1c −1.00 %, or −16.27 mg/dL and −0.41 % with I² above 86 % and an authors' verdict of "limited clinical importance"; the RoB-2 pool of five trials found no significant effect; the 204-patient trial giving 3 g/day in black tea found no glycemic change. The umbrella review grades HbA1c moderate and fasting glucose low certainty; eleven of twelve trials in the best-described pool were Iranian.

    21. Triglycerides fall in nearly every lipid pool (about −9 to −25 mg/dL); total and LDL cholesterol changes are small and inconsistent; certainty is very low and healthy adults show no change.

    Twelve trials give triglycerides −17.59 mg/dL with effects only at ≤2 g/day; a 26-trial multivariate pool gives TG −12.54, TC −6.53, LDL −5.14 and HDL +1.13 mg/dL; a 2023 pool finds TC and TG significant and LDL and HDL not; every lipid outcome is graded very low certainty with I² 94 to 98 %. A 6-week crossover in 35 healthy adults found no lipid, weight or blood-pressure change.

    22. Blood pressure falls by about 5/2 mmHg at 2 g/day or more in people whose pressure is already elevated, and not at all in normotensive adults.

    The 2026 GRADE dose-response meta-analysis of 12 RCTs gives SBP −5.03 mmHg (95 % CI −8.49 to −1.57) and DBP −2.03, larger with elevated baseline pressure, ≥2,000 mg/day and obesity, with low certainty; the earlier 6-trial pool found SBP −6.36 only in subsets aged ≤50, ≤8 weeks and ≥3 g/day; healthy normotensive adults showed no change.

    23. Four weeks of ginger extract raised, rather than lowered, gastric mucosal prostaglandins while diclofenac lowered them and damaged the mucosa; whatever ginger does in people, it does not do it by suppressing constitutive gastric COX-1.

    In 43 osteoarthritis patients, 340 mg/day of a standardized extract and 100 mg/day diclofenac relieved pain equally, but gastric PGE1, PGE2 and PGF2α rose on ginger and fell on diclofenac, with endoscopic mucosal degeneration only on diclofenac. This is the single best human data point against the "ginger is an oral NSAID" model.

    24. One double-blind RCT found lower sperm DNA fragmentation with 500 mg/day for 3 months, no change in sperm count, and motility and morphology trending against ginger.

    TUNEL fragmentation fell from 53.5 % to 17.8 % on ginger versus 56.8 % to 40.5 % on placebo (adjusted MD 3.21, 95 % CI 0.78 to 5.63, p = 0.009) in 100 infertile men; sperm concentration was unchanged (adjusted MD 1.08, CI −9.13 to 11.3) and progressive motility and normal morphology trended worse (both p = 0.06). No pregnancy or live-birth outcome exists, and the result has not been replicated.

    25. Ginger has no action on the vestibular system; for motion sickness the laboratory trials split and the sea trials show less vomiting or parity with standard drugs.

    The 1982 Lancet rotating-chair report in 36 volunteers found ginger better than dimenhydrinate; the next rotating-chair study found no protection from 500 to 1,000 mg powdered or fresh ginger while scopolamine worked; ginger did not alter caloric, rotatory or optokinetic nystagmus. At sea, 1 g reduced vomiting and cold sweating in 80 naval cadets, and a 1,741-tourist seven-agent comparison without placebo could not distinguish ginger from cinnarizine or dimenhydrinate. No meta-analysis exists.

    26. The one human intermediate tied to a nausea endpoint is gastric slow-wave rhythm: ginger blunts vection-induced tachygastria and the vasopressin surge, and blunts hyperglycemia-evoked dysrhythmia.

    In 13 susceptible volunteers, 1 and 2 g reduced vection nausea, tachygastria and plasma vasopressin but did not alter nausea produced by intravenous vasopressin; 1 g blunted the tachygastria produced by a hyperglycemic clamp but not misoprostol-evoked dysrhythmia.

    27. Weight effects are about −1 to −1.5 kg at 2 g/day over more than 8 weeks in one pool and non-significant in the largest; the "thermogenesis" effect is 43 kcal/day in a ten-man meal test.

    Twenty-seven RCTs give weight −1.52 kg, BMI −0.58 and waist −1.04 cm with low to very low certainty; the 36-RCT pool finds waist and body fat but not weight or BMI significant; the thermogenesis trial in 10 overweight men found thermic effect of food +42.7 kcal/day, no change in resting energy expenditure, and no effect on glucose, insulin, lipids or inflammatory markers; the obesity review concludes animal data support weight loss while human studies show "no changes or slight changes".

    28. In acute migraine, ginger beats placebo only as an add-on to intravenous ketoprofen (n = 60) and inside a feverfew-ginger combination; the only prophylaxis trial was negative and the 2026 GRADE synthesis rates everything low or very low.

    Ginger 400 mg added to IV ketoprofen gave better response at 1, 1.5 and 2 h (p = 0.04, 0.01, 0.04); the sublingual feverfew-ginger pilot gave 2 h pain-free 32 % versus 16 % from 15 placebo subjects analysed per attack; 600 mg/day for 3 months did not change responder rate or any secondary outcome in 107 migraineurs; the 8-RCT, 594-patient meta-analysis concludes it is "too uncertain if ginger is effective either as an acute or preventative treatment".

    Tier 3 — Preliminary / Contested (9 findings)

    29. Which molecule acts in vivo is unresolved: tissue deconjugation, gingerdiol metabolites and gut-microbial shogaol products are each proposed and none has human pharmacodynamic confirmation.

    After 56 days of 1 g extract twice daily, ketone reduction to gingerdiols was the main metabolic route and more than 90 % of 6-shogaol became phase I and cysteine-conjugated metabolites; β-glucuronidase treatment of mouse samples raised measured exposure 10- to 700-fold; germ-free mice do not metabolize 6-shogaol, and the microbial metabolite fraction inhibits COX-2 and NF-κB p65 in macrophages.

    30. The two human tissue tests of the COX/LOX hypothesis did not pass cleanly: colon PGE2 fell only under one normalization in normal-risk volunteers, and leukotriene B4 rose in people at increased colorectal risk.

    In 30 volunteers on 2 g/day for 28 days, no colon eicosanoid changed when normalized to protein; PGE2 and 5-HETE fell only when normalized to free arachidonic acid; in 20 increased-risk subjects arachidonic acid fell but LTB4 rose (p = 0.04) and the authors wrote that ginger "lacks the ability to decrease eicosanoid levels" in that group.

    31. Antioxidant biomarkers move in every pool (MDA down, GPx, TAC and SOD up), but heterogeneity is 83 to 98 % and the largest effects come from the smallest, shortest, lowest-dose trials.

    Pools give MDA Hedges' g −1.45 and GPx 1.93 with bigger MDA effects in trials of ≤1 g, under 12 weeks and n ≤ 40; GPx SMD 1.64, TAC 0.40, MDA −0.69; MDA −0.76 µmol/L and SOD +48 U/L in the GRADE pool; the umbrella review grades MDA and TAC very low certainty.

    32. CYP3A4, CYP2C9 and P-glycoprotein inhibition is real in vitro at micromolar concentrations, the only human probe (CYP2C9 with warfarin) was null, and first-pass gut-wall effects on narrow-index drugs remain untested.

    Gingerols inhibit CYP2C9, 2C19 and 3A4 with IC50 values of about 7 to 43 µM; 6-gingerol inhibits P-gp and CYP3A4 at 60 to 500 µM; whole extract is a far weaker inhibitor than single gingerols; a 2023 screen argues intestinal-lumen concentrations could exceed CYP IC50s after gram-scale doses; the warfarin study found an S-7-hydroxywarfarin excretion ratio of 1.00.

    33. The bleeding signal with anticoagulants comes from self-report and case reports, not from controlled data: ginger users on warfarin reported more bleeding (OR 3.20) without higher INR, and about ten case reports lack rechallenge.

    In 171 warfarin patients ginger was associated with self-reported bleeding (OR 3.20, 95 % CI 2.42 to 4.24) but not with supratherapeutic INR; a 314-patient survey found no association between interacting herbs and bleeding or out-of-range INR; case reports include a phenprocoumon INR of 10, a warfarin INR of 8 on a 48 mg chewable supplement, hemoptysis on rivaroxaban with about 3 L/day of ginger tea and a fatal gastrointestinal bleed on dabigatran with a boiled ginger-cinnamon mixture. The EU monograph adopted no bleeding warning; German and Dutch labels keep one.

    34. Two pregnancy cohorts carry unresolved signals: a 159-woman Korean cohort flagged stillbirth (OR 7.8, 95 % CI 0.9 to 70.3) and the Norwegian cohort found more vaginal spotting (adjusted OR 1.4); the EU monograph keeps "preferable to avoid" as a precaution.

    The Korean naturopathic dried-ginger cohort reported major malformation OR 4.9 (0.9 to 25.5) and stillbirth OR 7.8, confounded by dose and co-administered herbs and "requiring confirmation"; the Norwegian cohort reported bleeding after week 17 in 7.8 % versus 5.8 % with no excess beyond spotting. Rodent studies show embryo resorption at doses slightly above human equivalents (EMA monograph section 5.3).

    35. In rodents, ginger restores testosterone after chemical or metabolic testicular insult alongside normalized antioxidant enzymes; in healthy rats the effect is small and inconsistent, and isolated constituents were inert.

    Lead-, cadmium-, fungicide-, cisplatin-, diabetes- and fructose-model rescues are consistent, with LH unchanged in some models and restored in others; healthy-rat testosterone rose at 100 mg/kg but LH and FSH did not in one study and testicular cholesterol rose in an 8-day study; isolated zingerone, geraniol and 6-gingerol did not reverse the diabetic testosterone deficit. Effective rodent doses translate to roughly 0.5 to 11 g/day of extract for a 70 kg adult.

    36. Neither "ginger causes reflux" nor "ginger cures reflux" is supported: resting lower-esophageal sphincter pressure is unchanged in the only manometry study, heartburn occurs in roughly one user in eight in open-label series, and the GERD benefit claim rests on two multi-ingredient trials.

    In 14 healthy men, 1 g left resting LES pressure unchanged while swallow-induced relaxation increased; heartburn was 12.8 % in the 8-week dyspepsia series and not in excess of placebo in pregnancy RCTs; the dietary-GERD review pooled two "ginger-containing supplement" trials to an implausible OR 7.50. No pH-impedance study in GERD patients exists.

    37. The glycemic mechanism in humans is limited to lower HOMA-IR; GLUT4 translocation, AMPK activation, GLP-1 release and carbohydrate-enzyme inhibition are cell and rodent findings, and [8]-gingerol, not [6]-gingerol, was the most potent constituent on GLUT4.

    Gingerol fractions raise cell-surface GLUT4 in L6 myotubes with [8]-gingerol most potent; [6]-gingerol activates AMPK in L6 and C2C12 cells; 200 mg/kg [6]-gingerol raised GLP-1 in db/db mice; α-glucosidase and α-amylase inhibition is a test-tube result. No human study has measured any of these after ginger; the one PCOS RCT found metformin and cinnamon, but not ginger, lowered HOMA-IR.

    Tier 4 — Refuted Claims & Myths (5 findings)

    38. Myth: "Ginger is a natural ibuprofen, a dual COX-1/COX-2 inhibitor working in your blood."

    Free gingerols are undetectable or near 0.03 to 0.05 µM in plasma after 2 g of extract; purified gingerols and shogaols showed no COX-1 inhibition in the ultrafiltration assay, while 8-paradol, a minor constituent, inhibits COX-1 at 4 µM; in patients ginger extract raised gastric prostaglandins where diclofenac lowered them. Compound-dependent COX-2 activity at micromolar concentrations in a dish is the defensible statement.

    39. Myth: "Ginger is as effective as sumatriptan for migraine."

    The 100-patient trial behind the claim has no placebo arm, no non-inferiority margin, no confidence interval on the difference and no effect sizes in its abstract; the 250 mg and 50 mg doses circulate from secondary sources. The 2026 GRADE meta-analysis rates the comparison "very uncertain".

    40. Myth: "Ginger thins the blood like aspirin, so it must be stopped before surgery and never mixed with warfarin."

    Every controlled study at 1 to 4 g/day of dried powder was null for platelets, bleeding time and INR, including the warfarin PK/PD trial; the effects reported come from single 10 g doses, 5 g of raw ginger with a fat load, or litres of tea. Pausing supplements before surgery is a cost-free precaution, not a demonstrated hazard; the one mechanistically plausible unresolved case is dabigatran via P-gp.

    41. Myth: "Fresh ginger is stronger than dried, and heating it makes it more anti-inflammatory."

    Dried powder holds 4.4 to 11.3 mg/g of actives against 1.1 to 1.5 mg/g in fresh rhizome; drying and heating convert gingerols to shogaols rather than destroying them; and in the only human comparison, heat-treated ginger gave 23 % versus 25 % pain reduction with raw, "not enhanced by heat treatment".

    42. Myth: "Ginger ale, candied ginger, lozenges or tea work for nausea, and more is better."

    No randomized trial of ginger ale, candied ginger, lozenges or tea for nausea exists in PubMed; the positive trials used 0.5 to 1.2 g of powdered rhizome in capsules. Higher doses were not better: the 1.5 g chemotherapy arm did less than 0.5 to 1.0 g, the 2 g arm was null and the pregnancy subgroup favoured below 1,500 mg/day.

    Practical — what to actually do (4 findings)

    43. Dose by indication: 0.5 to 1 g/day of dried powder for nausea, 1.5 to 2 g/day in the glycemic, blood-pressure and inflammation trials, 2 g/day for 5 to 11 days for exercise soreness, and no more than 2.5 g/day by the EU monograph.

    Nausea benefits flatten or reverse above 1 g; the cardiometabolic pools used 1.6 to 3 g/day for 8 to 12 weeks; the soreness protocol is 2 g/day for 11 days; GI adverse effects rise with dose from 1 g upward. Capsule powder carried the positive results; ginger stirred into tea did not change glycemia.

    44. Timing matters more than dose: pre-load for nausea (three days before chemotherapy, one hour before travel) and for soreness (5 to 11 days before the session); same-day dosing was null in both settings.

    The positive chemotherapy trial started three days before infusion while the null trials started on or after the chemotherapy day; the EU monograph gives 1 to 2 g one hour before travel; acute single doses before or after exercise did nothing while 11-day loading did.

    45. A morning ginger shot is an unmeasured dose: no peer-reviewed assay of a commercial shot exists, the upper bound from fresh-rhizome content is tens of milligrams of actives, and pasteurization shifts gingerols to shogaols by an unquantified amount.

    Fresh rhizome holds 1.1 to 1.5 mg/g of gingerols plus shogaols, so even full transfer from 30 to 60 g of root gives at most about 35 to 90 mg, comparable to 1 to 2 g of dried powder or one 5 % extract capsule; two volunteers drinking ginger tea reached plasma 6-gingerol of 42 nmol/L; the gingerol-to-shogaol conversion is pH- and heat-dependent. Treat a shot as a food dose in the nausea and gastric-emptying range, not as the 1.5 to 2 g/day used in the cardiometabolic trials, and expect its content to vary with the product.

    46. Interaction rules that survive the evidence: additive effects with antidiabetic and antihypertensive drugs are plausible at 2 g/day, ginger plus aprepitant worsened acute chemotherapy nausea in one trial, label-dose anticoagulant interaction is unproven but heavy intake on a DOAC is not, and the gallstone caution rests on rat bile data.

    HbA1c fell at 2 g/day in diabetics on oral agents and systolic pressure fell 5 mmHg at ≥2 g/day; ginger plus aprepitant gave more severe acute nausea than aprepitant alone; 1 g/day potentiated nifedipine's antiplatelet effect in one small study; the cholagogue effect is a 1985 rat observation with no human gallstone report since. Separate ginger from narrow-index drugs by timing if in doubt, because gut-wall CYP3A4/P-gp effects are untested.
